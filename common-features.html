Public-knowledge write-ups of features shared across LTE and NR — RU/OAM, O-RAN fronthaul planes, transport and timing. Concepts only, no internal specifics. Each card feeds the Interview simulator.
CM01 RU Transmit-Power Validation Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Confirm each radio actually transmits the power it's told to, across bands and antennas — a core RU acceptance check.
How it works
A spectrum analyser measures the RU output per carrier/antenna; the framework compares measured power to the configured target within tolerance, accounting for cable/setup loss and any splitter correction, and flags branches that are low/high.
Interview Q&A
Q: How do you validate an RU's transmit power?
A: Drive the RU to a known configured power, measure the actual output on a calibrated analyser per antenna/carrier, subtract the measured cable/setup loss, and check it lands within tolerance of target. Deviations point to PA gain, calibration, or a bad TX branch.
CM02 RU Recovery & Fault-ID Procedures Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
When a radio hits a fault, recover it automatically and record why — instead of a manual truck roll.
How it works
The RU/OAM detects a fault ID (e.g. PLL unlock, thermal, fronthaul loss), runs the matching recovery (reset the affected block, re-init, or controlled reboot), and logs the cause for RCA. Escalation reboots the whole unit only if targeted recovery fails.
Interview Q&A
Q: How should a radio handle a recoverable fault?
A: Detect and classify it by fault ID, attempt the least-disruptive recovery first (re-init the affected function), and record the cause and action for root-cause analysis. Only escalate to a full reboot if targeted recovery fails — minimizing outage and giving traceability.
CM03 M-Plane (Management Plane) Aggregation Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
The management channel to the open radio — and a way to funnel many radios' management through one point.
How it works
O-RAN M-plane (NETCONF/YANG) configures and monitors the O-RU. Aggregation proxies multiple O-RUs' M-plane sessions through a manager, simplifying scale and the SMO's connection count. (M-plane is one of the fronthaul planes alongside C/U/S.)
Interview Q&A
Q: What are the O-RAN fronthaul planes and what does M-plane do?
A: C-plane (scheduling/beamforming commands), U-plane (IQ user data), S-plane (timing/sync), and M-plane (management). M-plane uses NETCONF/YANG to configure, software-manage and monitor the O-RU; aggregation lets a manager front many O-RUs' M-plane to the SMO.
CM04 RRH Firmware Upgrade Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Update the radio's firmware safely in the field without bricking it or dropping the site unexpectedly.
How it works
The new image is transferred, validated (checksum/signature), staged to a backup bank, then activated with a controlled restart; a fallback to the previous bank triggers if the new image fails to come up — a dual-bank A/B scheme.
Interview Q&A
Q: How do you make a field firmware upgrade safe?
A: Verify the image (signature/checksum), write to an inactive bank so the running one is untouched, activate with a controlled reboot, and auto-roll-back to the previous bank if the new image fails health checks. A/B banking means a bad upgrade never bricks the radio.
CM05 OAM–RRH Heartbeat Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
A periodic "are you alive" between the management layer and the radio so failures are caught fast.
How it works
The OAM/RRH manager and the radio exchange periodic heartbeats; missing several in a row marks the radio unreachable and triggers alarms/recovery. It also detects silent fronthaul/link failures the data plane might mask.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why have a heartbeat between OAM and the radio?
A: To detect loss of management connectivity quickly and deterministically — a few missed heartbeats flag the node down and kick off alarms/recovery, catching silent link or process failures that wouldn't otherwise surface until a user-impacting fault.
CM06 GTP-U Counters Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Measure the user-plane tunnel traffic between RAN and core to spot loss, drops and throughput issues.
How it works
GTP-U carries user data over S1-U/F1-U/N3 (per-bearer TEIDs). Counting packets/bytes, sequence gaps and error indications per tunnel exposes transport loss, path MTU problems and where throughput is bottlenecked.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is GTP-U and why count it?
A: GTP-U is the user-plane tunnelling protocol carrying subscriber data across S1-U/F1-U/N3, keyed by TEIDs per bearer/flow. Per-tunnel counters (packets, bytes, dropped, error indications) let you localise packet loss and throughput bottlenecks to the transport rather than the air interface.
CM07 Unified E2-Plane Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
A common approach to the control interface toward the near-real-time RIC across technologies.
How it works
O-RAN E2 connects the RAN (E2 nodes) to the near-RT RIC so xApps can monitor and control RAN functions via E2 service models (KPM for metrics, RC for control). A unified E2-plane presents 4G/5G nodes consistently to the RIC.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is the E2 interface and the near-RT RIC for?
A: E2 links RAN nodes to the near-real-time RIC, letting xApps subscribe to metrics (KPM service model) and issue control (RC service model) on a ~10 ms–1 s loop — e.g. traffic steering or interference control. A unified E2-plane exposes different RAT nodes to the RIC the same way.
CM08 AISG v2 & Fronthaul Split Option 7.2 Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
AISG controls antenna-line devices (like remote electrical tilt); Split 7.2x defines where the PHY is divided between DU and radio in O-RAN.
How it works
AISG is the standard control bus to RET/TMA antenna-line devices. Split 7.2x is the O-RAN low-PHY split: the O-DU does high-PHY, the O-RU does low-PHY (iFFT/CP, beamforming), connected by eCPRI carrying frequency-domain IQ — balancing fronthaul rate vs radio complexity.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is fronthaul split 7.2x and why was it chosen?
A: It splits the PHY so the O-RU handles low-PHY (iFFT/CP add, digital beamforming) and the O-DU handles high-PHY and above, exchanging frequency-domain IQ over eCPRI. Compared with a full-PHY-in-radio (option 8/CPRI) it cuts fronthaul bandwidth while keeping the radio relatively simple — the O-RAN mainstream choice.
CM09 4G + 5G on the Same BBU Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Host both LTE and NR on one baseband platform to save hardware, power and site space.
How it works
The shared BBU partitions compute, fronthaul and timing between the LTE and NR stacks; they share transport and sync while running independent cells, which also simplifies EN-DC anchoring on the same box.
Interview Q&A
Q: What are the benefits and challenges of 4G+5G on one BBU?
A: Benefits: less hardware/power/space, shared transport and timing, and easy EN-DC anchoring. Challenges: partitioning CPU/accelerator and fronthaul capacity so neither RAT starves the other, and keeping timing/sync and software lifecycles for both stacks isolated yet coordinated.

CM11 Timing & SFN Synchronization Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Keep every cell aligned in time and frame number — essential for TDD, handovers and features like CA/MIMO.
How it works
Sites lock to a common reference via GNSS and/or PTP (IEEE 1588) + SyncE over the transport; the S-plane delivers timing to the O-RU. TDD needs tight phase alignment so neighbouring cells don't transmit into each other's receive windows.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why is timing/phase sync so critical in TDD, and how is it delivered?
A: In TDD all cells must switch between UL and DL at the same instant; a phase error means one cell transmits while a neighbour is receiving, causing cross-link interference. Sync is delivered by GNSS and/or PTP (1588) with SyncE over the fronthaul (the O-RAN S-plane), holding phase within tens of nanoseconds.
CM12 Band Plans, Sub-bands & RF Front-End Filter Passband (why a "same-band" carrier may not be supported) Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
A 3GPP band is a wide range of frequencies — far wider than any single operator's licensed block. The AAU/RU's RF front-end (duplexers, cavity/SAW filters, combiners, PA/LNA matching) is built in hardware for a specific passband, chosen at design time. You cannot software-retune a physical filter. So a unit can be "band X capable" yet still not support a particular carrier in band X if that carrier falls outside the passband the filters were cut for — you need a hardware variant with a different filter, not a config change.
How it works
Each band has full DL/UL ranges — e.g. Band 28 (APT700 FDD): UL 703–748 MHz, DL 758–803 MHz (45 MHz duplex). An operator is licensed a sub-block within that (10–15 MHz somewhere in the range). A multi-band AAU that shares a low-band front end (e.g. B8/B20/B28 together) has its filter passbands + combiner + PA/LNA designed for chosen sub-ranges, captured in the block diagram / gain budget at design time. Near a passband edge, insertion loss rises (filter roll-off) → less TX power and worse RX sensitivity; outside the passband the signal is rejected. 3GPP also sometimes splits a wide band into variants (a lower vs upper sub-range) so a narrower-passband radio can target one half — a radio cut for one variant won't cover the other. Bottom line: center frequency ≠ band; map the deployment's exact DL & UL centers onto the AAU's designed passband before committing.

The edge can be asymmetric — TX vs RX
A duplexer has separate TX and RX passbands (independent filter paths), so a unit can reach a sub-block on the downlink but not the uplink (or vice-versa). In practice the receive filter is usually the tighter constraint: the transmitter can still deliver near-full EIRP across the band edge while Rx sensitivity collapses just outside its passband. So "the band isn't supported" often really means "the Rx filter doesn't cover it" — always characterise TX and RX separately. If a variant is needed, the change may be confined to the receive filter.
How to diagnose a passband-edge limitation
1. Sweep across the edge: a gentle few-dB roll-off just inside turning into a sharp collapse (tens of dB) just outside = a filter skirt, not a flat/uniform loss.
2. Look within one channel: if attenuation increases across the channel (lower RBs read much stronger than upper RBs), that frequency-selective tilt is the signature of a passband edge — a flat loss would hit all RBs equally.
3. Rule out firmware: reproduce on a different SW build — if the roll-off is identical, it's the hardware filter, not calibration/FW.
4. Don't trust the nominal edge: a carrier inside the supposed passband can still fail (roll-off starting earlier, or a bandwidth-specific issue) — treat the edge as characterised only after you've actually swept it, not from the datasheet number.
Interview Q&A
Q: A unit transmits fine into the upper part of a band but can't receive there — what's going on?
A: TX and RX use separate filter paths in the duplexer, so the DL passband can cover a sub-block the UL (Rx) filter doesn't. The receive filter is usually the tighter limit — you get near-full EIRP on the downlink but sensitivity collapses just outside the Rx passband. It's an Rx-filter limitation, not a whole-band one, so a fix may only need a new receive filter.
Q: How do you prove a band-edge sensitivity failure is the Rx filter, not firmware or a flat loss?
A: Three checks: (1) reproduce on a second SW build — identical roll-off ⟹ hardware, not FW/calibration; (2) look within the failing channel — if lower RBs are much stronger than upper RBs, that frequency-selective tilt is a passband skirt, not a uniform loss; (3) sweep across the edge — a sharp collapse just outside vs gentle roll-off just inside confirms the filter skirt.
Q: An operator is licensed in band X and your radio is "band-X capable", but it doesn't support their carrier. How?
A: The band is a wide range; the radio's RF front-end filters/duplexer are cut in hardware for a specific passband — usually one sub-block or band variant. If the operator's block falls outside that passband, the filter rejects it (or heavy roll-off kills performance). You can't retune a physical filter in software, so it needs a hardware variant with the right filter. Center frequency, not just band number, decides support.
Q: Why do you validate low / mid / high (band-edge) channels in TX and RX tests?
A: Filter insertion loss and roll-off are worst at the passband edges, so TX output power and RX sensitivity degrade there. Testing low/mid/high (and the exact deployment center frequency) proves the unit meets ACLR/EVM/sensitivity across the whole used block, not just the easy mid-band point.
Q: What's the difference between a band and a band variant (e.g. A vs B)?
A: 3GPP partitions some wide bands into variants covering different sub-ranges, so a radio with a narrower filter passband can target one part. A unit built for one variant's passband won't cover the other variant — same band number, different hardware filter.
CM13 Spectrum Sensing & Spectrograms — the RU as a sensor, visualised at the SMO Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Beyond serving traffic, a modern O-RAN radio (AAU/RU) can double as a wideband spectrum sensor: it captures the raw RF, computes a power-vs-frequency picture (a PSD / spectrogram), and streams it up to the management system (SMO/EMS) so operators can see the spectrum remotely — interference, rogue emitters, incumbents — in real deployments, not just on a lab debug port.
How it works
The radio captures time-domain IQ samples (wide bandwidth, high sample rate) in the FPGA, and the on-board CPU runs repeated FFTs, averaging over ~1 s to produce a Power Spectral Density (PSD) vector — power in each frequency bin. Resolution bandwidth ≈ sample_rate / FFT_size; a finer RBW (e.g. 15 kHz vs 30 kHz) = more bins, sharper detail, more data. A spectrogram / waterfall stacks PSD traces over time (X = frequency, Y = time, colour = power); the top trace is the latest PSD.
Transport: early/lab designs dump the PSD over a dedicated debug Ethernet port to a PC; the productised path carries it up the O-RAN fronthaul + M-plane (NETCONF/YANG) to the SMO/EMS as a periodic notification, so it works in the field. Because the payload is periodic (~1/s) and sizeable, encoding matters — sending 16-bit integer (deci-dBm) instead of 32-bit float roughly halves the on-wire size to fit the M-plane budget (plain file-upload used for PM counters is too slow for 1-second cadence).
Uses: interference hunting, PIM / rogue-emitter detection, incumbent sensing (DFS / CBRS-style), spectrum-occupancy monitoring, and feeding dynamic spectrum sharing / SON.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is a PSD / spectrogram and how does a radio generate one?
A: PSD = power per frequency bin, from the FFT of captured IQ (usually averaged over time to reduce noise). A spectrogram (waterfall) plots PSD over time — frequency × time × power. The radio captures wideband IQ in the FPGA, the CPU FFTs and averages it, and outputs the bin vector (e.g. thousands of bins per second).
Q: What sets the frequency resolution (RBW) of a spectrogram?
A: RBW ≈ sample_rate / FFT_size. A larger FFT gives finer resolution but more data and more compute; you trade spectral detail against data rate and processing. Sensing config picks RBW (e.g. 15 kHz for LTE/NR µ0, 30 kHz for µ1).
Q: Why carry sensing data over the O-RAN M-plane instead of a debug port, and what's the challenge?
A: A debug port is lab-only; real deployments need the data at the SMO/EMS via standard interfaces — the M-plane (NETCONF/YANG) over the fronthaul. The challenge is that the payload is periodic (~1/s) and large, versus the M-plane budget, so you compress/encode it (e.g. int16 deci-dBm instead of float32) and use NETCONF notifications rather than slow periodic file upload.
CM14 Power Monitoring & PSU Efficiency — self-reported RU power vs an independent reference Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
A modern RU/AAU reports its own power consumption (power-monitor / energy telemetry) — used for energy-efficiency KPIs, GreenRAN reporting, and power/thermal budgeting. But the radio only directly senses its DC input; to state "power consumed" it must correct for PSU (power-supply) conversion efficiency. If that efficiency model is wrong, the reported number drifts from reality — and the error tends to grow with load.
How it works
The RU measures its DC input (e.g. the −48 V feed: voltage × current) and applies a PSU-efficiency correction to estimate delivered power. The catch: PSU efficiency is load-dependent — a curve, not a constant (usually lower at very light and very heavy load, best mid-range). Using a datasheet / nominal efficiency instead of the unit's actual factory-measured efficiency injects a systematic, load-dependent error.
Validate against an independent reference: put a lab power analyzer on the common DC input and sweep many load scenarios (idle → max), comparing the RU's self-reported power against the analyzer. Calibrate by replacing the datasheet model with a factory-measured efficiency LUT (lookup table) → software update → re-validate end-to-end.
The residual gap has several contributors — don't blame one: PSU efficiency model, PSU telemetry accuracy (the sensor reading itself), and DC cable loss (−48 V feed resistance). After calibration, low/mid-load accuracy improves sharply; a small few-percent, load-dependent under-read can remain at high load — now quantified and treated as the next calibration step rather than an unknown.
Whole-AAU power = sum of per-PSU rails, with a built-in cross-check
A whole-AAU power reading is built up: a multi-PSU AAU (e.g. 3 PSUs) each converts the DC feed on several rails (e.g. a 50 V and a 12 V rail). Total estimated input = Σ over PSUs & rails of (rail output ÷ rail efficiency) = output power + modelled conversion loss. Below the characterised efficiency range the curve is extrapolated (flagged) and least trustworthy at light load. Crucially the AAU has two independent internal sensors: (a) this per-rail efficiency-model estimate, and (b) an independent on-board direct input measurement (a common-board V×I sense). Comparing them is a built-in sanity check — and they differ, with the model estimate reading lower.
Why the model estimate is a lower bound
• The estimate models only the converters + loss curve — it does not include unmodelled internal cable losses or the common board's own draw, which sit outside the model boundary → so the estimate is a lower bound; the independent direct measurement reads higher.
• Telemetry resolution limits it (e.g. 0.1 V / 0.1 A steps ≈ several watts per current step on a high-voltage rail).
• TDD bands (PA switching on/off continuously) make it harder for current sensors to capture true consumption → extra error on TDD vs an FDD/CW load.
• Different power-reporting paths use different sensors, so their numbers won't match — always know which sensor/model a figure came from.
Interview Q&A
Q: An AAU reports total power two ways and they differ by tens of percent — why, and which is more trustworthy?
A: One is an efficiency-model estimate (Σ per-PSU-rail output ÷ efficiency + datasheet loss) — it excludes unmodelled cable and common-board losses, so it's a lower bound. The other is an independent on-board direct input measurement (V×I) that captures everything downstream and reads higher. Neither is "wrong" — different sensors/models: the direct measurement is closer to true input, the model gives the per-rail breakdown. Reconcile with cable loss + telemetry resolution + (on TDD) PA-switching sensor error.
Q: Why is per-unit power telemetry harder on a TDD band?
A: TDD PAs switch on/off continuously, so instantaneous current swings hard; sensors with limited sample rate/resolution can't capture the true average, adding error versus a steady FDD/CW load. It's a measurement-accuracy effect, not a real power difference.
Q: An RU reports its own power draw but reads low — and worse at high load. Why, and how do you fix it?
A: It senses DC input and applies a PSU-efficiency correction. PSU efficiency is load-dependent, so if the correction uses a datasheet/nominal figure instead of the unit's real (factory-measured) curve, the estimate is systematically off — and because efficiency drops toward high load, the under-read grows with load. Fix: calibrate with a factory-measured efficiency LUT and re-validate against an independent power analyzer across the load range.
Q: How do you validate an RU's self-reported power, and what besides PSU efficiency affects the gap?
A: Compare the RU's reported power against an independent lab power analyzer on the common DC input, across scenarios from idle to max load, and quantify the mean and worst-case error. The residual gap also includes PSU telemetry (sensor) accuracy and DC cable loss on the −48 V feed — so characterise all three rather than attributing everything to the efficiency model.
Case study added — GRDEV-9526 PSU-efficiency calibration: the numbers and the power-measurement architecture
CM14 explains the concept. This block adds the real validation campaign behind it: GRDEV-9526, a PSU-efficiency calibration of chassis-level EPE POWER on the 3-flavour AAU. It covers the architecture that produces the number, the method, the results and the lessons.

1. Where the power number comes from (PCM architecture)
AAU power path and the measurement chain
PCM = Power Consumption Measurement (common-board MCU). PCMG = PCM Gathering (runs on each FHGW RFSoC, reads over I2C / FPCB). The reference supply sees everything to the left of the PSUs; EPE POWER is built from what the PSUs report.
Implication: the RU measures what leaves the PSUs, then has to model the PSU losses to estimate the −48 V input. A wrong loss model creates an error that grows with load. That was the problem statement: EPE/PM read low versus the independent supply, and the gap widened as load increased.

2. What was fixed before the model was touched. Four RU-side items: (1) PSU command framing and length handling, where a vendor command change plus stricter mid-board size checks exposed read failures; (2) a PSU temperature-sensor read defect; (3) the PSU ACK/NACK status register being misread, another source of read failures; (4) the datasheet-nominal efficiency model, replaced by a factory-measured efficiency LUT. Lesson: fix the read path first (compare the sentinel values in CM21). Tuning a model on top of dropped reads just hides them.

5. Reading the numbers like an expert
• The slope is the story. At high load only ≈91 % of each extra watt shows up in PM (slope 0.911), compared with ≈96 % at low load. The PSUs lose more at high load than the LUT predicts. • The spread between windows comes from TDD. In S3 (B41 full) the reference sat flat at ≈1005–1013 W, but per-window error ranged from −0.1 % to −7.4 %. • Idle jitter is quantisation. In S0 the per-window error moved between −2.8 % and −6.4 % because the low-band PM term stepped between ≈71 and 81 W on a steady 177 W reference. That is sensor resolution, not drift.
• The earlier archive is a different boundary. Before this calibration, the mid-band O-RAN-B1+B2 CSV and the whole-AAU DC supply differed by a constant ≈145 W (≈60 vs 205 W off, ≈355 vs 505 W at 0 %, ≈715 vs 850 W at 100 %). Stated limits (put them in any report that reuses these figures): aggregate chassis power only, so no per-PSU efficiency proof, and isolated-flavour deltas are confounded by different carrier counts; the low-band points exercise B8 only (B28 not driven); one chassis, one build, one session, with no thermal or unit repeat.

Quick check questions. Why is a constant offset less worrying than a slope below 1? A constant offset points to a boundary (something outside the model is always on). A slope below 1 means the model loses a fixed fraction of every extra watt, so the error grows with load. Why accept −5.3 % ± 1.2 % instead of fixing it? It is now characterised and reproducible, so reports can correct for it or quote it. The next step is a second LUT iteration at high load and a per-PSU validation.
CM15 BBU ↔ RU Communication — the four O-RAN planes and the full RU lifecycle: boot, call-home, sync, configure, activate, carry IQ, report Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
In an O-RAN 7.2x split the RU is deliberately simple. It does not schedule, decide or attach anyone. It does exactly what the DU (BBU) tells it, symbol by symbol, and reports back. The conversation runs over four planes on the same fibre. Understanding who says what, in which order, is the base for debugging every "carrier not coming up" or "no traffic" problem.

Level 2 — what the RU actually does at each step
Boot & POST: the RU checks its own hardware (HW monitor, I2C devices, eMMC, Ethernet, the clock chip, driver node) and loads its identity (model name, serial, calibration) from EEPROM-derived files. A unit that cannot identify itself cannot be configured safely.
Transport: DHCP on the M-plane VLAN gives an IP and tells the RU where the NETCONF client (the DU/SMO) is.
Call-home: the RU initiates the SSH/TLS session to the NETCONF client, which then acts as the manager. In the <hello> the RU lists the YANG modules it supports.
Capability read: the DU reads what the RU can do: bands, max carriers per band, min/max carrier bandwidth (o-ran-module-cap), array/endpoint layout (o-ran-uplane-conf) and hardware inventory. The DU must not ask for more than this.
Supervision: a watchdog. The DU must keep refreshing it, and if it stops, the RU assumes the manager is gone and takes its carriers down safely (no uncontrolled transmission).
Sync: the RU's sync-manager locks PTP/SyncE and reports LOCKED. Many later steps are sync-gated: no transmission until timing is right (TDD especially).
Configure: the DU writes processing elements (MAC/VLAN ↔ endpoint mapping), low-level endpoints (eAxC IDs) and array-carriers (centre frequency, bandwidth, gain) into the RU datastore (sysrepo). The RU's operations-manager validates them and programs the FPGA/DFE.
Activate: setting the carriers active makes the RU enable DAC and PA for them. The carrier state goes from DISABLED to READY/ACTIVE and a notification goes back.
Run: from now on C-plane messages arrive ahead of each symbol and the U-plane carries IQ. The RU must receive each packet inside its timing window, otherwise it drops it and counts it.
Report: alarms (FM), PM counters and files (RSSI, TX/RX statistics, energy/EPE), software management (download, install, activate) and troubleshooting logs, all over the M-plane.

Interview Q&A
Q: Name the four O-RAN fronthaul planes and what each carries.
A: M-plane: NETCONF/YANG management (config, alarms, PM, software). S-plane: PTP + SyncE timing. C-plane: per-symbol control telling the RU which PRBs/beams/compression to use. U-plane: the frequency-domain IQ samples, DL and UL, including PRACH. C and U run over eCPRI on Ethernet.
Q: Who initiates the M-plane connection, and why?
A: The RU, with NETCONF call-home: after DHCP it opens the SSH/TLS session to the DU/SMO NETCONF client, which then manages it. It solves the discovery problem, since the RU may get a dynamic address and the manager does not know it yet.
Q: What is M-plane supervision for?
A: It is a watchdog. The manager must refresh it periodically. If refreshes stop, the RU assumes it has lost its manager and takes its carriers down in a controlled way, so a radio is never left transmitting with nobody in control.
Q: Why is carrier activation "sync-gated"?
A: The RU must not transmit until its PTP/SyncE timing is locked. Otherwise its symbols would be misaligned with the DU and neighbours (fatal for TDD, and harmful interference in general). Steps like L1-Done/RRH-Ready and PA enable wait for a LOCKED state.
Q: The OAM shows the cell in service but no UE can attach. How do you find the truth?
A: Check bottom-up: RU carrier state in its datastore (ACTIVE?), RU alarms, DU fronthaul counters (U-plane RX from the RU non-zero?) and finally KPI availability counters. A cell state derived from the stack's intent can be green while the RU carriers are disabled.

Level 2 — the five hand-offs where it breaks
1. PTP / S-plane: the RU must be LOCKED before sync-gated steps. Holdover ("no external sync source"), slow re-acquisition or non-recovery after reboot stalls everything downstream.
2. Per-band frequency config: in one build the controller keyed processing elements on (vNode, carrier) without band, so two bands collided and one was silently skipped ("processing element already exists").
3. L1-Done / RRH-Ready: this is sync-gated. If emitted before In-Sync it is dropped as "not_synced", and in that build it was not re-driven on In-Sync, which deadlocks the activation.
4. TX power: energy-saving logic can disable TX power, and a co-located RU's energy-saving storm can saturate the controller/IPC and starve the target RU. An "Invalid gain configuration" alarm flags the gain churn (carriers flapping READY↔DISABLED).
5. ModifyPA / activation (RU): only after a valid TX-power message does the RU apply the PA and flip the carrier to ACTIVE.
Level 3 — case study: the outage after an RU firmware upgrade (three defects at once)
What was seen: after the RU firmware upgrade, both RUs rebooted in a loop, then came up clean (PTP LOCKED, no alarms), but all carriers stayed DISABLED forever. A full stack restart fixed it for about 6 minutes, until the next RU crash.

Defect 1 (BBU): configuration never re-driven after an RU reconnect. The per-cell frequency table lived only in the per-connection controller process, so it died with the session. The RRH manager did detect the disconnect/reconnect but only raised/cleared an alarm. OAM had sent SET_FREQ once at cell bring-up and considered provisioning done, so nothing re-sent it. Every later TX-power attempt aborted with "Frequency not yet configured for cell". Fix directions: replay the stored carrier map to the new session on RU connect, or propagate the disconnect so OAM re-drives, or have the controller pull the frequency when it is missing. Regression test: kill the RU M-plane mid-service and expect reconnect → frequency config → carriers READY without any pod restart.

Defect 2 (RU firmware): identity and calibration lost, FPGA crash at activation. After the upgrade the identity file derived from EEPROM was written as 0 bytes on every boot and the TX calibration file disappeared. The physical EEPROM read fine directly over I2C, so it was the firmware's dump step, not hardware. Faults followed: file not found, unit unidentified, FPGA out of order, self-reset. The FPGA fault fired seconds into applying the carrier/TX-power config: activating an unidentified front end crashed it, the NETCONF agent died mid-RPC, and the BBU logged "failed to receive reply". That BBU error was a symptom, not the cause.

Defect 3 (stack reporting): false cell-up. The eNB OAM reported RF TX status true from the cell state machine's intent, while the same report showed the PA state invalid. In 5G the DU declared the cell in service a minute before the carrier activation ran and failed. PHY fronthaul TX counters were flowing but RX = 0. Fire-and-forget eCPRI "transmitting" is not proof of RF on air. The KPI/PM availability (≈0) was the truthful witness.
Level 4 — the debugging method that worked (reuse it)
Kill false leads with a frequency histogram. Several scary ERROR lines appeared in the failure window, but counting them per hour across the whole day showed they also occurred during perfectly healthy hours, so they were background noise and were discarded. The rule: if a signature occurs when everything works, it is not your cause.

Interview Q&A
Q: A carrier stays DISABLED although the RU is up, PTP LOCKED and alarm-free. What do you check?
A: Walk the activation chain for the latest RU session: was the NETCONF client connected, was In-Sync received, and was the per-band frequency config (SET_FREQ → freqConfigd) sent after this connection? Then L1-Done/RRH-Ready, SET_TX_POWER and the UserPlaneConfiguration write, and finally the carrier state callbacks. The first missing step is the break. A classic case is configuration that is not re-sent after an RU reconnect.
Q: The BBU logs "failed to receive reply" on the TX-power NETCONF write. Is the BBU at fault?
A: Not necessarily. In the case study the RU's FPGA crashed seconds into applying the config (the unit had lost its identity/calibration after a firmware upgrade), killing the NETCONF agent mid-RPC. The BBU error was the symptom. Check RU alarms and reboot cause at the same timestamp.
Q: How do you decide whether an ERROR line in the log is your root cause?
A: Check its frequency over time. Count occurrences per hour across the whole day. If it also appears during known-healthy periods, it is background noise. Only signatures that appear or disappear with the failure are candidates.
Q: Why can a cell show "in service" while the RU transmits nothing?
A: The state may be derived from the stack's intent (the cell FSM or MAC cell-start toward its own PHY) rather than the RU's actual carrier state. PHY TX counters on eCPRI are fire-and-forget, so they flow even if the RU is not transmitting. Cross-check RU carrier state, fronthaul RX counters and KPI availability.
Q: What regression test proves a "reconfigure on reconnect" fix?
A: Kill or restart the RU M-plane session mid-service (or reboot the RU) and expect, without restarting any BBU pods: reconnect → frequency config re-sent → TX power → carriers READY/ACTIVE → traffic resumes.

Q: You edited startup.xml on the RU to change the bandwidth, but after reboot it is back. Why?

CM18 MCU Firmware Flashing with J-Link (SWD) — mid-board debug connector, pin mapping, JFlashLite procedure and what can go wrong Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Besides the big FPGA/CPU software (upgraded over the M-plane, CM04), the AAU boards carry small microcontrollers (housekeeping, sequencing, sensors). When one of them needs new firmware and cannot be updated in-system, you program it directly through its debug port with a SEGGER J-Link probe over SWD (Serial Wire Debug). It is a hands-on bench job, and wiring is 90% of it.
Level 1 — SWD basics
SWD is ARM's 2-wire debug interface: SWDIO (bidirectional data) and SWCLK (clock), plus GND, optionally RESET, and VTref. VTref is not a power supply. It lets the probe sense the target's I/O voltage so its drivers match it. Without VTref the J-Link reports no target voltage and will not connect.
That is why the procedure says the unit must be powered on and fully booted: the MCU and its VTref come from the board, not from the probe.
Level 2 — where to connect and the pin mapping
The AAU has a 5-pin debug connector on each board: (1) mid-band (B1/B3), lower layer; (2) B41, upper layer; (3) low-band. Pick the connector of the board you flash.

CM19 O-RAN Fronthaul Timing Windows — when the DU must send and the RU must receive (T1a/T2a/T12, Ta3/Ta4/T34), with the corrected relations Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
The RU cannot put a symbol on the air until its IQ samples have arrived, and it has only a small buffer to hold them. Think of a bakery oven that must be loaded at exactly 06:00. Flour deliveries must arrive between 05:30 and 05:50: too early and there is no shelf space, too late and the slot is missed. The DU is the supplier. It knows the road takes somewhere between 5 and 15 minutes, so it must dispatch inside a narrower window so that every delivery lands inside the bakery window whatever the traffic. In O-RAN these windows have names: T1a (DU send, downlink), T2a (RU receive, downlink), Ta3 (RU send, uplink), Ta4 (DU receive, uplink), and T12 / T34 for the fronthaul delay. Packets outside the window are dropped, and on an RF bench that looks like bad EVM or lost throughput, not like a timing fault.

Level 2 — where the values live, how to read them, and how a timing fault looks on an RF bench
Where the numbers live. In the O-RAN M-plane the RU publishes its receive/transmit windows as a read-only RU delay profile in the o-ran-delay-management YANG module (t2a-min-up, t2a-max-up, t2a-min-cp-dl, t2a-max-cp-dl, tcp-adv-dl, ta3-min, ta3-max, t2a-min-cp-ul, t2a-max-cp-ul). The DU side has its own values (t1a-max-up, ta4-max, and so on), and the controller may write the transport-delay container (t12-min/max, t34-min/max). The spec asks the controller to fill T12/T34 as if there were no external delay; an RU that adapts its profile may only do so while all carriers using those buffers are disabled.

Level 3 — expert detail: intersection vs union, C-plane lead, and look-alike delays
Why DL is an intersection and UL is a union. In the downlink the RU window is fixed by hardware (buffer size and processing latency). The DU must guarantee arrival inside it for the worst-case fast and the worst-case slow path, so the usable send window shrinks by the delay spread (T12_max − T12_min). In the uplink the RU sends inside its window, and the DU must accept every packet that could arrive, so the DU window grows by the delay spread (T34_max − T34_min). The design consequence is that fronthaul jitter, not just average delay, eats the DL budget. A switch that adds constant latency can be absorbed by shifting windows; one that adds variable queuing delay narrows the DU send window and can close it completely.

C-plane leads U-plane. The RU must decode the C-plane section (which PRBs, which beam, which compression) before the matching U-plane arrives, so the C-plane window sits earlier by at least Tcp_adv_dl. A DU that sends C- and U-plane in the same burst can pass in the lab and fail once a switch reorders traffic.

Do not confuse these with other "delays" on the site. The instrument external-trigger delay (the 9.9765 ms B41 trigger in B41T, the ext-delay entry in FWK3) aligns the analyser to the frame; it is not a fronthaul window. Air-interface timing offsets are also separate: on the B41 TDD FHGW the guide lists GRDEV-5757, where setting ul_delay (the NTA offset) to 20 µs made the PRACH-measured timing advance read 4 (about 2.1 µs, since one TA step is 16 Ts ≈ 0.52 µs) on a bench where it should read about 0. That is a UL timing-offset alignment issue between the RU and PHY, which you find in the PRACH TA statistics, not in rx-window counters.

DU CPU is part of the timing budget. A software DU sends DL U-plane from real-time L1 threads. If those cores are shared with other work (see the core-allocation step in OPS3 and /opt/cpualloc.json), sends slip past T1a_min_up and the RU counts them late. That is why a "radio" problem sometimes goes away after only the DU core map is changed.
Interview Q&A
Q: Write the relation between the DU send window and the RU receive window in the downlink.
A: T1a_max_up = T2a_max_up + T12_min and T1a_min_up = T2a_min_up + T12_max. The earliest send uses the minimum delay, so a fast packet is not early. The latest send uses the maximum delay, so a slow packet is not late. The DU window is therefore narrower than the RU window by T12_max − T12_min.
Q: And in the uplink?
A: Ta4_min = Ta3_min + T34_min and Ta4_max = Ta3_max + T34_max. The DU must accept everything the RU can send across the full delay range, so its window is wider by the UL delay spread.
Q: EVM fails on a few symbols per slot while channel power and ACLR are fine on an O-RAN RU. What do you suspect?
A: Late DL U-plane packets. The RU zero-fills or drops symbols whose IQ missed the T2a window. I would read rx-window-stats (RX_LATE / RX_EARLY), confirm PTP lock on both ends, and check whether the DU L1 real-time cores are shared or overloaded.
Q: Why does fronthaul jitter matter more than average latency?
A: A constant delay can be absorbed by moving the DU window earlier. Variation (T12_max − T12_min) shrinks the DU send window directly, and once it exceeds the RU buffer depth (T2a_max_up − T2a_min_up) no valid send time exists.
Q: Where does a DU learn the RU's windows?
A: From the RU delay profile in the o-ran-delay-management YANG module over the M-plane (t2a-min-up, t2a-max-up, ta3-min, ta3-max, the C-plane values and tcp-adv-dl). The transport delays T12/T34 can be configured into the RU in the same module.

Reading an alarm correctly
• An alarm instance is the triple fault-id + fault-source + severity. The same ID can be active on several sources (for example 15 on rffe-0…rffe-3), and those are separate alarms.
• Affected objects decide the blast radius. When the affected objects are all carriers, the RU operations manager holds every TRX down until the alarm clears, so one critical file alarm can take the whole radio off air.
• Timing matters as much as the ID. 17 then 18 some minutes later is the normal PTP-loss story. 16 then 15 then 25 right after a firmware upgrade is the identity-loss story. 15 appearing exactly when carriers are reconfigured, with no other symptom, may be benign.
• Severity maps to the LEDs: non-critical IDs give green fast blink, critical ones red fast blink on the RU or RF LED; sync IDs go to the FH LED (LED01).

How the Common-AAU suite proves raise and clear: 23 test cases in six groups (Thermal, Config/File/SW, Unit/Reset/Reboot, Sync, RF/Power/Transceiver, HO/Soak) inject real triggers while a UE is attached and TCP data flows, check the alarm is raised, remove the trigger, check it clears and traffic recovers. One run: 17 pass, 3 fail, 3 skip in 3 h 28 min. The three fails were "alarm did not raise on injection", which pointed at the injection method, not the product.
Level 3 — triage rules from real RCAs, and spec details that trip people up
Triage rules that came out of real cases
1. Every boot, every unit, every chain at once (GRDEV-8838: 15 + 26 on all four RFFEs) means a common cause: firmware, thresholds or packaging. Four simultaneous hardware failures are far less likely.
2. Present on one branch, absent on another (GRDEV-9046: release branch only) means packaging or regression, not the unit. Compare the scripts that differ.
3. File alarms: check the file, then the source. For 12/13/16 look at the file size first. A 0-byte file with a readable physical EEPROM (a direct I2C read shows serial and revision) points at the firmware step that dumps it.
4. A BBU error can be the symptom of an RU fault: "failed to receive reply" on a NETCONF write was the RU FPGA (fault 15) crashing mid-RPC. Line up RU alarm timestamps with the BBU log.
5. RX-out-of-order is not always a broken receiver. On low band it was the ADC clipping at DSA 0 (a configuration problem fixed by DSA). On one B28 unit it was a genuine LNA/sensitivity fault. The test that separates them: does raising DSA move the over-range point without moving the noise floor? A real receiver fault shows up as poor sensitivity at any DSA.
6. Benign alarms exist. Count occurrences against carrier reconfigurations before calling a fault (the 38 unit-out-of-order lines equalled the number of reconfigurations).

Spec details that trip people up: fault 18 must not be reported during the normal initial synchronisation at start-up, so a fault 18 at boot deserves a second look: either sync took abnormally long or the RU is reporting outside the spec. The vendor range starts at 1000, so 2003/2005 carry no O-RAN meaning at all. Fault 24 (unit blocked) only exists if the RU supports the optional hardware-state feature.

Q: An AAU shows fault 12 "Critical file not found" on its b41 carriers only. What do you check?
A: Which file: the alarm source names it. Q: Fault 17 and then fault 18 appear some minutes apart. What happened?

Q: Fault 15 "Unit out of order" is in the log 38 times after a conformance run. Is the unit broken?
A: Not necessarily. A: IDs 1–41 are the O-RAN common alarms with a defined meaning (20 = RX out of order). 1000–65535 is the vendor-specific range, so an ID such as 2003 has only the meaning the vendor documents.

Level 2 — how each one was narrowed, step by step
1. GRDEV-8976: "the config says 15 MHz, the radio still behaves like 10 MHz"
• First cut: is it the config layer? sysrepo and NETCONF both reported the new bandwidth, so the M-plane write had succeeded. The fault had to be below the datastore.
• Second cut: what does the signal path actually use? The DFE channel filter was still shaped for 10 MHz. With a 15 MHz carrier behind a 10 MHz filter the outer PRBs are cut off. SIB1 is scheduled across that region, so its PDSCH CRC fails and no UE can attach. That explains a "network" symptom from a "radio" bug.
• Third cut: which code path? Two routes set the filter, and both were broken in different ways. At run time lphy_config_device() was only called from the branch that handles a centre-frequency change; a width-only change keeps the centre, so it never ran. After an ruReset, lphy_config_init() sized the filter from the personality default instead of the startup.xml value, so there was no old-versus-new difference to trigger a reload either.
• Fourth cut: why only this branch? The PWK branch forked from develop on 22 June, after an LPHY refactor but before the GRDEV-8199 fix merged on 1 July. develop already had the fix; the release branch was never affected because it programs bandwidth by a different route. Same root cause as 8199, hence "Duplicate" and a cherry-pick (PR #2064).
• Proof: 10 → 15 MHz on all four B28 TX and RX array carriers, ruReset, filter reported "3xNR15MHz Configuration" with config_error 0. CM17 covers the boot-time bandwidth chain this sits on.

2. GRDEV-9046: "fault 12 on every b41 bring-up"
• First cut: unit or software? The alarm appeared on release/REL_2026.2.x but not on the PWK branch. A unit or provisioning problem would not care which branch is loaded; this followed the branch, so packaging or a regression.
• Second cut: who creates the file? The start-up script decides which calibration EEPROM to read by matching the model name. The b41 sub-variant under test was not in its list, so it fell through to read_calibration_eeprom rffe, and tx_calibration_eeprom.json was never produced.
• Fix design: do not whitelist models. Ask the personality for the flavour and match on the band family, which covers current and future b41 models.
• Why an RF engineer cares: an uncalibrated TX carrier is what a B41 power deficit looks like (the ~6 dB B41 shortfall discussed in GAIN_CAL/L28 appears with fault 12). Always read the alarm list before chasing a power miss.

3. GRDEV-8838: "every RFFE over-current on every boot"
• First cut: how many at once? Faults 15 and 26 on all four RFFEs simultaneously, every boot. Four PA supplies failing together at every power-up is implausible; a shared supervisor or firmware is the natural suspect.
• Second cut: where does the decision come from? The mid-board firmware (V1.5 board, FW v2.6) reads over-current status through registers 0x03ED / 0x03EE on I2C. Reading those registers directly separates "the supervisor claims over-current" from "the rail is actually overloaded".
• Status: fixed in the mid-board firmware delivered with release 2026.2.2 and verified on a bench unit. It was not an RCT-found bug, but the RCT verification record was the most detailed evidence on the ticket.

Level 3 — traps found in the real archive (and how to parse safely)
1. CONTROLLER1 is a mirror. Its voltage, current and power are identical to CONTROLLER0, and its temperature reads 0. Adding both double-counts 35–50 W per flavour, about 120 W for the AAU. Use CONTROLLER0 only, or simply use B1 + B2.
2. Sentinel values = failed sensor read. In one 100 %-load scenario the whole low-band flavour reported VOLTAGE/CURRENT 6553.5 (= 0xFFFF × 0.1), POWER -1310.71, band totals -655.355, and all temperatures 0. A naive sum gave a negative AAU power. Treat V > 100, I > 100 or P < 0 as invalid. Do not replace them with 0, which would fake a saving. Re-run or exclude the flavour. The GRDEV-9526 work (CM14) later fixed several RU-side PSU read failures of exactly this kind.
3. Isolated zero samples. Watch for min = 0 while avg > 0, e.g. B41 RFFE0 min 0.000, max 1.139, avg 0.922 A. One dropped sample drags the average down. It was most common on the B41 (TDD) flavour. Keep the avg, but flag minutes with min == 0 and check that they do not cluster in one test arm.
4. Missing blocks. In one run, 11 of 20 files held 4 one-minute blocks instead of 5. Always align by the row's start timestamp, never by file index × 5. Also expect the first block after enabling to be missing.
5. Parsing details. The start time uses +00:00 and the end time uses Z. Object ids can carry a trailing space ("O-RAN-RFFE-CONTROLLER0 "). Walk the tokens as (id, "min", v, "max", v, "avg", v) groups rather than using fixed column numbers, because the POWER row is longer (it adds B1/B2).
6. Resolution. The RFFE current moves in steps of about 0.0325 A. At 40 V that gives POWER values of 36.44 / 37.76 / 39.04 / 40.36 / 41.64 W, which is ≈1.3 W per step. The controller current resolves 0.1 A (≈1.2 W). So an "idle 1.27 W" RFFE is one LSB, not a real leakage measurement. A per-flavour ΔP below a few watts in one minute is within resolution. The uSleep or RoP saving you claim must be well above it, averaged over many minutes.
7. Know the boundary before comparing with a lab supply. In the April–May 2026 archive every POWER value is plain rail V×I and the band totals are plain sums, so no PSU-loss correction is visible. The lab supply measures the whole chassis at its DC input. Two real comparisons:

Interview Q&A
Q: An RU's EPE CSV gives O-RAN-B1 = 176 W, but the four RFFEs of band 1 only add up to 152 W. Is the file wrong?
A: No. The band total also includes a share of the RFFE-CONTROLLER (the 12 V FHGW + mid-board rail): half of 47.6 W on a two-band flavour, all of it on the single-band B41 flavour. Flavour power is B1 + B2, which equals the sum of all RFFEs plus CONTROLLER0 once.
Q: Your summed AAU power comes out ~120 W higher than get-PSU. What is the first thing you check?
A: Double counting of RFFE-CONTROLLER1. It mirrors CONTROLLER0 for V/I/P, and its temperature reads 0. Summing both adds 35–50 W per flavour. After that, check for mixed builds and for summing min/max instead of avg.
Q: A PM file shows 6553.5 V and -1310.71 W. What happened and how should automation treat it?
A: 0xFFFF × 0.1 is a failed sensor read (a sentinel), not a measurement. Mark the minute or the whole flavour as invalid, exclude it and report the gap. Never coerce it to 0 W: that would look like a huge energy saving.
Q: Why must the lab supply and get-PSU be binned on hh:mm:00 boundaries?
A: O-RAN aligns PM measurement intervals to midnight (seconds mod interval = 0), so each 60-s EPE block runs from :00 to :00. If another source uses free-running minutes from when the script started, you compare different time windows. During warm-up or load changes that shows up as fake error.
Q: PM and the lab DC supply differ by a near-constant ~50 W at light load. Error or boundary?
A: Mostly boundary. The CSV sums rail outputs (RFFE rails + 12 V controller rail) as V×I. The lab supply sees the chassis input, including PSU conversion loss, the common board and cabling. A constant offset points to a boundary difference. An error that grows with load points to the efficiency model, which is what GRDEV-9526 calibrated.

Level 3 — where PW uSleep and RoP fit, and what the data says about the ceiling
uSleep is vendor-internal. The FPGA detects all-zero symbols in the DL IQ and drops the PA enable line (TX_EN AND MC_EN on the RFFE; see RFFE02, USLEEP01), with a fixed advance before the next occupied symbol. No O-RAN message is involved and the DU does not know it happens. The nearest standard idea is a C-plane sleep of the lightest type (SLEEP_MODE_0, guaranteed wake-up). But standard sleeps are commanded by the DU for whole array elements over slots. uSleep is autonomous and data-driven, per symbol. The price is a first-symbol quality risk, so EVM on the symbol after a gap is always part of the test.

RoP (3 / 10 dB) corresponds to MAX-POWER-CONTROL. The standard adds two things PW testers should copy. (1) The DU must keep the IQ level below the reduced maximum (a CUS-plane input-level restriction), otherwise the PA clips at its new bias. (2) Changes must be ordered and confirmed by notification. RoP tests should therefore check ACLR/EVM and DPD re-convergence after each change, not just output power (M13, GRDEV-7438).

Interview Q&A
Q: Name the O-RAN M-plane energy-saving techniques and the one that also stops the M-plane.
A: Carrier deactivation (and carrier SLEEP), RF channel switch-off via C-plane or M-plane TRX control, data-layer control, Advanced Sleep Modes, deep hibernate and maximum output power control. Deep hibernate is the one that stops C/U/S and then the M-plane. The O-RU restarts when hibernate-time expires, with restart-cause DEEP-HIBERNATE-RESTART.
Q: The DU sends deep-hibernate while one carrier is still active. What must the O-RU do?
A: Reject the RPC and include the reason in the error message. All tx and rx array-carriers must be deactivated first. This is a good negative test case.
Q: How is PW uSleep different from O-RAN Advanced Sleep Modes?
A: ASM is commanded by the DU over the C-plane, using advertised sleep types and wake-up durations, and works at array or processing level. uSleep is decided autonomously inside the RU FPGA per empty OFDM symbol, and gates the PA enable line. It is transparent to the DU and not part of any O-RAN interface.
Q: What is the standard counterpart of Reduced Output Power, and what extra obligation does it place on the DU?
A: MAX-POWER-CONTROL, per tx-array index or per carrier reduction in dB. The DU must restrict scheduled PRBs so the IQ level stays below the reduced maximum. It must lower power before increasing the reduction, and only raise power after the max-power-level-used notification confirms a smaller reduction.
Q: Why might carrier re-activation be slow after an energy-saving period, and how do you guarantee it is instant?
A: With energy-saving-enabled=true and no active carrier the O-RU may go SLEEPING and stop the S-plane. Re-activation then waits for sync. Setting energy-saving-enabled=false keeps the O-RU AWAKE and ready to activate immediately.

Interview Q&A
Q: Why do modern sites rarely use TMAs?
A: The RRU or AAU sits within a few metres of the antenna, so less than about 1 dB of loss precedes the receiver. A TMA recovers feeder loss on the uplink; with no long feeder there is little to recover, and it adds a failure point and DL insertion loss.
Q: How is a TMA powered, and how do you know it is working?
A: The radio or a bias-tee puts DC (typically 7–30 V) on the coax. The radio watches the DC current and raises a TMA alarm if it leaves the expected window, and the TMA falls back to bypass. The idle UL RSSI also rises by roughly the TMA gain, which must be entered in the configuration.
Q: Size the battery for a 3.6 kW site that needs 4 hours of backup.
A: 3.6 kW at 48 V is 75 A. 3600 × 4 / (48 × 0.8) ≈ 375 Ah, so fit 400 Ah (two strings of 200 Ah). Also size the rectifiers for load plus recharge current (≈115 A) with one spare module, and remember that cold batteries deliver less capacity.
Q: A tower radio reboots only during mains failures. Why?
A: Voltage drop. On battery the bus falls towards 44–46 V, and the IR drop in a long, thin DC cable takes the radio below its minimum input voltage. Check the cable cross-section against the load current and length; the fix is a thicker cable or a DC booster.
Q: Why is site earthing tested in the dry season?
A: Soil resistivity is highest when dry, so that is the worst case. A value that meets the limit only after rain will fail when it matters.
CM25 On-Site Path Verification — VSWR/return-loss sweep, distance-to-fault, PIM, fibre (loss budget, OTDR) and microwave backhaul checks: procedures, maths and pass criteria Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Before a site goes live, the engineer proves that the "pipes" are clean: the RF path from radio to antenna, and the transport (fibre or microwave) back to the network. A line sweep sends a signal into the cable and measures how much comes back — a good path swallows almost everything. Distance-to-fault (DTF) uses the same data to show where a reflection happens, like sonar. For fibre, an optical power meter and an OTDR do the same job with light. For microwave, the received level must match the link design. Every trace is saved; it becomes the reference for all later maintenance.

Level 2 — procedures: RF sweep, DTF, PIM, fibre and microwave
Line sweep / DTF (cable and antenna analyser):
  • Lock the cell or switch off TX for that port. The sweep is a passive, TX-off test: the analyser replaces the radio. Never connect an analyser to a transmitting port.
  • Set the frequency range of the band on that port. Calibrate open–short–load at the end of the test lead, including the adapter you will use.
  • For DTF enter the cable type: velocity factor (foam feeder ≈0.88, superflex jumper ≈0.82–0.83) and loss per metre.
  • Sweep RL/VSWR for the whole line, then DTF. Save both per port with site, sector and port in the file name. Reconnect, torque and weatherproof.
DTF maths: d = vf·c·t/2. Resolution Δd ≈ 1.5×10⁸·vf/span(Hz): about 0.13 m for a 1 GHz span, about 1.3 m for a 100 MHz span. Maximum range = (points − 1) × Δd. A wrong velocity factor places the fault at the wrong height (0.80 instead of 0.88 is a 10 % error).
Symptom → cause: all bands high → common cable, jumper or connector; one band only → antenna element, diplexer/filter or RET section; reading changes with weather or touch → loose or wet connector; very low RL (<5 dB) → open, short or crushed cable.

Level 3 — pitfalls and expert habits
  • Sweep in-band only. An antenna is matched only in its passband, and a diplexer or TMA reflects everything outside its filter. An out-of-band "fail" means nothing.
  • The aggregate number can hide one bad joint. A line that passes RL overall but shows a 20 dB spike halfway still needs fixing, because that joint will get worse.
  • AAU sites have no external RF path to sweep. Checks move to fronthaul optical power, the radio's built-in VSWR and antenna-calibration results, and UL noise per branch.
  • Radio-side cross-checks need no instrument: idle UL noise per PRB (LTE, ≈−119 to −116 dBm: −121.4 dBm thermal in 180 kHz plus receiver NF); RX branches within ~2–3 dB of each other; UL noise that rises with DL load points to PIM.
  • OTDR habits: use a launch (and tail) cable so the first connector is not hidden in the dead zone. Measure splices from both directions and average, because a single direction can show "gainers".
  • Microwave rain fade grows sharply above ≈10 GHz. A link that passes on a dry commissioning day can fail its availability target in the rainy season if it was accepted with low margin.
  • Keep every trace. A later DTF or OTDR trace compared with the acceptance baseline shows exactly what changed and where to climb.
Corrections to common training sheets: RL values are positive (a joint "at RL 28 dB" is healthy; printing "−28 dB" means S11). A sweep is not done while transmitting at low power. VSWR 1.0–1.5 is not "excellent" for a full line: 1.5 is only 14 dB RL.
Interview Q&A
Q: Convert VSWR 1.5 to return loss. Is it good?
A: Γ = 0.5/2.5 = 0.2, so RL ≈ 14 dB, with 4 % of the power reflected and 0.18 dB mismatch loss. The loss is small, but for a new installation it is only marginal. Many operators want RL ≥ 17–18 dB (VSWR ≈1.3) for the antenna line.
Q: A sweep from the bottom of a 60 m feeder passes, but the site drops calls in rain. What next?
A: Run DTF with the correct velocity factor and cable loss, and compare it with the baseline. Feeder attenuation masks a bad joint at the top in the aggregate RL. Check UL noise per branch in wet and dry conditions, and do a PIM test. Water in a top connector is the usual cause.
Q: Why must the velocity factor be right?
A: DTF converts round-trip time to distance using the propagation speed in the cable. A 10 % error puts you metres away from the real fault on a tall tower.
Q: Rx optical power on a fronthaul link is −13.8 dBm with an LR SFP. Pass?
A: It is within the window but only about 0.6 dB above the ≈−14.4 dBm sensitivity, so there is no margin. Clean and inspect the connectors, check for tight bends and bad splices with the OTDR, and aim for several dB of margin before accepting.
Q: What proves a microwave hop is correctly aligned?
A: RSL within a couple of dB of the planned value on both ends (not a side lobe, which reads 20 dB or more lower), expected cross-polar discrimination, and an error-free service test at the planned capacity.

Level 3 — radio datafill rules that the planning tools enforce (and training sheets forget)
  • PCI collision: two neighbouring cells with the same PCI. PCI confusion: one cell has two neighbours with the same PCI, so a handover can go to the wrong one. Both must be avoided.
  • Mod rules: in LTE keep co-site and adjacent cells on different PCI mod 3 (PSS and CRS positions) and different PCI mod 30 (uplink reference-signal sequence groups). In NR use different mod 3 (PSS) and mod 30 (uplink DMRS). The simple pattern X, X+1, X+2 for three sectors of one site satisfies mod 3.
  • No PCI values are "reserved" by 3GPP — a rule like "never use PCI 0 and 1" is an operator habit at most.
  • TAC design: a tracking area that is too large overloads paging; one that is too small causes TAU/registration storms. Keep high-handover clusters in one TA and avoid TA borders along highways and railways.
  • Neighbours: intra-frequency, inter-frequency and inter-RAT relations; ANR can add them automatically but initial lists come from the plan. Typical A3 settings: offset about 1–5 dB, hysteresis 1–3 dB, time-to-trigger 160–480 ms (TTT is chosen from a fixed list from 0 to 5120 ms). An A3 offset of −6 dB would trigger on a weaker neighbour, so treat such values in a plan with suspicion.
  • Cell parameters: bandwidth (LTE 1.4–20 MHz, NR FR1 up to 100 MHz), TX power per port (e.g. 43 dBm = 20 W for an LTE 20 MHz carrier; always within the radio rating and the RF plan), antenna ports, cell range, SSB position (NR), barred flag.
For an RU test engineer this is the field twin of the lab set-up: the same frequency, power and sync parameters that RCT verifies (CM16 shows how a carrier is activated inside the node).
Interview Q&A
Q: SCTP to the MME is ESTABLISHED but S1 Setup keeps failing. Where do you look?
A: At the S1 Setup Failure cause and the identities it carries. The PLMN and TAC must be configured on the MME, and the eNB ID must be allowed and unique. Transport is already fine because SCTP is up.
Q: Which ports would you open on a firewall for X2 and Xn?
A: SCTP 36422 for X2AP and SCTP 38422 for XnAP, plus UDP 2152 for GTP-U data forwarding. 36412 (S1AP) and 38412 (NGAP) are the core-facing ports that people copy by mistake.
Q: Convert EARFCN 1650 and NR-ARFCN 636000 to frequencies.
A: EARFCN 1650 is in Band 3: 1805 + 0.1 × (1650 − 1200) = 1850 MHz downlink. NR-ARFCN 636000 is in the 3–24.25 GHz range: 3000 + 0.015 × (636000 − 600000) = 3540 MHz.
Q: Why is NTP not enough to synchronise a TDD cell?
A: NTP delivers time of day with millisecond-level accuracy over the network, and no frequency reference. TDD needs ±1.5 µs phase alignment and ±0.05 ppm frequency, which require GNSS or PTP with suitable network support (plus SyncE if used).
Q: Explain PCI confusion with an example.
A: Cell A has two neighbours, B and C, which both use PCI 150. When a UE reports PCI 150, cell A cannot tell which one is meant and may prepare the handover towards the wrong cell. The fix is to re-plan one of the two PCIs.

Level 3 — how acceptance goes wrong
  • Conditional acceptance: minor items → accept with a punch list and dates; major items (open critical alarm, KPI miss, safety defect) → fix and re-test. Classify honestly: marking a site PASS with majors open contradicts the gate rule and returns as a dispute later.
  • Top rejection reasons: missing or inconsistent documents (a photo that does not show the label, traces not saved per port), high earth resistance, unsealed connectors, poor cable management, crossed sectors found in the drive test, configuration that differs from the plan, incomplete O&M handover.
  • Separate the site from the cluster: a new site can miss drop or handover targets because of a neighbour's tilt or a missing relation. Break KPIs down per neighbour relation before accepting the blame.
  • Pre-run everything: the customer usually witnesses a sample of cells. Run the full set yourself first so the witnessed run is a formality.
  • Numbers come from the contract: generic material often quotes different targets for the same KPI (>95 % on one page, ≥98 % on another). Always use the KPI annex of the contract.
  • Lab-to-field chain: field ATP is the last link after lab RCT and conformance (L37). Margins shown in the lab (ACLR, EVM, sensitivity) are what keep field KPIs stable through temperature and ageing.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is the difference between SCFT and SSV?
A: SCFT tests each cell's functions next to the site in good RF: attach, data, voice, SMS, intra-site handover. SSV is a drive test around the site that checks the footprint per sector, PCI versus azimuth and handovers to real neighbours. It catches crossed feeders and overshooting, which SCFT cannot see.
Q: What is the difference between PAC and FAC?
A: Provisional acceptance lets the site carry commercial traffic while minor punch items stay open. Final acceptance comes after a KPI observation period, once every item and document is closed. It is usually tied to payment and the start of the warranty.
Q: The drive test shows sector B's PCI in sector C's direction. What happened, and how do you prove the fix?
A: Crossed feeders or fibres, or a wrong cell-to-radio mapping in the configuration. Correct the cabling or mapping, then repeat the PCI-versus-azimuth drive and the per-branch RSSI check, and attach the new results to the punch-list item.
Q: How do you test alarm integration during commissioning?
A: Create real conditions: pull a fronthaul fibre, open the cabinet door, switch mains off so the site runs on battery. Confirm that each alarm reaches the NMS with the correct severity, object and timestamp, and that it clears when the condition is removed.
Q: Your new site misses its drop-rate target, but its radio and transport are clean. What next?
A: Break the drops down by cause and by neighbour relation. Drops often cluster on handovers to one neighbour (missing relation, PCI conflict, the neighbour's tilt). Fix it or document it as a cluster issue, so the site is not rejected for a problem it did not cause.

Correlation habits:

Interview Q&A
Q: The NOC reports 40 alarms on a site at once. How do you start?
A: Sort by time and by layer. Take the earliest alarm in the lowest layer (power, then transport, then sync), fix that and watch whether the others clear. Most of the list is usually consequences.
Q: A VSWR alarm appears every morning and clears by midday. Likely cause?
A: A connector or jumper that lets in moisture, or whose contact changes with temperature: dew at night, drying and expansion during the day. Check the weatherproofing and compare DTF with the acceptance trace to find the joint.
Q: GNSS is lost but the cells are still on air. Is that OK?
A: Only for a while. The clock is in holdover and stays within limits for a vendor-dependent time. Fix the GNSS before holdover expires, because TDD cells normally stop transmitting once phase accuracy can no longer be guaranteed.
Q: Ping to the MME works but the S1 alarm stays. What else do you check?
A: The SCTP association (a firewall may pass ICMP but block SCTP 36412), the peer addresses configured on both ends, and the S1 Setup result. A PLMN or TAC mismatch fails at the application layer even when transport is fine.
Q: Why is preventive maintenance worth the truck roll?
A: Most site outages come from slow degradation: ageing batteries, drying weatherproofing, clogged filters, drifting optical levels. Finding them on a planned visit is much cheaper than an outage, and trend data (VSWR, optical Rx, battery backup time) shows which sites to visit first.
Cloud-native RAN platform
CM29 Cloud-Native RAN Platform — Kubernetes for vRAN, CPU pinning/isolation, huge pages, DPDK/SR-IOV, NUMA, RT kernel: what goes wrong and how to check Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
A virtualised or cloud-native RAN runs the baseband (DU/CU) as software containers on ordinary servers orchestrated by Kubernetes. The catch: the physical layer has hard deadlines — an LTE subframe or NR slot is processed in well under a millisecond, and fronthaul packets must arrive inside a microsecond-scale window (CM19). A normal Linux server is tuned for average throughput, not for "never late". So the platform is carefully bent into a real-time machine: dedicated CPU cores nobody else may touch, huge memory pages so lookups are fast and predictable, DPDK and SR-IOV so packets bypass the kernel, everything placed on the same NUMA node, and a real-time kernel. When one of these is wrong, the RAN does not crash neatly — it produces rare late slots, HARQ failures and throughput dips that look like radio problems. This card is generic (no specific product).

Level 3 — what goes wrong in real deployments
  • "Rare late slot" syndrome: throughput fine in short tests, then a few HARQ failures per hour and occasional UL CRC bursts. Root causes: an IRQ (e.g. storage or management NIC) migrated onto an isolated core by irqbalance, a kernel worker thread, or a monitoring agent exec-ing into the pod. Fix: pin IRQs to housekeeping cores, disable irqbalance or ban isolated cores, keep agents off RT cores.
  • Cross-NUMA placement: the pod got cores on socket 0 but the SR-IOV VF or FEC card sits on socket 1 (the device plugin did not report NUMA or Topology Manager was "none"). Symptom: higher CPU load per cell and load-dependent jitter.
  • Hugepages fragmented: 1 GB pages must be reserved at boot; allocating at runtime often fails after uptime. Pod stays Pending with "insufficient hugepages-1Gi".
  • SR-IOV lost on reboot: VFs created by a manual echo into sriov_numvfs disappear after reboot; the DU pod then cannot start. Use the operator/daemon that re-creates VFs, and include a reboot in every acceptance run.
  • Firmware/driver mismatch: NIC firmware, kernel driver and DPDK PMD versions must be a supported combination; PTP hardware timestamping in particular breaks silently.
  • SMT/hyper-thread siblings: pinning an RT thread to one logical CPU while its sibling runs something noisy — allocate full physical cores (CPU Manager option "full-pcpus-only").
  • Throttling: CPU CFS quota applied to a Guaranteed pod on older setups causes micro-throttling; check cpu.stat nr_throttled.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why does a DU pod need Guaranteed QoS?
A: Only Guaranteed pods (requests = limits, integer CPUs) get exclusive cores from the kubelet static CPU Manager; without exclusivity RT threads share cores and miss slot deadlines.
Q: What do isolcpus, nohz_full and rcu_nocbs do?
A: isolcpus removes cores from the general scheduler, nohz_full stops the periodic tick on them when a single task runs, rcu_nocbs moves RCU callbacks off them — together they remove OS noise from RT cores.
Q: Why DPDK plus SR-IOV for fronthaul?
A: SR-IOV gives the pod a hardware NIC function directly; DPDK polls it from user space with no interrupts or kernel copies, which keeps latency low and predictable at high packet rates.
Q: How do you check NUMA alignment for a running DU?
A: Compare the pod's exclusive CPUs (cpu_manager_state) with lscpu NUMA mapping, and the numa_node of the NIC PF and accelerator in sysfs; all should be on the same node, and Topology Manager should be single-numa-node.
Q: Throughput dips a few times an hour on a vRAN cell — first checks?
A: cyclictest on the RT cores, /proc/interrupts for IRQs on isolated cores, C-state/frequency with turbostat, NIC drop counters, PTP offset history, and whether any other process was scheduled on those cores.
CM30 Upgradability & Resilience — rolling/in-service upgrade, NIC & RU firmware, multi-node cluster failure, geo-redundant SMO, load profiling Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
A mobile network is expected to stay up 24/7, yet its software changes every few weeks and its hardware eventually fails. Upgradability is about changing software and firmware — baseband pods, NIC firmware, radio-unit firmware — with the least possible outage and a guaranteed way back. Resilience is about surviving failures — a crashed process, a dead server, a lost data centre for the management system — and recovering automatically. Load profiling tells you how much headroom you really have before either of these becomes dangerous. This card collects generic patterns and, most importantly, how to test them. See CM04 (RRH firmware upgrade), CM29 (the platform), ZT4 (NSA resilience tests).

Level 1 — resilience building blocks
  • Process/pod level: liveness/readiness probes, restart policies, PodDisruptionBudgets so voluntary operations never take all replicas; SCTP (S1/NG/X2/F1) re-establishment after restart.
  • Node level: pods reschedule to healthy nodes — except hardware-bound DU pods, which need a pre-provisioned standby node with the same NIC/FEC/fronthaul reach (N+1 pooling).
  • Cluster control plane: 3 (or 5) control-plane nodes; etcd needs a majority (quorum = ⌊N/2⌋+1), so 3 nodes survive 1 failure, 5 survive 2. Losing quorum freezes changes but running pods keep running.
  • Geo-redundancy of management/SMO: two sites, data replicated (synchronous for small RPO, asynchronous across long distance), a witness/arbiter to avoid split-brain, defined RTO (time to restore service) and RPO (data you may lose). Network elements must reconnect to the surviving site automatically (multiple NETCONF call-home / VES endpoints, DNS or VIP switchover).
  • RU level: dual software slots, watchdogs, supervision (M-plane keep-alive; RU stops transmitting if supervision is lost — CM05).

Load profiling: build a traffic model (users per cell, active ratio, attach/HO/paging rates per hour, throughput mix), run it at 50/80/100/120% of target, and record the worst core and the 99.9th-percentile L1/L2 processing time — averages hide the late slots that matter. The headroom left at 100% is what upgrades, failovers (where one node absorbs another's load) and traffic peaks will consume.

Level 3 — pitfalls learned the hard way
  • "Zero-downtime" that isn't: rolling updates of a stateful service without externalised state drop sessions; readiness probes that turn green before SCTP links are up send traffic to a pod that cannot serve it.
  • Version skew: during a rolling upgrade old and new versions run together — interfaces (internal APIs, config schema, database migrations) must be backward compatible for at least one version.
  • Firmware compatibility matrix: a NIC firmware upgrade that works with the new driver but not the old one turns a rollback into an outage. Always test the rollback path, not only the upgrade.
  • Standby DU never tested: failover capacity exists on paper but the standby node has a different BIOS, NIC firmware or VF setup. Periodic scheduled failover drills catch it.
  • Split-brain in geo-redundancy: both sites become active after a link cut and both push configuration to network elements. A witness in a third location or strict fencing is mandatory.
  • Memory leaks show only in soak tests; a service that grows 50 MB/day will be OOM-killed weeks later at busy hour. Track RSS trends, not snapshots.
  • Alarm storms during upgrades hide real faults; suppression windows must be scoped and must end automatically.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why can't a DU be upgraded with a normal Kubernetes rolling update?
A: A surge copy would need the same exclusive hardware (SR-IOV VFs, FEC, fronthaul link to the RU) on the same node, which is already in use. So a DU upgrade means a short cell outage unless a standby DU/node or overlapping coverage takes the users.
Q: How does O-RAN RU firmware upgrade stay safe?
A: Via M-plane software management: download and install into the inactive slot, then activate and reset. If the new image fails to boot or connect, the RU falls back to the previous slot.
Q: How many control-plane nodes do you need to tolerate one failure?
A: Three — etcd needs a majority (2 of 3). Five nodes tolerate two failures.
Q: What are RTO and RPO?
A: RTO is the maximum time to restore service after a failure; RPO is the maximum amount of data (time window) that may be lost. Geo-redundancy design and replication mode are chosen to meet both.
Q: What would you record in an upgrade-under-traffic test?
A: Per-cell outage duration, dropped calls/sessions, KPI recovery time, alarms raised/cleared, configuration integrity after upgrade, and the same metrics for rollback.
