Common / Platform FeaturesCROSS-TECH
Cross-technology & platform — RU/OAM, fronthaul (O-RAN 7.2), transport, timing · plain-English + how-it-works + interview Q&A
Public-knowledge write-ups of features shared across LTE and NR — RU/OAM, O-RAN fronthaul planes, transport and timing. Concepts only, no internal specifics. Each card feeds the Interview simulator.
CM01 RU Transmit-Power Validation Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Confirm each radio actually transmits the power it's told to, across bands and antennas — a core RU acceptance check.
How it works
A spectrum analyser measures the RU output per carrier/antenna; the framework compares measured power to the configured target within tolerance, accounting for cable/setup loss and any splitter correction, and flags branches that are low/high.
Interview Q&A
Q: How do you validate an RU's transmit power?
A: Drive the RU to a known configured power, measure the actual output on a calibrated analyser per antenna/carrier, subtract the measured cable/setup loss, and check it lands within tolerance of target. Deviations point to PA gain, calibration, or a bad TX branch.
CM02 RU Recovery & Fault-ID Procedures Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
When a radio hits a fault, recover it automatically and record why — instead of a manual truck roll.
How it works
The RU/OAM detects a fault ID (e.g. PLL unlock, thermal, fronthaul loss), runs the matching recovery (reset the affected block, re-init, or controlled reboot), and logs the cause for RCA. Escalation reboots the whole unit only if targeted recovery fails.
Interview Q&A
Q: How should a radio handle a recoverable fault?
A: Detect and classify it by fault ID, attempt the least-disruptive recovery first (re-init the affected function), and record the cause and action for root-cause analysis. Only escalate to a full reboot if targeted recovery fails — minimizing outage and giving traceability.
CM03 M-Plane (Management Plane) Aggregation Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
The management channel to the open radio — and a way to funnel many radios' management through one point.
How it works
O-RAN M-plane (NETCONF/YANG) configures and monitors the O-RU. Aggregation proxies multiple O-RUs' M-plane sessions through a manager, simplifying scale and the SMO's connection count. (M-plane is one of the fronthaul planes alongside C/U/S.)
Interview Q&A
Q: What are the O-RAN fronthaul planes and what does M-plane do?
A: C-plane (scheduling/beamforming commands), U-plane (IQ user data), S-plane (timing/sync), and M-plane (management). M-plane uses NETCONF/YANG to configure, software-manage and monitor the O-RU; aggregation lets a manager front many O-RUs' M-plane to the SMO.
CM04 RRH Firmware Upgrade Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Update the radio's firmware safely in the field without bricking it or dropping the site unexpectedly.
How it works
The new image is transferred, validated (checksum/signature), staged to a backup bank, then activated with a controlled restart; a fallback to the previous bank triggers if the new image fails to come up — a dual-bank A/B scheme.
Interview Q&A
Q: How do you make a field firmware upgrade safe?
A: Verify the image (signature/checksum), write to an inactive bank so the running one is untouched, activate with a controlled reboot, and auto-roll-back to the previous bank if the new image fails health checks. A/B banking means a bad upgrade never bricks the radio.
CM05 OAM–RRH Heartbeat Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
A periodic "are you alive" between the management layer and the radio so failures are caught fast.
How it works
The OAM/RRH manager and the radio exchange periodic heartbeats; missing several in a row marks the radio unreachable and triggers alarms/recovery. It also detects silent fronthaul/link failures the data plane might mask.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why have a heartbeat between OAM and the radio?
A: To detect loss of management connectivity quickly and deterministically — a few missed heartbeats flag the node down and kick off alarms/recovery, catching silent link or process failures that wouldn't otherwise surface until a user-impacting fault.
CM06 GTP-U Counters Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Measure the user-plane tunnel traffic between RAN and core to spot loss, drops and throughput issues.
How it works
GTP-U carries user data over S1-U/F1-U/N3 (per-bearer TEIDs). Counting packets/bytes, sequence gaps and error indications per tunnel exposes transport loss, path MTU problems and where throughput is bottlenecked.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is GTP-U and why count it?
A: GTP-U is the user-plane tunnelling protocol carrying subscriber data across S1-U/F1-U/N3, keyed by TEIDs per bearer/flow. Per-tunnel counters (packets, bytes, dropped, error indications) let you localise packet loss and throughput bottlenecks to the transport rather than the air interface.
CM07 Unified E2-Plane Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
A common approach to the control interface toward the near-real-time RIC across technologies.
How it works
O-RAN E2 connects the RAN (E2 nodes) to the near-RT RIC so xApps can monitor and control RAN functions via E2 service models (KPM for metrics, RC for control). A unified E2-plane presents 4G/5G nodes consistently to the RIC.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is the E2 interface and the near-RT RIC for?
A: E2 links RAN nodes to the near-real-time RIC, letting xApps subscribe to metrics (KPM service model) and issue control (RC service model) on a ~10 ms–1 s loop — e.g. traffic steering or interference control. A unified E2-plane exposes different RAT nodes to the RIC the same way.
CM08 AISG v2 & Fronthaul Split Option 7.2 Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
AISG controls antenna-line devices (like remote electrical tilt); Split 7.2x defines where the PHY is divided between DU and radio in O-RAN.
How it works
AISG is the standard control bus to RET/TMA antenna-line devices. Split 7.2x is the O-RAN low-PHY split: the O-DU does high-PHY, the O-RU does low-PHY (iFFT/CP, beamforming), connected by eCPRI carrying frequency-domain IQ — balancing fronthaul rate vs radio complexity.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is fronthaul split 7.2x and why was it chosen?
A: It splits the PHY so the O-RU handles low-PHY (iFFT/CP add, digital beamforming) and the O-DU handles high-PHY and above, exchanging frequency-domain IQ over eCPRI. Compared with a full-PHY-in-radio (option 8/CPRI) it cuts fronthaul bandwidth while keeping the radio relatively simple — the O-RAN mainstream choice.
CM09 4G + 5G on the Same BBU Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Host both LTE and NR on one baseband platform to save hardware, power and site space.
How it works
The shared BBU partitions compute, fronthaul and timing between the LTE and NR stacks; they share transport and sync while running independent cells, which also simplifies EN-DC anchoring on the same box.
Interview Q&A
Q: What are the benefits and challenges of 4G+5G on one BBU?
A: Benefits: less hardware/power/space, shared transport and timing, and easy EN-DC anchoring. Challenges: partitioning CPU/accelerator and fronthaul capacity so neither RAT starves the other, and keeping timing/sync and software lifecycles for both stacks isolated yet coordinated.
CM10 External Alarm & Environmental Monitoring Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Bring site/cabinet alarms (door, temperature, power) into the same management view as the radio.
How it works
An external alarm box maps dry-contact/environmental inputs to OAM alarms; thresholds (e.g. over-temperature) raise faults that operators see alongside RF alarms, enabling proactive action before a hardware trip.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why integrate external/environmental alarms with RAN OAM?
A: So site conditions (power loss, high temperature, intrusion) surface in the same fault management as radio alarms, letting operators correlate and act before an environmental problem forces a thermal shutdown or outage — rather than discovering it only after service drops.
CM11 Timing & SFN Synchronization Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Keep every cell aligned in time and frame number — essential for TDD, handovers and features like CA/MIMO.
How it works
Sites lock to a common reference via GNSS and/or PTP (IEEE 1588) + SyncE over the transport; the S-plane delivers timing to the O-RU. TDD needs tight phase alignment so neighbouring cells don't transmit into each other's receive windows.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why is timing/phase sync so critical in TDD, and how is it delivered?
A: In TDD all cells must switch between UL and DL at the same instant; a phase error means one cell transmits while a neighbour is receiving, causing cross-link interference. Sync is delivered by GNSS and/or PTP (1588) with SyncE over the fronthaul (the O-RAN S-plane), holding phase within tens of nanoseconds.
CM12 Band Plans, Sub-bands & RF Front-End Filter Passband (why a "same-band" carrier may not be supported) Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
A 3GPP band is a wide range of frequencies — far wider than any single operator's licensed block. The AAU/RU's RF front-end (duplexers, cavity/SAW filters, combiners, PA/LNA matching) is built in hardware for a specific passband, chosen at design time. You cannot software-retune a physical filter. So a unit can be "band X capable" yet still not support a particular carrier in band X if that carrier falls outside the passband the filters were cut for — you need a hardware variant with a different filter, not a config change.
How it works
Each band has full DL/UL ranges — e.g. Band 28 (APT700 FDD): UL 703–748 MHz, DL 758–803 MHz (45 MHz duplex). An operator is licensed a sub-block within that (10–15 MHz somewhere in the range). A multi-band AAU that shares a low-band front end (e.g. B8/B20/B28 together) has its filter passbands + combiner + PA/LNA designed for chosen sub-ranges, captured in the block diagram / gain budget at design time. Near a passband edge, insertion loss rises (filter roll-off) → less TX power and worse RX sensitivity; outside the passband the signal is rejected. 3GPP also sometimes splits a wide band into variants (a lower vs upper sub-range) so a narrower-passband radio can target one half — a radio cut for one variant won't cover the other. Bottom line: center frequency ≠ band; map the deployment's exact DL & UL centers onto the AAU's designed passband before committing.

The edge can be asymmetric — TX vs RX
A duplexer has separate TX and RX passbands (independent filter paths), so a unit can reach a sub-block on the downlink but not the uplink (or vice-versa). In practice the receive filter is usually the tighter constraint: the transmitter can still deliver near-full EIRP across the band edge while Rx sensitivity collapses just outside its passband. So "the band isn't supported" often really means "the Rx filter doesn't cover it" — always characterise TX and RX separately. If a variant is needed, the change may be confined to the receive filter.
How to diagnose a passband-edge limitation
1. Sweep across the edge: a gentle few-dB roll-off just inside turning into a sharp collapse (tens of dB) just outside = a filter skirt, not a flat/uniform loss.
2. Look within one channel: if attenuation increases across the channel (lower RBs read much stronger than upper RBs), that frequency-selective tilt is the signature of a passband edge — a flat loss would hit all RBs equally.
3. Rule out firmware: reproduce on a different SW build — if the roll-off is identical, it's the hardware filter, not calibration/FW.
4. Don't trust the nominal edge: a carrier inside the supposed passband can still fail (roll-off starting earlier, or a bandwidth-specific issue) — treat the edge as characterised only after you've actually swept it, not from the datasheet number.
Interview Q&A
Q: A unit transmits fine into the upper part of a band but can't receive there — what's going on?
A: TX and RX use separate filter paths in the duplexer, so the DL passband can cover a sub-block the UL (Rx) filter doesn't. The receive filter is usually the tighter limit — you get near-full EIRP on the downlink but sensitivity collapses just outside the Rx passband. It's an Rx-filter limitation, not a whole-band one, so a fix may only need a new receive filter.
Q: How do you prove a band-edge sensitivity failure is the Rx filter, not firmware or a flat loss?
A: Three checks: (1) reproduce on a second SW build — identical roll-off ⟹ hardware, not FW/calibration; (2) look within the failing channel — if lower RBs are much stronger than upper RBs, that frequency-selective tilt is a passband skirt, not a uniform loss; (3) sweep across the edge — a sharp collapse just outside vs gentle roll-off just inside confirms the filter skirt.
Q: An operator is licensed in band X and your radio is "band-X capable", but it doesn't support their carrier. How?
A: The band is a wide range; the radio's RF front-end filters/duplexer are cut in hardware for a specific passband — usually one sub-block or band variant. If the operator's block falls outside that passband, the filter rejects it (or heavy roll-off kills performance). You can't retune a physical filter in software, so it needs a hardware variant with the right filter. Center frequency, not just band number, decides support.
Q: Why do you validate low / mid / high (band-edge) channels in TX and RX tests?
A: Filter insertion loss and roll-off are worst at the passband edges, so TX output power and RX sensitivity degrade there. Testing low/mid/high (and the exact deployment center frequency) proves the unit meets ACLR/EVM/sensitivity across the whole used block, not just the easy mid-band point.
Q: What's the difference between a band and a band variant (e.g. A vs B)?
A: 3GPP partitions some wide bands into variants covering different sub-ranges, so a radio with a narrower filter passband can target one part. A unit built for one variant's passband won't cover the other variant — same band number, different hardware filter.
CM13 Spectrum Sensing & Spectrograms — the RU as a sensor, visualised at the SMO Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Beyond serving traffic, a modern O-RAN radio (AAU/RU) can double as a wideband spectrum sensor: it captures the raw RF, computes a power-vs-frequency picture (a PSD / spectrogram), and streams it up to the management system (SMO/EMS) so operators can see the spectrum remotely — interference, rogue emitters, incumbents — in real deployments, not just on a lab debug port.
How it works
The radio captures time-domain IQ samples (wide bandwidth, high sample rate) in the FPGA, and the on-board CPU runs repeated FFTs, averaging over ~1 s to produce a Power Spectral Density (PSD) vector — power in each frequency bin. Resolution bandwidth ≈ sample_rate / FFT_size; a finer RBW (e.g. 15 kHz vs 30 kHz) = more bins, sharper detail, more data. A spectrogram / waterfall stacks PSD traces over time (X = frequency, Y = time, colour = power); the top trace is the latest PSD.
Transport: early/lab designs dump the PSD over a dedicated debug Ethernet port to a PC; the productised path carries it up the O-RAN fronthaul + M-plane (NETCONF/YANG) to the SMO/EMS as a periodic notification, so it works in the field. Because the payload is periodic (~1/s) and sizeable, encoding matters — sending 16-bit integer (deci-dBm) instead of 32-bit float roughly halves the on-wire size to fit the M-plane budget (plain file-upload used for PM counters is too slow for 1-second cadence).
Uses: interference hunting, PIM / rogue-emitter detection, incumbent sensing (DFS / CBRS-style), spectrum-occupancy monitoring, and feeding dynamic spectrum sharing / SON.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is a PSD / spectrogram and how does a radio generate one?
A: PSD = power per frequency bin, from the FFT of captured IQ (usually averaged over time to reduce noise). A spectrogram (waterfall) plots PSD over time — frequency × time × power. The radio captures wideband IQ in the FPGA, the CPU FFTs and averages it, and outputs the bin vector (e.g. thousands of bins per second).
Q: What sets the frequency resolution (RBW) of a spectrogram?
A: RBW ≈ sample_rate / FFT_size. A larger FFT gives finer resolution but more data and more compute; you trade spectral detail against data rate and processing. Sensing config picks RBW (e.g. 15 kHz for LTE/NR µ0, 30 kHz for µ1).
Q: Why carry sensing data over the O-RAN M-plane instead of a debug port, and what's the challenge?
A: A debug port is lab-only; real deployments need the data at the SMO/EMS via standard interfaces — the M-plane (NETCONF/YANG) over the fronthaul. The challenge is that the payload is periodic (~1/s) and large, versus the M-plane budget, so you compress/encode it (e.g. int16 deci-dBm instead of float32) and use NETCONF notifications rather than slow periodic file upload.
CM14 Power Monitoring & PSU Efficiency — self-reported RU power vs an independent reference Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
A modern RU/AAU reports its own power consumption (power-monitor / energy telemetry) — used for energy-efficiency KPIs, GreenRAN reporting, and power/thermal budgeting. But the radio only directly senses its DC input; to state "power consumed" it must correct for PSU (power-supply) conversion efficiency. If that efficiency model is wrong, the reported number drifts from reality — and the error tends to grow with load.
How it works
The RU measures its DC input (e.g. the −48 V feed: voltage × current) and applies a PSU-efficiency correction to estimate delivered power. The catch: PSU efficiency is load-dependent — a curve, not a constant (usually lower at very light and very heavy load, best mid-range). Using a datasheet / nominal efficiency instead of the unit's actual factory-measured efficiency injects a systematic, load-dependent error.
Validate against an independent reference: put a lab power analyzer on the common DC input and sweep many load scenarios (idle → max), comparing the RU's self-reported power against the analyzer. Calibrate by replacing the datasheet model with a factory-measured efficiency LUT (lookup table) → software update → re-validate end-to-end.
The residual gap has several contributors — don't blame one: PSU efficiency model, PSU telemetry accuracy (the sensor reading itself), and DC cable loss (−48 V feed resistance). After calibration, low/mid-load accuracy improves sharply; a small few-percent, load-dependent under-read can remain at high load — now quantified and treated as the next calibration step rather than an unknown.
Whole-AAU power = sum of per-PSU rails, with a built-in cross-check
A whole-AAU power reading is built up: a multi-PSU AAU (e.g. 3 PSUs) each converts the DC feed on several rails (e.g. a 50 V and a 12 V rail). Total estimated input = Σ over PSUs & rails of (rail output ÷ rail efficiency) = output power + modelled conversion loss. Below the characterised efficiency range the curve is extrapolated (flagged) and least trustworthy at light load. Crucially the AAU has two independent internal sensors: (a) this per-rail efficiency-model estimate, and (b) an independent on-board direct input measurement (a common-board V×I sense). Comparing them is a built-in sanity check — and they differ, with the model estimate reading lower.
Why the model estimate is a lower bound
• The estimate models only the converters + loss curve — it does not include unmodelled internal cable losses or the common board's own draw, which sit outside the model boundary → so the estimate is a lower bound; the independent direct measurement reads higher.
• Telemetry resolution limits it (e.g. 0.1 V / 0.1 A steps ≈ several watts per current step on a high-voltage rail).
• TDD bands (PA switching on/off continuously) make it harder for current sensors to capture true consumption → extra error on TDD vs an FDD/CW load.
• Different power-reporting paths use different sensors, so their numbers won't match — always know which sensor/model a figure came from.
Interview Q&A
Q: An AAU reports total power two ways and they differ by tens of percent — why, and which is more trustworthy?
A: One is an efficiency-model estimate (Σ per-PSU-rail output ÷ efficiency + datasheet loss) — it excludes unmodelled cable and common-board losses, so it's a lower bound. The other is an independent on-board direct input measurement (V×I) that captures everything downstream and reads higher. Neither is "wrong" — different sensors/models: the direct measurement is closer to true input, the model gives the per-rail breakdown. Reconcile with cable loss + telemetry resolution + (on TDD) PA-switching sensor error.
Q: Why is per-unit power telemetry harder on a TDD band?
A: TDD PAs switch on/off continuously, so instantaneous current swings hard; sensors with limited sample rate/resolution can't capture the true average, adding error versus a steady FDD/CW load. It's a measurement-accuracy effect, not a real power difference.
Q: An RU reports its own power draw but reads low — and worse at high load. Why, and how do you fix it?
A: It senses DC input and applies a PSU-efficiency correction. PSU efficiency is load-dependent, so if the correction uses a datasheet/nominal figure instead of the unit's real (factory-measured) curve, the estimate is systematically off — and because efficiency drops toward high load, the under-read grows with load. Fix: calibrate with a factory-measured efficiency LUT and re-validate against an independent power analyzer across the load range.
Q: How do you validate an RU's self-reported power, and what besides PSU efficiency affects the gap?
A: Compare the RU's reported power against an independent lab power analyzer on the common DC input, across scenarios from idle to max load, and quantify the mean and worst-case error. The residual gap also includes PSU telemetry (sensor) accuracy and DC cable loss on the −48 V feed — so characterise all three rather than attributing everything to the efficiency model.
CM15 BBU ↔ RU Communication — the four O-RAN planes and the full RU lifecycle: boot, call-home, sync, configure, activate, carry IQ, report Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
In an O-RAN 7.2x split the RU is deliberately simple. It does not schedule, decide or attach anyone. It does exactly what the DU (BBU) tells it, symbol by symbol, and reports back. The conversation runs over four planes on the same fibre. Understanding who says what, in which order, is the base for debugging every "carrier not coming up" or "no traffic" problem.

RU lifecycle, message by message
Order matters: each step depends on the one before. A stall anywhere leaves the carriers DISABLED.
Level 2 — what the RU actually does at each step
Boot & POST: the RU checks its own hardware (HW monitor, I2C devices, eMMC, Ethernet, the clock chip, driver node) and loads its identity (model name, serial, calibration) from EEPROM-derived files. A unit that cannot identify itself cannot be configured safely.
Transport: DHCP on the M-plane VLAN gives an IP and tells the RU where the NETCONF client (the DU/SMO) is.
Call-home: the RU initiates the SSH/TLS session to the NETCONF client, which then acts as the manager. In the <hello> the RU lists the YANG modules it supports.
Capability read: the DU reads what the RU can do: bands, max carriers per band, min/max carrier bandwidth (o-ran-module-cap), array/endpoint layout (o-ran-uplane-conf) and hardware inventory. The DU must not ask for more than this.
Supervision: a watchdog. The DU must keep refreshing it, and if it stops, the RU assumes the manager is gone and takes its carriers down safely (no uncontrolled transmission).
Sync: the RU's sync-manager locks PTP/SyncE and reports LOCKED. Many later steps are sync-gated: no transmission until timing is right (TDD especially).
Configure: the DU writes processing elements (MAC/VLAN ↔ endpoint mapping), low-level endpoints (eAxC IDs) and array-carriers (centre frequency, bandwidth, gain) into the RU datastore (sysrepo). The RU's operations-manager validates them and programs the FPGA/DFE.
Activate: setting the carriers active makes the RU enable DAC and PA for them. The carrier state goes from DISABLED to READY/ACTIVE and a notification goes back.
Run: from now on C-plane messages arrive ahead of each symbol and the U-plane carries IQ. The RU must receive each packet inside its timing window, otherwise it drops it and counts it.
Report: alarms (FM), PM counters and files (RSSI, TX/RX statistics, energy/EPE), software management (download, install, activate) and troubleshooting logs, all over the M-plane.

Interview Q&A
Q: Name the four O-RAN fronthaul planes and what each carries.
A: M-plane: NETCONF/YANG management (config, alarms, PM, software). S-plane: PTP + SyncE timing. C-plane: per-symbol control telling the RU which PRBs/beams/compression to use. U-plane: the frequency-domain IQ samples, DL and UL, including PRACH. C and U run over eCPRI on Ethernet.
Q: Who initiates the M-plane connection, and why?
A: The RU, with NETCONF call-home: after DHCP it opens the SSH/TLS session to the DU/SMO NETCONF client, which then manages it. It solves the discovery problem, since the RU may get a dynamic address and the manager does not know it yet.
Q: What is M-plane supervision for?
A: It is a watchdog. The manager must refresh it periodically. If refreshes stop, the RU assumes it has lost its manager and takes its carriers down in a controlled way, so a radio is never left transmitting with nobody in control.
Q: Why is carrier activation "sync-gated"?
A: The RU must not transmit until its PTP/SyncE timing is locked. Otherwise its symbols would be misaligned with the DU and neighbours (fatal for TDD, and harmful interference in general). Steps like L1-Done/RRH-Ready and PA enable wait for a LOCKED state.
Q: The OAM shows the cell in service but no UE can attach. How do you find the truth?
A: Check bottom-up: RU carrier state in its datastore (ACTIVE?), RU alarms, DU fronthaul counters (U-plane RX from the RU non-zero?) and finally KPI availability counters. A cell state derived from the stack's intent can be green while the RU carriers are disabled.

Level 2 — the five hand-offs where it breaks
1. PTP / S-plane: the RU must be LOCKED before sync-gated steps. Holdover ("no external sync source"), slow re-acquisition or non-recovery after reboot stalls everything downstream.
2. Per-band frequency config: in one build the controller keyed processing elements on (vNode, carrier) without band, so two bands collided and one was silently skipped ("processing element already exists").
3. L1-Done / RRH-Ready: this is sync-gated. If emitted before In-Sync it is dropped as "not_synced", and in that build it was not re-driven on In-Sync, which deadlocks the activation.
4. TX power: energy-saving logic can disable TX power, and a co-located RU's energy-saving storm can saturate the controller/IPC and starve the target RU. An "Invalid gain configuration" alarm flags the gain churn (carriers flapping READY↔DISABLED).
5. ModifyPA / activation (RU): only after a valid TX-power message does the RU apply the PA and flip the carrier to ACTIVE.
Level 3 — case study: the outage after an RU firmware upgrade (three defects at once)
What was seen: after the RU firmware upgrade, both RUs rebooted in a loop, then came up clean (PTP LOCKED, no alarms), but all carriers stayed DISABLED forever. A full stack restart fixed it for about 6 minutes, until the next RU crash.

Defect 1 (BBU): configuration never re-driven after an RU reconnect. The per-cell frequency table lived only in the per-connection controller process, so it died with the session. The RRH manager did detect the disconnect/reconnect but only raised/cleared an alarm. OAM had sent SET_FREQ once at cell bring-up and considered provisioning done, so nothing re-sent it. Every later TX-power attempt aborted with "Frequency not yet configured for cell". Fix directions: replay the stored carrier map to the new session on RU connect, or propagate the disconnect so OAM re-drives, or have the controller pull the frequency when it is missing. Regression test: kill the RU M-plane mid-service and expect reconnect → frequency config → carriers READY without any pod restart.

Defect 2 (RU firmware): identity and calibration lost, FPGA crash at activation. After the upgrade the identity file derived from EEPROM was written as 0 bytes on every boot and the TX calibration file disappeared. The physical EEPROM read fine directly over I2C, so it was the firmware's dump step, not hardware. Faults followed: file not found, unit unidentified, FPGA out of order, self-reset. The FPGA fault fired seconds into applying the carrier/TX-power config: activating an unidentified front end crashed it, the NETCONF agent died mid-RPC, and the BBU logged "failed to receive reply". That BBU error was a symptom, not the cause.

Defect 3 (stack reporting): false cell-up. The eNB OAM reported RF TX status true from the cell state machine's intent, while the same report showed the PA state invalid. In 5G the DU declared the cell in service a minute before the carrier activation ran and failed. PHY fronthaul TX counters were flowing but RX = 0. Fire-and-forget eCPRI "transmitting" is not proof of RF on air. The KPI/PM availability (≈0) was the truthful witness.
Level 4 — the debugging method that worked (reuse it)
Kill false leads with a frequency histogram. Several scary ERROR lines appeared in the failure window, but counting them per hour across the whole day showed they also occurred during perfectly healthy hours, so they were background noise and were discarded. The rule: if a signature occurs when everything works, it is not your cause.
[1] count a suspect signature per hour across the whole day
grep -a '<signature>' messages | awk '{print substr($1,12,2)}' | sort | uniq -c

[2] was provisioning sent at all after the reconnect?
grep -a 'SET_FREQ' messages | awk '{print substr($1,12,2)}' | sort | uniq -c

[3] carrier state truth (READY = alive; only DISABLED after an event = dead)
grep -a 'ArrCarrStateCb' messages | grep -oaE 'new state "[A-Z]+"' | sort | uniq -c

[4] RU side: faults and identity files (a 0-byte file = firmware dump problem)
tail -20 alarms.log ;  ls -la <identity/calibration files>
Trace one reconnect cycle end to end (connect → In Sync → SET_FREQ → freqConfigd → L1-Done → RRH-Ready → SET_TX_POWER → UserPlaneConfiguration → state READY) and find the first missing line. Truth-check a "green" cell bottom-up: RU carrier state → PHY fronthaul RX counters → KPI availability. Any one of them exposes a false green. Separate harness from radio: a CLI cold-start race delaying a reboot, or an expired UE automation session reporting "UE didn't latch", can look like RAN faults. Rule them out before filing.
Interview Q&A
Q: A carrier stays DISABLED although the RU is up, PTP LOCKED and alarm-free. What do you check?
A: Walk the activation chain for the latest RU session: was the NETCONF client connected, was In-Sync received, and was the per-band frequency config (SET_FREQ → freqConfigd) sent after this connection? Then L1-Done/RRH-Ready, SET_TX_POWER and the UserPlaneConfiguration write, and finally the carrier state callbacks. The first missing step is the break. A classic case is configuration that is not re-sent after an RU reconnect.
Q: The BBU logs "failed to receive reply" on the TX-power NETCONF write. Is the BBU at fault?
A: Not necessarily. In the case study the RU's FPGA crashed seconds into applying the config (the unit had lost its identity/calibration after a firmware upgrade), killing the NETCONF agent mid-RPC. The BBU error was the symptom. Check RU alarms and reboot cause at the same timestamp.
Q: How do you decide whether an ERROR line in the log is your root cause?
A: Check its frequency over time. Count occurrences per hour across the whole day. If it also appears during known-healthy periods, it is background noise. Only signatures that appear or disappear with the failure are candidates.
Q: Why can a cell show "in service" while the RU transmits nothing?
A: The state may be derived from the stack's intent (the cell FSM or MAC cell-start toward its own PHY) rather than the RU's actual carrier state. PHY TX counters on eCPRI are fire-and-forget, so they flow even if the RU is not transmitting. Cross-check RU carrier state, fronthaul RX counters and KPI availability.
Q: What regression test proves a "reconfigure on reconnect" fix?
A: Kill or restart the RU M-plane session mid-service (or reboot the RU) and expect, without restarting any BBU pods: reconnect → frequency config re-sent → TX power → carriers READY/ACTIVE → traffic resumes.
CM17 Channel-Bandwidth Change on the AAU — why editing startup.xml does nothing, the boot-time config chain, and the band-capability limits Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
You want a carrier at 15 MHz instead of 20. The obvious move is to edit the RU's startup.xml, but after the reboot the old value is back. That file is generated on every boot from another file. Change the source (bw_mhz in on_the_fly_configs.json), reboot, and verify. The RU also advertises what bandwidths it can do to the BBU, and the BBU must stay inside that.
Boot-time bandwidth configuration flow
Only the first box is yours to edit. Everything after it is regenerated or loaded automatically at boot.

Valid carrier BWs: 5 MHz = 5000000, 10 = 10000000, 15 = 15000000, 20 = 20000000 (the values are in Hz). These come from the O-RAN module-capability model. A BBU request outside them (e.g. a 3rd carrier, or 2 × 20 MHz + 5 MHz) must be rejected, and that is a classic "config rejected" cause (CM15).
Level 3 — the procedure
[1] edit the source (OLD/NEW are Hz values, e.g. 20000000 -> 15000000)

[2] reboot the FHGW
reboot

[3] verify after boot: generated file, running datastore, then the carrier on air
grep channel-bandwidth <configs>/<model>-startup.xml
sysrepocfg -X -d running -m o-ran-uplane-conf | grep -i -A2 channel-bandwidth
Then verify on air: channel power/OBW on the analyser (FC17: 99% OBW ≈ 4.5/9/13.5/18 MHz for 5/10/15/20), and that the BBU cell BW matches the RU. A mismatch shows up as no attach or throughput far below the KPI.
Level 4 — pitfalls
• The edit "didn't take": you edited startup.xml, or the sed pattern did not match (check the exact JSON spacing and the value in Hz), or you forgot to reboot.
• BW changed on the RU but not on the BBU (or the reverse): the carrier may activate but the PRB grid does not match, so no attach or garbage throughput. Change both, then check the cell config.
• Test limits depend on BW: ACLR/SEM/OBW limits and KPI thresholds (e.g. the 2CC throughput KPI per BW in LF19) change with bandwidth. Re-select them.
• PRACH and PUCCH placement move with NRB. Recompute the FHGW PRACH register (PRA02).
Interview Q&A
Q: You edited startup.xml on the RU to change the bandwidth, but after reboot it is back. Why?
A: startup.xml is regenerated on every boot by a startup script from on_the_fly_configs.json. The permanent change is to edit bw_mhz in that JSON (value in Hz), reboot, then verify the generated startup.xml, the running sysrepo datastore and the on-air OBW.
Q: What does the RU's band-capability advertisement contain and why does it matter?
A: Per band: max total supported bandwidth (e.g. 40 MHz), max and min carrier bandwidth (20 / 5 MHz) and max number of carriers (2). The BBU must configure within these limits. A request outside them is rejected, and that is a common cause of config failures after a planning change.
Q: After a bandwidth change, what else must you recheck?
A: The BBU cell bandwidth (it must match the RU), PRACH/PUCCH placement and the FHGW PRACH register (they depend on N_RB), the test limits and KPIs that are per-bandwidth (ACLR/SEM/OBW, throughput), and the occupied bandwidth on air.
CM18 MCU Firmware Flashing with J-Link (SWD) — mid-board debug connector, pin mapping, JFlashLite procedure and what can go wrong Common click to expand ▾
In plain English
Besides the big FPGA/CPU software (upgraded over the M-plane, CM04), the AAU boards carry small microcontrollers (housekeeping, sequencing, sensors). When one of them needs new firmware and cannot be updated in-system, you program it directly through its debug port with a SEGGER J-Link probe over SWD (Serial Wire Debug). It is a hands-on bench job, and wiring is 90% of it.
Level 1 — SWD basics
SWD is ARM's 2-wire debug interface: SWDIO (bidirectional data) and SWCLK (clock), plus GND, optionally RESET, and VTref. VTref is not a power supply. It lets the probe sense the target's I/O voltage so its drivers match it. Without VTref the J-Link reports no target voltage and will not connect.
That is why the procedure says the unit must be powered on and fully booted: the MCU and its VTref come from the board, not from the probe.
Level 2 — where to connect and the pin mapping
The AAU has a 5-pin debug connector on each board: (1) mid-band (B1/B3), lower layer; (2) B41, upper layer; (3) low-band. Pick the connector of the board you flash.

On the J-Link 20-pin header, pin 1 is at the top-right in the reference photo. Odd pins are on one row and even pins on the other, and most of the even pins are GND.
Level 3 — the procedure (JFlashLite)
1. Install the J-Link software package (Windows installer), then launch JFlashLite.
2. Click … next to Device and select the target MCU. On this board it is a TI MSPM0G-series part, so type the exact device name given in the procedure and select it. Interface: SWD.
3. Select the firmware file (the mid-board programmer .bin).
4. Set Prog. addr. = 0x00000000 (a .bin has no address information, so you must supply it. A .hex/.elf would carry its own).
5. Click Program Device. Progress runs and the log ends with "Done."
6. Power-cycle the unit so the MCU boots the new image, then verify the firmware version from the unit CLI/inventory.

Interview Q&A
Q: What is VTref on a J-Link and what happens if you leave it unconnected?
A: VTref is the target reference voltage input. The probe measures the target's I/O voltage to set its signal levels. It does not power the target. With it unconnected the J-Link sees no target voltage and refuses to connect, which is one reason the board must be powered and booted while flashing.
Q: Why must you enter a programming address when flashing a .bin but not a .hex?
A: A .bin is a raw byte image with no address information, so the tool must be told where to place it (0x00000000 for this MCU's flash). Intel-HEX and ELF files carry their load addresses.
Q: Name the SWD signals and their J-Link 20-pin positions used here.
A: RESET → 15, SWDIO → 7, GND → 4, SWCLK → 9, VTref → 1. The 5-pin board header is RESET, SWDIO, GND, SWCLK, VTref from left to right.
