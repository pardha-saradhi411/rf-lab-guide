16a RF Signal Path — TX/RX building blocks explained: LNA, PA, Duplexer, Mixer, Filter Beginner–Intermediate click to expand▾

RF path components — duplexer, BPF, LNA, VGA, mixer, ADC

Component purpose and NF contribution (B1 RX chain):
Duplexer: separates TX and RX paths at antenna port. Insertion loss 0.5 dB → NF contribution 0.5 dB. TX rejection >50 dB (prevents PA from entering LNA).
Pre-BPF: rejects out-of-band blockers before LNA. Loss 0.3 dB → NF 0.3 dB. Worth it — without it, a strong blocker could compress LNA.
LNA: NF 2.0 dB, gain +20 dB. Most critical component. Sets system NF. GaAs pHEMT.
Mixer: NF 7 dB, but after LNA gain ÷100 = 0.07 dB contribution. Negligible.
VGA: variable gain ±30 dB range. AGC-controlled. Keeps ADC at −12 dBFS.
ADC: 12-bit, 74 dB SQNR, 122.88 MSPS.
TX path components — FHGW to PA:
DAC: converts digital IQ to analog. 16-bit, 122.88 MSPS. Clock from GPS-locked reference.
Upconverter: baseband IQ → RF. Mixer × LO frequency. IQ imbalance here → constellation rotation.
PA: GaN, Class AB, +43 dBm output, ~35% efficiency. Protected by: thermal shutdown, VSWR protection, over-current detection.
Duplexer: TX side routes PA output to antenna. Loss 0.5 dB from rated power.
Failure modes per component:
LNA dead → sensitivity fails, all columns for that band. LNA compressed (blocker) → NF increases, sensitivity degrades but LNA not dead. VGA/AGC stuck → RSSI reads wrong, may clip ADC. ADC_ADJUST wrong → RSSI off by fixed offset. PA degraded → TX power low, ACLR may worsen. Duplexer cracked → TX-to-RX leakage → RX desensitisation, possible LNA damage.
Duplexer / T-R Switch — first after antenna
In FDD (B1/B3/B8/B28): the duplexer simultaneously passes UL and DL on separate ports with >50 dB TX→RX isolation. It stops the PA's +43 dBm TX power from flooding the LNA input and desensitising the receiver. In TDD (B41): a T/R switch replaces the duplexer — faster switching, but cannot transmit and receive simultaneously on the same antenna.
BPF — Band-pass filter
Placed after the duplexer (and sometimes again after the LNA) to provide additional out-of-band rejection. Blockers passing through the duplexer with only 50 dB suppression are further attenuated, protecting the LNA from strong interferers that would cause compression and desensitisation. Also suppresses the image frequency before downconversion.
LNA — the most critical component
David: "you have here the LNA, right? Low noise amplifier... if we receive the signal in low power, we amplify it. This is why we have the LNA here."
The LNA amplifies the weak incoming signal (maybe −80 dBm from a distant UE) while adding minimal noise. By Friis formula: the first stage dominates system NF. A 3 dB NF LNA before a 10 dB NF mixer gives system NF ≈ 3.3 dB. Swap them, and system NF ≈ 10 dB — 6.7 dB worse sensitivity.

VGA — Variable Gain Amplifier (AGC engine)
After the LNA, the VGA provides adjustable gain (typically −20 dB to +20 dB range). The AGC loop reads RSSI and adjusts VGA gain to keep the signal level at the ADC input within the optimal range — not too strong (clipping) and not too weak (buried in quantisation noise). David: "we calibrate the RF chain to make sure we are not compressing the ADC and we are not transmitting in too much low power."
Mixer + Local Oscillator (LO) — frequency downconversion
The mixer multiplies the RF signal (e.g. 1747.5 MHz for B3 UL) by the LO signal at the same frequency → output is the difference frequency = baseband (0 Hz for direct conversion / zero-IF architecture). Modern RUs use zero-IF: I and Q channels come out at baseband, 90° apart. Together they fully represent the complex baseband signal including both amplitude and phase.
LO phase noise directly limits EVM. Phase noise at 10 kHz offset from the LO causes ICI between adjacent LTE subcarriers (spaced 15 kHz apart). This is why EVM tests fail when the reference oscillator ages or runs too hot.

12-bit ADC — dynamic range, SQNR, dBFS, clipping

SQNR and what 12 bits gives you:
SQNR (Signal to Quantisation Noise Ratio) = 6.02×N + 1.76 dB where N = number of bits. For 12 bits: SQNR = 6.02×12 + 1.76 = 74 dB. This is the ratio between the full-scale signal and the quantisation noise floor. 74 dB of dynamic range means signals up to 74 dB above the quantisation noise floor are cleanly digitised. A 16-bit ADC would give 98 dB — used in audio. RF systems use 12–16 bits because the VGA/AGC keeps the signal in a narrow range.
Operating point — why −12 dBFS is the target:
Operating at 0 dBFS (full scale) means any instantaneous peak clips → severe distortion → wrong RSSI, high EVM. Operating too low (−40 dBFS) wastes dynamic range — more quantisation noise relative to signal. −12 dBFS is the standard operating point: 12 dB of headroom for signal peaks above average, and 62 dB of SQNR still available below the signal. The VGA/AGC loop constantly adjusts gain to maintain this target.
Clipping — what happens and how to detect:
When input exceeds full scale (0 dBFS), the ADC output saturates at the maximum code (all 1s). RSSI reading wraps around or saturates at a wrong value. On EXA: flat-top distortion visible on spectrum (compressed waveform). In CLI: get_rssi returns a value close to 0 dBFS regardless of actual input power. Fix: reduce gain (check VGA/AGC settings, check if LNA is in compression from a strong blocker).
What the ADC does
The ADC (Analog-to-Digital Converter) is the boundary between the analog RF world and digital processing. It samples the baseband IQ signal at the Nyquist rate and converts each sample to a binary number. David: "we are working with 12 bits. Sorry, 11 bits. It means 2 by 11 steps of resolution."
Resolution: N bits → 2^N amplitude levels 11-bit ADC: 2^11 = 2048 levels 12-bit ADC: 2^12 = 4096 levels SQNR = 6.02 × N + 1.76 dB 11-bit: SQNR = 68 dB dynamic range 12-bit: SQNR = 74 dB dynamic range
Dynamic range — exactly what David drew on the whiteboard
The ADC has a fixed input voltage range from minimum (noise floor) to maximum (full scale). David's explanation: "the dynamic range is the minimum value that the ADC is starting to see some power from the noise itself — to separate the signal from the noise. And the maximum value. This is called dynamic range."

dBFS — decibels relative to Full Scale
David: "DBFS is DB to full scale related to the maximum dynamic range of the ADC." All dBFS values are ≤ 0 dBFS. 0 dBFS = at the clipping threshold. −20 dBFS = 10% of full-scale voltage. −74 dBFS = at the ADC noise floor for a 12-bit converter.
The get_rssi CLI command returns RSSI in both dBm AND dBFS. The dBFS reading tells you where the signal sits inside the ADC's operating range. If dBFS is close to 0 → near clipping, reduce VGA gain. If very negative (−60 dBFS or below) → signal is weak, near noise floor.
David's clipping explanation: "you're cutting the information if you're transmitting too high. You got the information — if this is the time domain and this is the amplitude domain — you're cutting the information." The ADC chops the sine wave peaks flat → massive harmonic distortion → EVM and ACLR both fail catastrophically.
16c RSSI Measurement — CLI dBFS vs OAM dBm: two methods, calibration, what they tell you Intermediate click to expand▾

RSSI — CLI dBFS method vs DU dBm method, when they disagree

CLI method — get_rssi N:
Reads the ADC digital output directly. Returns dBFS value (e.g. −12.5). Converted to dBm via: P_dBm = dBFS + ADC_ADJUST. Fast, per-column, direct hardware reading. Does not go through DU processing. Useful for: initial calibration, column-by-column fault isolation, debugging without DU. Limit: only as accurate as ADC_ADJUST calibration.
DU method — testmac rssi:
Reads RSSI computed inside the DU baseband after FFT processing. Averages power across active subcarriers. Includes interference contributions. Goes through eCPRI fronthaul → FHGW → DU. More representative of what the scheduler sees. More affected by fronthaul issues. Useful for: validating end-to-end chain, checking FHGW BFP accuracy, confirming what the network actually "sees".
Disagreement diagnosis — what each pattern means:
CLI accurate, DU off by 2–3 dB → FHGW BFP decompression issue or eCPRI timing problem → check FHGW firmware.
Both off from EXM by same amount → ADC_ADJUST wrong → re-run RSSI cal.
One column CLI wrong → hardware fault on that column (LNA, cable, connector) → inspect physically.
All columns drift together as unit warms → temperature compensation not set → check TEMP_COMP in hardware.json.
CLI varies but DU is stable → ADC channel mapping wrong in configuration.
Two RSSI measurement methods
David described two independent ways the system measures RSSI:
Method 1 — DU RSSI (dBm): After FFT processing, IQ samples flow to the DU via eCPRI fronthaul. The DU sums power across all subcarriers and computes RSSI in dBm. This is what your test automation reads from the OAM portal or DU CLI. Also: the DU calculates the "blur" (sensitivity point) from these same samples.
Method 2 — CLI RSSI at ADC (dBFS + dBm): The FHGW firmware measures power directly at the ADC output, before FFT or compression. Command: get_rssi. Returns 8 readings (ADC channels 0–7) in both dBm (gain-corrected) and dBFS (raw ADC reference). One reading per antenna element/RX chain.
The gap — and how it was closed
David: "We had a gap between the readings from the DU and the readings from the CLI. So we managed to close this gap by adding new features from the software side to the DU." The gap existed because the two methods have different reference planes, different gain compensation tables, and different averaging windows. The DU software update added calibration offsets to align them.

16d Fronthaul Gateway (FHGW) — how it processes IQ data: eCPRI, BFP compression, timing Advanced click to expand▾

FHGW internals — BFP compression, IFFT, eCPRI, what can go wrong

BFP compression — why it is needed:
Raw 16-bit IQ for a 20 MHz 4T4R system: 4 columns × 2 (I+Q) × 1200 subcarriers × 14 symbols/ms × 16 bits = ~430 Mbps. Standard 10GbE fronthaul supports 10 Gbps — but with overhead, practical is ~8 Gbps. With BFP: each resource block (12 subcarriers) shares one 4-bit exponent, and each sample uses 9-bit mantissa instead of 16-bit. Compression ratio ≈ 9/16 = 56% → bandwidth = ~240 Mbps. Fits easily in 10GbE with margin.
IFFT — frequency to time domain:
The DU schedules which UEs get which subcarriers (resource blocks). It passes the frequency-domain IQ values per subcarrier to the FHGW. The FHGW performs IFFT (Inverse FFT) to convert this to a time-domain OFDM symbol. Then adds cyclic prefix (4.69 μs for normal CP). The resulting time-domain waveform goes to the DAC and then to the RF chain. For UL: the FHGW performs FFT (removes CP first) to convert received time-domain samples back to frequency-domain subcarriers for the DU to decode.
FHGW faults that cause RSSI discrepancies:
BFP expansion error: if the exponent bits are corrupted, the IQ amplitudes are wrong by a power of 2 → RSSI off by 6 dB steps.
IFFT timing: if FHGW IFFT timing drifts, the cyclic prefix overlaps with the data → inter-symbol interference → EVM increase.
eCPRI packet loss: dropped eCPRI packets cause missing subcarriers → DU sees lower RSSI.
Clock reference loss: FHGW loses GPS/PTP sync → frame timing drifts → TDD B41 DL/UL collision.

BFP compression — why it matters for your tests
Block Floating Point (BFP) encodes a block of 12 IQ samples (one resource block) with a shared 4-bit exponent and individual mantissas. Going from 14-bit raw to 9-bit BFP adds quantisation noise equivalent to ~5 bits of resolution loss. This contributes a small but real EVM floor (~0.1–0.3% EVM) even with a perfect PA. Your EVM spec limits must account for this irreducible floor.
The IQ compression ratio directly affects fronthaul bandwidth. A 20 MHz LTE channel (100 RBs × 12 subcarriers) at 30.72 Msps, 14-bit IQ, 2 antennas = ~3.7 Gbps raw. With 9-bit BFP: ~2.4 Gbps. eCPRI over 10GbE handles this comfortably. This is why the fronthaul is specified at 10G/25G in O-RAN deployments.
KTB Thermal Noise Floor — kTB = −174 dBm/Hz, the absolute limit of all receivers Beginner click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRST kTB in one sentence: −174 dBm/Hz is the absolute minimum noise any radio can have — it comes from the random jiggling of electrons in any warm object. You cannot engineer away from this limit. Every sensitivity calculation starts here.
Quick link — how this connects to DSA:
kTB = −174 dBm/Hz is the first term in the sensitivity formula. This card derives it from first principles. The full chain showing how kTB feeds into sensitivity and then DSA is in the DSA card.
→ For the complete chain (kTB → NF → Sensitivity → DSA → PRACH), open the DSA card.

Why −174 dBm/Hz is the absolute floor

Full numerical derivation:
P = kTB = 1.38×10⁻²³ × 290 × 1 = 4.002×10⁻²¹ watts
= 4.002×10⁻¹⁸ milliwatts
= 10·log(4.002×10⁻¹⁸) = −173.98 dBm ≈ −174 dBm/Hz

This is noise power in 1 Hz bandwidth. For any real channel add 10·log(BW_Hz). Common mistake: entering BW in MHz — 10·log(10) = 10 dB, not 70 dB. Always convert to Hz first.
Why 290 K is used even though your lab is warmer:
IEEE Std 60268 defines 290 K as the standard noise temperature for all NF and sensitivity specs. This is a measurement convention — it makes results from different labs and manufacturers directly comparable. At your actual lab temperature of ~298 K (25°C), the real noise floor is about 10·log(298/290) = +0.12 dB higher. This is small enough that all 3GPP specs are written using 290 K as the reference without correction.
🎯 Analogy: kTB is the sound of the universe itself

Imagine you are in a quiet room trying to hear someone whisper. Even if the room is completely soundproofed, you still hear a faint hiss — the hiss of air molecules randomly vibrating against your eardrum. That is thermal noise. In electronics: electrons in any resistor randomly jiggle because they have thermal energy (k×T). This jiggling creates tiny random voltages — the noise you can never eliminate unless you cool the component to absolute zero (−273°C). At 290K (room temp), this noise is exactly −174 dBm per Hz of bandwidth. Every RF receiver starts its noise calculation from this floor — it cannot do better.

Yes — −174 dBm/Hz is the minimum noise at room temperature
You said it exactly right. kTB = −174 dBm/Hz is the minimum possible noise at room temperature. It is not something you can engineer away. As long as your receiver is at 290 K (room temperature), every 1 Hz of bandwidth will contain at least −174 dBm of thermal noise power. The only way to go below this is to physically cool the receiver (cryogenic LNAs used in radio telescopes operate at 4–20 K and achieve noise floors approaching −190 dBm/Hz).
Why 290 K and not 300 K (25°C)?
IEEE defined 290 K as the standard noise temperature reference because it makes kT a round number: kT = 4.0×10⁻²¹ W/Hz exactly. At 300 K it would be 4.14×10⁻²¹ — messier. The difference is only 0.15 dB, negligible for engineering purposes. All 3GPP specs, all Keysight instruments, and all noise figure calculations use 290 K as the reference.
