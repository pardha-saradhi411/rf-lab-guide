DANL and why preamp matters:
DANL (Displayed Average Noise Level) is the EXA's own noise floor — the minimum signal it can display. With preamp OFF and 0 dB attenuation: DANL ≈ −120 dBm/Hz. With preamp ON: DANL ≈ −130 dBm/Hz. For your sensitivity tests, the signal at the RU output is around −97.5 dBm in 10 MHz = −167 dBm/Hz spectral density. EXA preamp ON gives −130 dBm/Hz — 37 dB above the signal. Fine for direct spectrum viewing. But for ACLR on TX: signal is −17 dBm at BOB SMA after pad — preamp OFF absolutely required.
Common EXA mistakes to avoid:
Preamp ON with TX signal → input compression → ACLR reads 10–15 dB better than reality (wrong, fail in field).
Attenuation too low → ADC in EXA compresses → same wrong result.
RBW too wide → noise floor appears higher → sensitivity appears worse.
Not waiting for sweep → single sweep gives noise-contaminated ACLR → always average ≥10 sweeps.
Wrong centre frequency → measuring wrong channel → wrong ACLR offsets.

VNA phase calibration — why phase matters for beamforming

Why phase calibration is required:
Beamforming steers the antenna beam by applying precise phase shifts to each column's TX signal. If Column B's actual phase is 5° different from Column A due to cable or connector variation, the beam will point 2° off from the commanded direction. Over a 200m cell radius, a 2° beam error means the signal maximum is 7m away from where you think it is — reducing effective coverage. Phase cal measures the actual phase of each column and corrects it in the PE44820DS phase shifter register.
What the VNA measures:
S21 at the TX band centre frequency (e.g. 2140 MHz for B1). Source: VNA Port 1 → RU BOB SMA (one column at a time). Sink: VNA Port 2 → reference point. The S21 phase for each column gives: electrical path length from BOB SMA to measurement plane. Difference between columns A, B, C, D = phase mismatch to correct. Target: all four columns within ±2° of Column A. If Column C shows −6.4°, adjust PE44820DS register for Column C by +6.4°.
VNA E5071C — key settings for phase cal:
Freq range: single point or narrow span (±10 MHz) around band centre. IFBW: 1 kHz (low noise for accurate phase). Power: −10 dBm (keep RU in linear region). Cal: SOLT full 2-port, performed at measurement cable tips. Warm-up: 30 minutes before any measurement. Format: Phase (degrees). Do NOT bend cables between cal and measurement — phase shifts by 2–5° per 90° bend.
Phase in your AAU — the core reason
Your Metro AAU has 12 antenna elements (B1/B3/B41) steered by digital phase shifters (PE44820DS). Each element must transmit at a precise phase relative to the others. The beam points in the direction where the phases add constructively. A 5° phase error on one element degrades the beam pattern — the main lobe shifts, the gain drops, and side lobes increase. This is why TX_CAL (transmit calibration) exists and why the VNA is your primary tool for verifying it.

VNA2 VNA — Calibration (SOLT/SOLR, what errors it removes) Intermediate click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRST VNA calibration in one sentence: Before you can trust any VNA measurement, you must remove the errors introduced by the cables and connectors between the VNA and your device. SOLT calibration (Short-Open-Load-Through) does this in 12 steps. Skip it and your S21 phase measurement includes the cable electrical length — meaningless for comparing column-to-column phase.

VNA calibration — SOLT, error models, reference plane

What SOLT calibration removes:
SOLT (Short-Open-Load-Through) calibration corrects 12 systematic errors: directivity, source match, reflection tracking, transmission tracking, load match, and isolation — for both ports. After calibration, the VNA measurement reference plane moves to the tips of the calibration standard, removing cable and connector effects. Skipping cal means your S21 phase includes the electrical length of the measurement cables — meaningless for per-column phase comparison.
12-step SOLT procedure:
1. Warm up VNA 30 min. 2. Set frequency range (e.g. 2100–2180 MHz for B1). 3. Set IFBW 1 kHz. 4. Port 1: connect SHORT, press Measure. 5. Port 1: connect OPEN, press Measure. 6. Port 1: connect LOAD (50Ω), press Measure. 7. Port 2: connect SHORT. 8. Port 2: connect OPEN. 9. Port 2: connect LOAD. 10. Connect THROUGH between ports. 11. Press Apply Cal. 12. Verify: S11 of LOAD should be < −40 dB. Then connect DUT without moving cables.
What invalidates your calibration:
Bending cables after cal, changing adapters, temperature change > 10°C, loosening any connector. If in doubt: re-cal. A 5-minute re-cal is always cheaper than a wrong measurement leading to a wrong register correction.
Why you MUST calibrate before any phase measurement
The VNA measures phase at its test ports. But everything between the VNA port and your DUT (cables, adaptors, connectors) adds phase. Without calibration, you are measuring the phase of your test setup, not the DUT. A 30 cm test cable at 2 GHz adds ~72° of phase. After SOLT calibration, the reference plane moves to the cal kit connector tips — the cable phase is mathematically removed.

Cable electrical length — phase matching, propagation delay

Electrical length vs physical length:
Electromagnetic waves travel slower in coax than in free space. Velocity factor (VF) = speed in cable / speed of light. RG316 (your lab cables): VF = 0.695. A 1m RG316 cable has electrical length = 1 / (VF × c) = 1 / (0.695 × 3e8) = 4.78 ns propagation delay = 4.78 ns × 2140 MHz × 360° = 3680° electrical length. This is why cable length directly determines S21 phase — and why cables to each column must be matched in electrical length (not just physical length).
Phase matching cables for beamforming:
For accurate beamforming, the cable from each column's BOB SMA to the measurement point must be phase-matched. Use VNA to measure S21 phase of each cable at band centre frequency. Target: all cables within ±2° of each other. If one cable is 5° longer, the column will appear 5° phase-shifted — exactly the error you are trying to measure in the RU. Use matched-pair cables (same manufacturer, same batch, same length) or trim electrical length using phase extender adapters.
Port extension — moving the reference plane after cal:
If you cal at the cable tips but then add an adapter, VNA port extension moves the reference plane electrically. Measure the adapter electrical length separately. Enter as port extension delay (ps) in VNA setup. This avoids a full re-cal whenever adapters change.
Why cable phase matching matters
In your test setup, multiple cables connect the FHGW BOB SMA ports to the EXA/EXM. For multi-port measurements (e.g. comparing TX power across 4 antenna elements simultaneously using a switch matrix), each cable must have the same electrical length — otherwise you are adding cable-to-cable phase differences to your measurement. For beamforming calibration, cable phase differences directly appear as beam pointing errors.
Electrical length vs physical length
Electrical length (degrees) = 360 × f × L × √εr / c Where: f = frequency (Hz) L = physical length (metres) εr = relative permittivity of the cable dielectric c = 3×10⁸ m/s For SF141 semi-rigid cable (εr ≈ 2.1, velocity factor VF ≈ 0.695): At 2140 MHz, 1 metre of SF141: Phase = 360 × 2140e6 × 1 × √2.1 / 3e8 = 360 × 2140e6 × 1.449 / 3e8 = 360 × 10.33 = 3720° = 10.33 full wavelengths So 1 mm difference in SF141 cable length at 2140 MHz = 3.72° phase difference For beamforming: target match within ±2° = within ±0.54 mm physical length!

Antenna element phase calibration — per-column measurement

Measurement procedure per column:
1. SOLT cal at cable tips. 2. Connect Port 1 to Column A BOB SMA. 3. Connect Port 2 to reference point (or use a known reference cable). 4. Measure S21 at band centre. 5. Record magnitude and phase. 6. Repeat for columns B, C, D. 7. Compute Δφ = each column phase − Column A phase. 8. Δφ > ±2° → update PE44820DS register for that column by −Δφ (opposite sign correction).
Real B1 example (unit 26050509):
Col A: −125.4° (ref). Col B: −127.1° → Δ = −1.7° (within ±2° ✓). Col C: −131.8° → Δ = −6.4° (FAIL) → adjust PE44820DS by +6.4°. Col D: −126.9° → Δ = −1.5° (within ±2° ✓). When phase error is larger than ±5°:
Δφ > 5° usually means: cable swap (wrong cable connected to that column), connector not fully mated (1–2 turns left to tighten), or physical damage to the RF trace/connector on the BOB. Beamforming angle calibration — phase to beam angle mapping

Beam angle formula and your numbers:
θ = arcsin(λ × Δφ_rad / (2π × d)). For B1 at 2140 MHz: λ = c/f = 3e8/2140e6 = 0.140 m. d = λ/2 = 0.070 m. Δφ = 20° = 0.349 rad → θ = arcsin(0.140 × 0.349 / (2π × 0.070)) = arcsin(0.111) = 6.4°. This means: if each consecutive column is programmed 20° more phase shift than the previous, the beam points 6.4° off boresight.
Per-band beam angle differences:
Different bands have different wavelengths and different element spacings, so the same phase step gives a different beam angle per band. B1 (2140 MHz): 20° Δφ = 6.4° beam angle. B3 (1843 MHz): 20° Δφ = 5.5°. B8 (942 MHz): 20° Δφ = 2.8°. B28 (758 MHz): 20° Δφ = 2.2°. This is why beam calibration is done per band, not once for all bands. The PE44820DS register value for a given beam direction differs by band.
Verifying beam steering with OTA measurement:
After phase cal, verify beam steering optionally using a reference antenna on a tripod at known angle from the AAU. Measure receive power at multiple angles while sweeping phase commands. Maximum received power angle should match commanded angle within ±1°. If not: re-run VNA phase cal, check PE44820DS register writes were accepted, verify mutual coupling between columns is within design spec.
From phase difference to beam angle
For a uniform linear array with element spacing d: Beam angle θ = arcsin(λ × ΔΦ / (2π × d)) Where: λ = wavelength = c/f ΔΦ = progressive phase shift between adjacent elements (radians) d = element spacing (typically λ/2 for maximum scan range) For λ/2 spacing: d = λ/2 → θ = arcsin(ΔΦ/π) ΔΦ = 0° → θ = 0° (broadside, beam perpendicular to array) ΔΦ = 30° → θ = arcsin(30/180) = 9.6° scan ΔΦ = 60° → θ = 19.5° scan ΔΦ = 90° → θ = 30° scan ΔΦ = 180° → θ = 90° (endfire — maximum scan, impractical) Your B1/B3 4-column array (12 elements): Scan range: typically ±60° in azimuth Each 1° phase step → ~0.3° beam shift (at broadside)

The VNA verifies: (1) each state produces the correct phase, (2) insertion loss is consistent across states, (3) there are no stuck bits.

Practical VNA tips — common mistakes, cable handling

Six most common VNA mistakes:
1. Not warming up — LO drifts 5–10° in first 15 min. Always wait 30 min.
2. Wrong torque — SMA spec is 0.9 N·m. Over-torque destroys center pin. Always use torque wrench.
3. Cal at wrong plane — cal WITH measurement cables attached, not before.
4. Skipping isolation step — SOLT needs isolation between ports for accurate S21 at high loss.
5. Wrong IFBW — use 1 kHz for phase cal (slow but accurate), not 1 MHz (fast but noisy).
6. Moving cables after cal — phase shifts 2–5° per cable flex.
Connector inspection before every connection:
Look for: bent center pin (replace immediately), scratches on mating face (replace), debris in connector (clean with IPA and wooden toothpick — never metal). After cleaning, inspect with loupe. A bent center pin costs 2 minutes to catch and replace. A bent pin that makes contact costs a 2-day measurement mystery.
Port extension vs full recal — when to use which:
Full recal: whenever you change cable, add/remove adapter, or temperature changes > 10°C. Port extension: when you want to move reference plane by a fixed electrical delay (e.g. accounting for a short adapter). Port extension does not correct impedance mismatch — it only shifts phase. Never use port extension as a substitute for proper recal when making precision measurements.

Backend fallback order in the lab script: (1) the default VISA (Keysight IO Libraries or NI-VISA); (2) an explicit DLL search over the Keysight, IVI Foundation and System32/SysWOW64 paths; (3) the pure-Python backend ResourceManager("@py").
The bitness trap: 64-bit Python can only load visa64.dll, and 32-bit Python only visa32.dll. Loading the wrong one fails with a Windows "not a valid Win32 application" style error. Hidden trap in the fallback: the script only tries @py if pyusb imports, but pyusb is only needed for USB instruments. For a LAN supply, pip install pyvisa-py alone is enough, so the script can give up when it did not need to.

A drift-free version of the logging loop (condensed; it bins on wall-clock minutes and flags minutes with fewer than 45 samples):
import time, pyvisa

ADDR = "TCPIP0::<psu-ip>::inst0::INSTR"
rm   = pyvisa.ResourceManager()          # or ResourceManager("@py") for pure Python
psu  = rm.open_resource(ADDR); psu.timeout = 5000
assert "N8737A" in psu.query("*IDN?")

def minute_bins(run_s=3600):
    t0 = time.time(); bins = {}
    while time.time() - t0 < run_s:
        ts = time.time()
        v = float(psu.query("MEASure:VOLTage?"))
        i = float(psu.query("MEASure:CURRent?"))
        bins.setdefault(int(ts // 60) * 60, []).append(v * i)   # wall-clock minute, :00 anchored
        time.sleep(max(0, 1 - (time.time() - ts)))
    for m, p in sorted(bins.items()):
        flag = "" if len(p) >= 45 else "LOW-COVERAGE"
        print(time.strftime("%H:%M", time.gmtime(m)),
              f"avg={sum(p)/len(p):.2f} max={max(p):.2f} min={min(p):.2f} n={len(p)}", flag)

Level 3 — pitfalls seen in real runs
1. "Minutes" that are not minutes. The original loop does 60 × (sleep(1) + two SCPI round-trips) and calls that a minute. Each "minute" is really 61–63 s, so after an hour the lab minutes have slid 1–3 minutes against the PM blocks, which are anchored to :00. Its INTERVAL = 60 constant is defined but never used. Fix: timestamp every sample, bin by wall-clock minute, and require ≥ 45 samples per minute. GRDEV-9526 used exactly this gate and got ≈60/min, a max gap of 1.99 s and 0 read errors.
2. V and I are two separate queries. They are not simultaneous. That is fine on a steady load, but adds noise on bursty or TDD loads. Average over the minute and don't trust single samples.
3. Logging dropouts. RUN10 of the archive had 49 minutes instead of 60, and its second-half mean was 209 W against 296 W at the start. That is a logging problem, not a saving. Always print n per minute and the minute count per run.
4. Warm-up drift. A 100 % run started at 1266 W and settled at ≈1424 W (≈ +160 W as the PAs heated). Mid-band both-carriers runs drifted 823 → 869 W in about 20 minutes. Discard the warm-up, or run a cool-down before each arm of an OFF/ON comparison.
5. Clock offset. The RU is PTP-locked; the logger PC is on NTP. GRDEV-9526 measured +0.16…+0.35 s, which is negligible for 60-s windows. An un-synced laptop can be minutes off, though, which silently shifts the whole comparison.
6. Sanitise before sharing. The first line of DATA.txt prints the supply's LAN IP, and the original script saves plots to a personal cloud path. Strip both before a result leaves the lab.
7. Start collection before RF. Start the supply logger, get-PSU and the PM collection first, then activate carriers and IQ playback. The idle segment at the start of the plot is your in-run baseline.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why do you need a lab DC supply if the RU already reports its own power?
A: The RU measures PSU outputs and has to model the conversion losses. It also leaves out the common board and cabling. Only a supply on the chassis input sees the true total. It is the reference for calibrating and validating the RU's EPE POWER (CM14, GRDEV-9526).
Q: pyvisa fails to load visa32.dll on your laptop. What is the likely cause and fix?
A: A bitness mismatch: 64-bit Python needs visa64.dll. Install the 64-bit Keysight IO Libraries (or NI-VISA), pass the visa64.dll path to ResourceManager, or for a LAN instrument use the pure-Python backend: pip install pyvisa-py and ResourceManager("@py").
Q: Your lab-supply average and the PM average disagree most at the start and end of each run. Why?
A: The windows are not aligned. The logger's minutes drift (sleep plus query time) and are not anchored to :00, while PM blocks are. During warm-up or load transitions, misaligned minutes compare different power levels. Bin both on wall-clock minutes and trim transitions.
Q: What checks prove a 1-hour lab log is usable?
A: About 60 minutes present; at least 45 samples in each minute; no read errors or large gaps; an idle segment before RF; the warm-up excluded; the clock offset to the RU known; and the model confirmed by *IDN? rather than assumed from a label.
Addition: soak-length power logging from a managed PDU — Telegraf → InfluxDB → Grafana
For long soaks, a time-series pipeline beats one-off readings: a Telegraf agent collects samples, InfluxDB stores them as time series, and Grafana draws dashboards. In the lab this was used to log per-outlet power from a managed PDU: a small poller script reads each outlet and reformats the values for Telegraf (an [[inputs.execd]] entry per socket in /etc/telegraf/telegraf.conf, with the socket number in its command line).
  • Managed PDUs cope badly with parallel SSH sessions: nominally a few are allowed, in practice one at a time is reliable — use one dedicated poller account and do not poll the same PDU from several tools.
  • Check the right socket is mapped to the equipment under test before a run, and stop the collector service when not in use.
  • PDU outlet power is AC wall power for the whole unit (PSU losses included); it complements, not replaces, the DC supply logging above for feature-level power savings.
  • Pattern to reuse: log RU or server power over a 24 h soak alongside traffic load, then compare energy per period between feature on and off.

Level 3 — reading the logs and avoiding false conclusions
Key chipset log packets when you go below the drive-test tool (QXDM-class, UE01): 0xB0C0 LTE RRC OTA, 0xB0EC/0xB0ED LTE NAS EMM OTA (incoming/outgoing), 0xB821 NR RRC OTA. Classic false conclusions.
• "Coverage hole" that is really a missing neighbour: the UE clings to a weak cell because the strong one is not in its list; the scanner shows the strong cell.
• "Network drop" that is a UE or tool fault: USB disconnect, tool crash, UE overheating or battery. Check device-status events and time gaps.
• "Low throughput" caused by the test server or a TCP window limit, not RF. Compare with UDP iPerf or a second server.
• A scanner vs UE difference of a few dB is normal (antenna placement, in-car loss, UE antenna gain). Compare trends, not absolute values, unless the setup is calibrated.
• A band/RAT lock left on: results look great or awful but do not represent customers.

Interview Q&A
Q: Why is the DU a “boundary clock” in this bench?
A: It is a PTP slave to the grandmaster on ens1f0 and a PTP master to the radios on the fronthaul ports; all four ports share one NIC hardware clock, so time recovered on one port is served on the others (LLS-C1).
Q: The radio will not transmit. What sync checks do you run?
A: DU: /var/run/ptpmanager/tx/status must show SYNC_LOCKED with timeSource ens1f0; radio: o-ran-sync sync-state LOCKED over M-plane. Not locked = no transmission.
Q: How does ruauto find the RU’s IP address?
A: The DU hands each radio a link-local 169.254.x.y address via DHCP on its fronthaul port, and ruauto discovers it by ARP on that interface.
Q: Name the setup files ruauto needs for one bench.
A: setup.ini pointing to env_setup.json (EXM), fdd_test_setup.json (DU/PHY/bands), rrh_setup.json (RU), tc_cadence.json, cable_loss.json and the KPI thresholds JSON.
Q: Why can’t mid band and B41 be tested at the same time on the ECS bench?
A: They share the same AAU-to-EXM connectors and cables through a hand-set RF switch, so only one band group is cabled at a time.

CABLE01 Bench Cable & Path Loss on the VNA — RG223 vs LMR-240-UltraFlex, splitter/attenuator chains, and why open splitter ports lie Lab click to expand ▾
In plain English
Every conducted RF test on the bench (TX power, ACLR, sensitivity, blocking) assumes you know the loss between the RU connector and the instrument. That loss is mostly coax jumpers, splitters and attenuators. This card is a real bench measurement on a VNA: two common jumper types (RG223 and LMR-240-UltraFlex) were measured at several lengths, then splitter and attenuator chains were measured both as single parts and as complete paths.

Three take-aways: (1) LMR-240-UltraFlex loses roughly 0.4 dB/m at 1.7–2.0 GHz and RG223 about 0.57 dB/m, so RG223 costs ~40–45 % more; (2) a loss budget built from individually measured parts matches the full chain to a few hundredths of a dB; (3) an unterminated splitter port wrecks the match and makes path loss unstable — always put a 50 Ω load on unused ports. See VNA2/VNA3 for VNA basics and SET1/SET2 for the bench these paths sit in.

Level 2 — how to measure it yourself
Setup used: E5080B VNA, 85032F Type-N 50 Ω cal-kit definitions, Smart Cal full 2-port with Unknown Thru (SOLR), sweep 600 MHz–9 GHz, 801 points, IF BW 1 kHz.
  1. Warm the VNA up for 30 minutes. Set span, points, IF BW and port power before calibrating — changing them afterwards interpolates or invalidates the cal.
  2. Select the cal kit (85032F), then Smart Cal → 2 Port, ports 1–2, Type N male on both ports.
  3. Open / Short / Load on each bare N(f) port in the order the wizard asks.
  4. For the thru, connect the cable under test with its adapters as an Unknown Thru (the VNA works out its delay; it only has to be reciprocal).
  5. Save as a user cal set. Confirm the status bar shows C 2-Port and Correction Properties lists 2-port 1-2 with an Unknown Thru delay.
  6. Show S11, S21, S12, S22. Put markers on the UL and DL frequencies of the band under test; read S21 (insertion loss) and S11/S22 (match). Repeat for every cable without touching the cal.

Level 3 — expert detail and pitfalls
  • Why LMR-240-UF has lower loss: conductor loss grows with √f (skin depth) and falls with conductor diameter, and foam PE has a lower loss tangent than solid PE, cutting the dielectric term that grows linearly with f. That is also why the gap widens toward 2.6 GHz.
  • Open splitter ports lie. With an unused 2:1 port left open, return loss collapsed to −8 to −10 dB; on the 4:1 chain to −4.5 dB. Insertion loss moved by up to 0.5 dB (frequency-dependent ripple) and changed again after reconnecting. A 0.5 dB error is enough to fail or falsely pass a TX-power or sensitivity limit. Terminate every unused port.
  • Receiver overflow: every screenshot at 0 dBm port power showed “Receiver A ADC overflow”. A low-loss thru can drive the VNA receiver into compression; re-measure at −10 dBm (narrow IF BW keeps the noise down) before trusting the last 0.05 dB.
  • Connector changes break the comparison: one 3 m cable had a different connector type at both ends, so its point carries extra mismatch — record connector types with every reading.
  • Budgets you can trust: because the parts added up to within ~0.05 dB of the whole chain, a per-part table is a valid way to build path loss for an automated bench. The values feed the framework’s per-antenna / per-frequency cable-loss file (stored as negative dB). Save one S2P file per cable so a new band can be read off without re-measuring.
  • Still open: straight vs bent flex test on both types, and low-band (740/795 MHz) values for every length.
Real case: for a customer multi-band bench, the 30 dB attenuator + 4:1 splitter + jumpers path measured 38.41 dB at 1860 MHz and 38.94 dB at 2585 MHz — the sum of the parts predicted it to within 0.06 dB.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why measure several cable lengths instead of one?
A: Each reading includes the adapters. Fitting loss vs length gives the cable dB/m as the slope and the adapter pair as the intercept, and exposes any outlier cable.
Q: What is an Unknown Thru calibration and why use it here?
A: A SOLR 2-port cal where the thru standard only needs to be reciprocal, not characterised. You can use a real cable with adapters as the thru when the two ports cannot mate directly.
Q: A 4:1 splitter measures 6.7 dB. Is it faulty?
A: No. The ideal split is 6.02 dB; 0.5–0.8 dB of excess loss from the internal couplers and lines is normal.
Q: What happens if you leave a splitter port open?
A: Power reflects back through the splitter, return loss falls toward −5 to −10 dB, and the through loss ripples by up to ~0.5 dB and is not repeatable. Always fit 50 Ω loads.
Q: Why is RG223 lossier than LMR-240-UltraFlex at 2 GHz?
A: Smaller diameter and solid PE dielectric (VF 0.66) give higher conductor and dielectric loss than foam PE (VF ~0.8); the dielectric part grows linearly with frequency so the gap widens.
SENSE02 RU Sensing on the FHGW side — rf-sensing CLI, tmpfs JSON contract, Welch PSD checks and how to validate an in-radio spectrum analyser Lab click to expand ▾
In plain English
SENSE01 describes the browser viewer that shows the RU's built-in spectrum analyser. This card is about the other half: the radio-side (FHGW) engine that actually captures IQ, computes the spectrum and publishes it. A green run of the GUI test suite only proves the client is right, because it runs against a demo stub. It says nothing about whether a given radio senses the right antenna, at the right frequency, at the right level.

The FHGW side has a small command (rf-sensing), a DMA capture engine, a Welch PSD, a set of JSON files on a RAM disk, and a gRPC server that streams frames to the GUI. Validating it means checking the maths (bin width, bin placement, FFT-size scaling), the RF paths (RX tap and the PA-observation tap), the shared resources (DMA, memory, cores, timing) and a list of real bugs that must never come back. The golden rule: never hard-code what the radio should report — read its own capability file and compare it with a committed reference.

Level 3 — pitfalls and real lessons
Absolute levels are never asserted. On the OBS path the RX ADC offset (indexed by antenna) is applied to an ORX capture, and the sensing scale has no RX-gain term. Both are known debts, so every amplitude test is a delta: carrier on vs off (peak must drop ≥ 10 dB), generator step, port vs port. On the reference B3 bench an 18 MHz ETM1 carrier was recovered 15–21 dB over the floor on ports 0–1.

Shared resources. Sensing shares the AXI DMA core and a 25 MB CMA pool with IQ record/playback, runs on cores 2–3 and must not disturb PTP/SyncE on cores 0–1. IQ record and sensing refuse each other (iq_path_busy); a running playback must survive a full sensing session. Stopping a session must return the ORX state machine to DPD-driven auto mode — a frozen DPD loop after an OBS session is a defect (see DPD02).

Interview Q&A
Q: Why does a green GUI suite not prove the RU sensing works?
A: It runs against a demo stub, so it validates the client only. Capture, PSD maths, RF routing and resource interlocks can only be proven on the FHGW with real hardware.
Q: You halve the FFT size. What happens to the displayed per-bin power of the noise floor?
A: It rises by about 3 dB, because each bin is twice as wide and collects twice the noise power. RBW = Fs / FFT size exactly.
Q: Why are OBS amplitude tests written as deltas?
A: The OBS scale applies the RX ADC offset to an ORX capture and has no gain term, so absolute dBm is uncalibrated. Carrier on/off, generator steps and port-to-port comparisons are still valid.
Q: A sensing run on OBS shows a perfectly flat, very low floor. What do you suspect?
A: An all-zero capture from the wrong observation receiver. Check the IQ is non-zero and that the routing register read-back matches the expected selector.
Q: What must happen when an OBS sensing session stops?
A: The ORX state machine must go back to auto mode under DPD control; otherwise DPD stays frozen on one channel.

In plain English
Real phones are great for final proof, but they are slow to script, hard to multiply and impossible to make misbehave on purpose. An Amarisoft UE simulator is a PC with SDR cards that pretends to be one or many UEs (4G and 5G NR, SA or NSA) over a real RF link, with full control of IMSI, keys, power on/off timing and traffic. Pair it with an Amarisoft core (EPC + 5GC + IMS in one service) and a content server that sources and sinks the data, and you have a complete, repeatable end-to-end rig: UE sim → RU → DU/CU → core → data network.

This card covers the parts you actually touch: the ue.cfg radio settings, a multi-UE scenario in the web UI, per-UE traffic, how the core hands out PDN addresses, and where the logs and captures are. For the Python library that drives Amarisoft in automation see FWK6 and AUTO06.

PDN vs APN/DNN: the PDN is the network behind the core (internet, IMS ...); the APN (4G) / DNN (5G) is just the name of the access point to it. The core creates one tun interface per PDN and NAT-masquerades traffic that leaves the server.

Level 3 — pitfalls and expert notes
  • K1 mismatch (rx_to_tx_latency vs DU K1) is the classic "UE attaches but DL throughput collapses" fault; check it first after any DU TDD/K1 change.
  • Attach storm limits: 1 attempt/s is gentle; raising it tests RACH/RRC capacity but mixes two variables if you only wanted throughput.
  • Dropouts at the end are expected: the scheduled power-off is a deregistration, not a drop — automation must not count it (see the verdict rules in AUTO06).
  • RF level: SDR gains are coarse and drift with temperature; use calibrated attenuators for any sensitivity- or coverage-type statement (AUTO11).
  • Captures fill disks: the ring buffer (10 x 100 MB) bounds it; a plain tcpdump left running does not.
  • Why bother with a full core? It implements abnormal cases and error handling, multi-UE, IMS and 4G EPC, so you can test reject causes, re-registration and VoNR set-up that a minimal core simulator cannot.
Interview Q&A
Q: What does rx_to_tx_latency in ue.cfg represent and why must it match the DU?
A: It is K1, the PDSCH-to-HARQ-ACK delay the simulated UE uses. If it differs from the DU, ACK/NACKs arrive in the wrong slot, the DU sees DTX and retransmits everything.
Q: A 10-UE, 600 s scenario has power-on 595 s. Why does it fail?
A: UEs start one per second, so UE 6 would still be on at 601 s, beyond the run duration. Power-on + start offset must fit inside the duration.
Q: Difference between a PDN and an APN/DNN?
A: The PDN is the data network itself; the APN/DNN is the name the UE uses to select the access point to it. The core maps each to a tun interface and address pool.
Q: Why give each IMSI a static IP?
A: So addresses never clash between scenarios or boxes and any IP in a capture maps back to exactly one UE.
Q: Why use -l 1410 in UDP iperf over the RAN?
A: To keep each datagram plus GTP-U/IP overhead below the transport MTU, avoiding fragmentation that would distort throughput and loss figures.

SFP01 Fronthaul Optics for the Bench — approved SFP+ types, MMF vs SMF, and modules that silently link at the wrong rate Lab click to expand ▾
In plain English
The fronthaul between a DU server and a radio is usually a fibre with a small pluggable optical transceiver (SFP+) at each end. The optic converts the electrical 10 Gb/s signal to light at a given wavelength, and the fibre must suit that wavelength. Getting this wrong rarely gives a clean "no link": more often the link comes up at the wrong speed or with marginal power, and the symptoms show up later as fronthaul errors, late packets or a cell that will not stay up.

On RCT and E2E benches the rule is: use only the product-approved optics, match fibre to wavelength, keep vendor-supplied optics paired with their radio, and verify the negotiated rate and optical power on both ends instead of trusting a link LED. Related: CM34 (fronthaul maths), CM31 (C/U-plane packets), OPS3 (bring-up), DUOPS01 (DU server ops).

Level 2 — how to check a fronthaul optic
Verify a link properly (on the DU server; on the RU use its own CLI/ethtool equivalent):
ethtool <if>                 # Speed: 10000Mb/s ? Link detected: yes ?
ethtool -m <if>              # module info + DDM: wavelength, Tx/Rx power (dBm), temperature, vendor
ethtool -S <if> | grep -iE "crc|err|drop"   # error counters - should not grow
ethtool -p <if> 10           # blink the port LED to find the physical port
Record for each end: module type and wavelength, negotiated speed, Tx and Rx optical power, temperature. Rx power near the receiver's low limit, or a large difference between the two directions, points at a dirty connector, a wrong fibre type or a damaged patch lead.
Level 3 — why it matters and habits that save hours
Why a wrong-rate link is worse than no link: fronthaul traffic for even one 20 MHz carrier needs far more than 1 Gb/s (see the fronthaul maths in CM34). A module that negotiates 1 G gives "link up" on both sides, management traffic may even work, but U-plane packets are dropped or arrive late, so you chase timing windows, PHY late-packet counters or RU alarms instead of the optic.

Pitfalls
  • Swapping an optic "temporarily" from another bench and forgetting it: label approved optics per setup.
  • Mixing MMF patch cords on an LR link (or the reverse): short lab runs can still link with marginal power and then fail when warm.
  • Trusting the DU side only: always check DDM on the RU side too.
  • Dirty connectors: inspect and clean before blaming the module.
Practical habit: add an "optics check" line to the bench bring-up log — module type both ends, speed 10G, Rx power — so that a later fronthaul problem can rule the physical layer in or out in seconds.

A: ethtool -m on the interface shows the module data and DDM values: Tx/Rx power, temperature, wavelength.
Q: Why use only approved optics on a test bench?
A: Unknown modules give results that depend on the optic, not the device under test, and compatibility varies per radio and DU platform.

A: ethtool -p blinks the port LED.

Level 3 — known behaviours that look like gNB bugs
  • PDU session only after a Service Request. The module's initial Registration Request carries the follow-on request bit = 0, so the network releases the signalling connection after registration and the PDN/PDU session only comes up with the next Service Request. A test that expects data right after registration sees an extra idle/connected cycle — that is UE behaviour, not a core or gNB fault.
  • UE Context Release during UL TCP. On sustained UL TCP the UL MCS degrades and the DU requests a UE Context Release. The module does not support multi-stream UL, so UL results with this UE say little about the cell's UL capacity. Reproduce with UDP before raising a DU bug.
  • Missing serial ports after a reboot or on a new host is a driver-binding issue, not a dead module — re-run the new_id step.
  • No traffic despite "connected": check that the default route really points at wwan0; a host with another default route (Ethernet, Wi-Fi) will send iperf the wrong way.

PROPSIM01 Keysight PROPSIM channel emulator in the RAN lab — fading, delay, Doppler, MIMO correlation, level budget and calibration Lab click to expand ▾
In plain English
A cable between a radio unit and a phone is a perfect, boring channel: one path, no motion, no multipath. Real air is nothing like that — the signal arrives as many delayed, attenuated copies, those copies change as the phone moves (Doppler), and the MIMO branches are partly correlated. A channel emulator such as the Keysight PROPSIM FS16 sits in the conducted RF path and plays a mathematically defined channel in real time: tapped-delay-line fading, extra delay, Doppler, antenna correlation, path loss / shadowing and calibrated AWGN.

Why bother? Drive tests are realistic but never repeatable. With an emulator, EVA70 today is EVA70 next month, so a regression after a software fix is a real regression, and you can sweep one variable (Doppler, SNR, delay spread, correlation) with everything else frozen. This card covers the box itself: what it does, the FS16 hardware, the emulation workflow and the level budget, the 3GPP models, and the pitfalls. Card PROPSIM02 covers end-to-end UE testing through it (attach vs RSRP, timing advance, UL link adaptation). For the propagation theory behind the models see A08 and PROP02; for where fading tests sit in conformance see UPERF01.

Typical RCT use. TS 36.141 / TS 38.141-1 clause 8 performance tests (PUSCH, PUCCH, PRACH — EPA/EVA/ETU for LTE, TDL-A/B/C for NR) are defined under fading with specified correlation, so they need an emulator in the UL path: the test signal source feeds the emulator, the emulator adds the TDL/EPA/EVA/ETU channel plus AWGN at the specified SNR, and the RU receives it. A cabled AWGN bench covers sensitivity and smoke tests but cannot prove clause-8 numbers (see UPERF01, AAUTS01, A08). Software fading inside a UE simulator (e.g. a built-in channel simulator) is fine for scheduler logic, but only the hardware emulator puts the real transmitter, RF front end, AGC and receiver in the loop with calibrated absolute levels.

Insertion delay and TA. About 2.6 µs each way ≈ 5.2 µs round trip, so the cell reports a non-zero TA even with a “0 m” channel — normal. Extended-delay options (hundreds of ms, for NTN) push PRACH outside the reception window and the UE will never attach unless the stack supports NTN TA handling (NS36).

Interview Q&A
Q: Why use a hardware channel emulator instead of a drive test?
A: Repeatability. The emulator plays the same statistically defined channel every time, so a before/after comparison isolates the software change, and you can sweep one variable (Doppler, SNR, correlation, delay spread) with the rest frozen.
Q: What do the letters and the number in “ETU300” mean, and why is it the hardest LTE case?
A: ETU = Extended Typical Urban delay profile: 9 taps, 991 ns RMS delay spread, 5 µs span. 300 = maximum Doppler in Hz (about 162 km/h at 2 GHz, coherence time ~1.4 ms). Largest delay spread plus fastest fading stresses channel estimation, CQI ageing and HARQ.
Q: The DUT sees a level 8 dB lower than expected. Where do you look?
A: Walk the level chain from the start: real Max TX, In loss (missing pad or long cable?), crest factor, path loss (one direction only changed?), channel gain trim, Out loss, then compare Output level with what the DUT reports. Also check whether the output-gain-limit dialog reduced levels, and recalibrate if cabling changed.
Q: What is the maximum input to an FS16 port, and how do you protect it from a 46 dBm RU?
A: +35 dBm peak (+15 dBm below 100 MHz). Insert enough attenuation that average power plus crest factor stays below that, e.g. 40 dB of pads gives +6 dBm average and about +18 dBm peak for a 12 dB crest factor.
Q: How does antenna correlation show up in KPIs?
A: Low correlation (α=β=0) lets the UE report RI=2 and reach full MIMO throughput; high correlation (0.9/0.9) should drive RI to 1 and roughly halve 2×2 peak throughput. If rank stays at 2 under high correlation, BLER spikes — a rank-adaptation bug.

1. How to sweep RSRP through the emulator. A calibrated emulation file carries different gain trims per direction (in the example, DL channel gain adj. −36 dB and UL +5 dB, both landing on the same total). Writing one absolute gain to all channels (as some older scripts did) silently breaks it. The in-house regression suite does the same thing: read gain per channel, step it down, and replay the steps in reverse for the return leg.
# pattern only - command names depend on firmware / wrapper
base = {ch: emu.getgain(ch) for ch in channels}          # e.g. DL -36, UL +5
rsrp0 = median(ue_rsrp_samples())                        # baseline, e.g. -98 dBm
for delta in coarse_then_fine(step1=5, step2=2):         # stop coarse at first 0% step
    for ch in channels: emu.setgain(ch, base[ch] + delta)   # same delta everywhere
    nominal = rsrp0 + delta                              # x-axis
    for n in range(cycles): run_attach_cycle(log=nominal)

Interview Q&A
Q: How do you sweep RSRP through a PROPSIM without breaking its calibration?
A: Read each channel’s gain from the calibrated emulation and add the same delta to all of them, so DL and UL keep their individual trims and path loss stays symmetric. Nominal RSRP = baseline UE RSRP + delta; check the scale with one known step.
Q: Your attach cliff sits 10 dB lower on the emulator bench than on the attenuator bench. What happened?
A: Probably leakage or low-level non-linearity: below some level the UE RSRP stops following the gain, so the nominal axis is optimistic. Compare the benches on UE-reported SNR (they agreed there, ~−4 dB for 50%) and do a leakage check with the link muted.
Q: You set a 2 µs tap delay and TA does not change. Why?
A: The emulator already has about 2.6 µs of insertion delay; a tap delay at or below it adds nothing. TA started moving only above ~2.6 µs.
Q: In the TA test, TA rose ~7.7 per µs of delay. Is that right?
A: The delay applies in both directions, so slope = 2/u with u the TA unit. 7.7 per µs matches u = 0.26 µs (NR, 30 kHz SCS); LTE’s 0.52 µs unit would give ~3.8. Verify the unit of the tool reporting TA before converting to distance.
Q: What do you log for an UL link-adaptation run through fading, and why report HARQ DTX separately?
A: Measured RSRP and SINR as the axis, UL MCS, PRB, BLER, PHR, CQI/RI, throughput per second, RTT, and HARQ ACK/NACK/DTX from the MAC per-TTI trace, both legs of the traverse separately. DTX means the UE did not transmit on the grant (missed PDCCH or power-limited), which needs a different fix from a NACK.

SIMBURN01 Programming Test SIMs In-House — nibble-swapped IMSI (EF_IMSI) and Access Control Class (EF_ACC) with APDU scripts Lab click to expand ▾
In plain English
A lab with real phones needs its own SIM cards, so that the phones attach to the lab core and not to a public network. Blank programmable test SIMs are cheap. "Burning" one means writing a few files on the card with a card reader and a short script of APDU commands. The two files that matter most are EF_IMSI (the subscriber identity, stored in an odd nibble-swapped format) and EF_ACC (the access class, which decides whether the phone is barred when the cell enables access-class barring). The secret key K and OPc are set when the card is made and must match the lab core's subscriber database (HSS/HLR). This card explains the method only. Every IMSI shown is a made-up value in the 001-01 test PLMN, and no real keys are given.
How an IMSI is laid out in EF_IMSI
The example IMSI is fictitious. Use the same method with the IMSI your lab core expects.
Level 1 — what is written, and how it is encoded

Tools: a PC/SC smart-card reader/programmer (the green LED shows a card is seated), a script-runner tool (Java 17) that runs .ldr APDU scripts, and a SIM-explorer tool to reset the card and read files back.

EF_IMSI encoding (3GPP TS 31.102 / 51.011):

  • Byte 1 is the length of the data that follows: 08.
  • Byte 2: the high nibble is the first IMSI digit. The low nibble is 9, meaning an odd number of digits and identity type IMSI.
  • Bytes 3–9: the remaining 14 digits in pairs, each pair nibble-swapped. Digits "a b" are stored as the byte ba.
StepExample (fictitious IMSI 001010000012345)
Split after the first digit0 | 01 01 00 00 01 23 45
Swap each pair09 | 10 10 00 00 10 32 54
Prefix length byte08 09 10 10 00 00 10 32 54

EF_ACC is 2 bytes with one bit per access class. Normal subscribers get one class from 0–9, by convention taken from the last digit of the IMSI: 0 gives 0001, 1 gives 0002, 2 gives 0004 … 8 gives 0100, 9 gives 0200. Bits for classes 11–15 (special classes such as PLMN staff and emergency services) sit higher up and are normally left at 0 on test SIMs.

Level 2 — burning and verifying a SIM

The two script files hold one command each, followed by a read-back. Edit the data bytes, then run them:

; UpdateIMSI script
CMD A0D6000009 080910100000103254      ; UPDATE BINARY, 9 bytes to EF_IMSI
    (9000)
CMD A0B0000009 [080910100000103254]    ; READ BINARY, 9 bytes, expect what was written
    (9000)

; UpdateACC script
CMD A0D6000002 0020                    ; UPDATE BINARY, 2 bytes to EF_ACC
    (9000)
CMD A0B0000002 [0020]
    (9000)

Class byte A0 is the GSM SIM command class. D6 is UPDATE BINARY, B0 is READ BINARY, P1/P2 = 0000 is offset 0, and the last byte is the length. Status word 9000 means success. The scripts select the right EF before these lines.

Procedure:

  1. Seat the SIM in the programmer and connect it to the laptop.
  2. Convert the IMSI to nibble-swapped form and paste it into the IMSI script. Set the ACC from the last digit.
  3. In the script runner, load both scripts. Run the IMSI script first, then the ACC script. Every command must return 9000.
  4. In the SIM explorer, choose the reader, reset the card, then use Read IMSI. The result is shown in nibble-swapped form, so swap it back to check.
  5. Add the subscriber to the lab core with the same IMSI and the card's K/OPc, then put the SIM in a phone and attach.

Core side (lab EPC CLI, values are placeholders): the subscriber entry sets the AMBR and, per APN, the default QCI. A data-only entry has epsinfo1=1,internet,,9 (QCI 9 internet APN). For voice tests, add epsinfo2=2,ims,,5, an IMS APN with QCI 5 signalling, so the phone can register to IMS and set up QCI-1 bearers. See AMARI01 for the Amarisoft core equivalent.

Level 3 — pitfalls
  • Most common mistake: no swap, or a wrong swap. Writing the IMSI as plain digits gives a different IMSI. The phone then attaches with an identity the HSS does not know, and the core rejects it with "unknown subscriber" or an authentication failure. Always read the IMSI back and un-swap it by hand.
  • Even-length IMSIs (14 digits) use 1 as the low nibble of byte 2 and pad with F. Test IMSIs are normally 15 digits.
  • ACC matters for barring tests. If you test access-class barring (SIB2 / SIB1 UAC), spread your phones over different classes on purpose, by choosing IMSIs with different last digits. Otherwise every phone is barred or allowed together.
  • Card reset is required after writing: the phone and the explorer may cache the old file content.
  • K and OPc are not touched by these scripts. If authentication fails even though the IMSI is right, the card's keys do not match the core entry. Re-provision the core with the card vendor's values; never reuse or publish real keys.
  • Other files (EF_AD MNC length, EF_HPLMN, EF_FPLMN) can make a phone reject or ignore the lab network. If a newly burned SIM shows "no service", check the MNC length in EF_AD (2 for 001-01).
Interview Q&A
Q: How is a 15-digit IMSI stored in EF_IMSI?
A: Length byte 08, then the first digit in the high nibble with 9 in the low nibble (odd, IMSI type), then the remaining 14 digits in pairs, each pair nibble-swapped.
Q: What APDUs write and verify EF_IMSI?
A: A0 D6 00 00 09 + 9 data bytes (UPDATE BINARY), then A0 B0 00 00 09 (READ BINARY). Both must return SW 9000.
Q: What does EF_ACC contain and how is the class chosen on test SIMs?
A: A 2-byte bitmap, one bit per access class. One class 0–9 is set, by convention the IMSI's last digit, so digit 5 gives 0020.
Q: A freshly burned SIM is rejected by the core with an authentication failure. What do you check?
A: Read the IMSI back and un-swap it; then check that the HSS entry's K/OPc match the card. The burn scripts do not change keys.
Q: What core settings are needed for QCI-1 voice tests?
A: A second APN for IMS (epsinfo2 with QCI 5 for signalling) in addition to the QCI-9 internet APN, so the phone registers to IMS and gets dedicated QCI-1 bearers.

Addition — RU command, gRPC contract and test coverage
RU side in one line: the server is fed by the FHGW command rf-sensing --antenna 0-3 [--band] [--obs-window ms] [--fft-size 2048|4096|16384] [--source rx|obs], which runs a PS-side DMA IQ capture, computes a Welch PSD and writes JSON. On TDD it senses the UL slots of the configured pattern; on FDD it takes the leading slots. Sub-commands: off, status, capability, iq-debug on|off, iq-stream --center <Hz> --bw <Hz> (channelised I/Q tap). It refuses with "IQ path busy" while ul-rct-test or an IQ recording is running. Full detail and validation in SENSE02.

Contract: the gRPC interface IntegSenseV1 has six RPCs (StreamPsd, GetStatus, SetAntenna, Start, Stop, SetIqDebug); the port is fixed at 50051, and changes are backward-compatible (new fields get new tags; a V2 is added alongside). The browser bridge is a FastAPI app; inside IntegUI it is served same-origin through an nginx path proxy, and because every URL in the page is relative the same bundle works both embedded and standalone on a NUC.

Test suite: a hardware-free pytest suite runs the GUI + bridge against a demo stub (headless Playwright for browser behaviour): 96 automated cases, 81 pass and 15 skipped by design (hardware/soak-gated), out of 151 cases in the plan. A green run validates the tool, not a specific radio — the radio half is SENSE02.
