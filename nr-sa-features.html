5G NR Standalone — access, mobility, beamforming, scheduling, QoS, voice & O1 · plain-English + how-it-works + interview Q&A
Public-knowledge write-ups of 5G NR Standalone features (native 5GC). Concepts only, no internal specifics. Each card feeds the Interview simulator.
NS01 Cell Startup & Bring-up 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Everything that has to happen for an NR cell to go on air and start serving UEs.
How it works
The DU/CU configure PHY (SSB, numerology, TDD pattern), bring up the fronthaul (7.2x), start broadcasting SSB and SIB1, and open F1 (CU↔DU) and NG (to the 5GC). Once sync, power and cell config are validated the cell is operational.
Interview Q&A
Q: What must come up before an NR cell can serve a UE?
A: Timing/sync and fronthaul, then PHY (SSB burst, numerology, TDD pattern), the F1 setup between CU and DU, and NG setup to the AMF. The UE then finds the cell via SSB, reads MIB/SIB1, and can start random access.
NS02 SIB Management (NR system information) 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
How NR broadcasts the system information a UE needs to camp, access and reselect — restructured from LTE.
How it works
MIB (on PBCH) points to SIB1 (cell access + scheduling of other SIBs). In NR, SIB2 carries cell-reselection common info, SIB3 intra-frequency and SIB4 inter-frequency neighbours — a different numbering from LTE (where reselection is SIB3/4/5). Some SIBs are broadcast, others sent on-demand.
Interview Q&A
Q: Where are the reselection parameters in NR vs LTE?
A: NR: SIB2 (reselection common), SIB3 (intra-frequency), SIB4 (inter-frequency). LTE: SIB3 (serving/intra), SIB4 (intra neighbours), SIB5 (inter-frequency). Mixing the two numbering schemes is a classic interview trap.
NS03 Neighbour Relation Table (NRT) Design 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
The map that lets a cell translate "the UE reported PCI X" into "hand it to that specific gNB/cell over this interface".
How it works
The NRT holds each neighbour's PCI → CGI, the transport/interface (Xn) to reach it, and per-relation flags (no-HO, no-remove, no-X2/Xn). Measurement reports give a PCI; the NRT resolves it to a target cell and the Xn peer to send the Handover Request to.
Interview Q&A
Q: How does a cell turn a reported PCI into a handover target?
A: Via the Neighbour Relation Table: the PCI from the measurement report is looked up to its CGI and the Xn/X2 peer that serves it. PCIs are reused across the network, so the NRT (populated manually or by ANR reading the target's CGI) disambiguates and gives the transport to send the HO request.
NS04 ANR — Automatic Neighbour Relations 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Let the network discover and maintain neighbour relations automatically instead of hand-planning every one.
How it works
When a UE reports an unknown PCI, the network asks it to read that cell's CGI (global ID); the new relation is added to the NRT, and stale ones are pruned — reducing dropped handovers from missing neighbours.
Interview Q&A
Q: What problem does ANR solve?
A: Manual neighbour planning is error-prone and misses relations, causing too-late/wrong-cell handovers. ANR uses UE CGI reporting to auto-add neighbours it discovers and remove unused ones, keeping the NRT correct as the network changes.
NS05 5G Handover (Xn / N2) 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Connected-mode mobility in standalone 5G — over the direct gNB link where possible, else through the core.
How it works
Xn handover goes gNB-to-gNB with direct data forwarding (fast). N2 (NG) handover routes via the AMF when there's no Xn or when AMF/UPF relocation is needed. The target prepares resources and returns an RRCReconfiguration with the new cell config and RACH resources.
Interview Q&A
Q: Xn vs N2 handover in 5G?
A: Xn is the direct gNB-to-gNB path with direct forwarding, analogous to LTE X2 — low latency. N2/NG handover is via the AMF (with possible UPF relocation), used when there is no Xn or across AMF boundaries — analogous to LTE S1 handover.
NS06 Xn Interface 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
The direct interface between two gNBs — the 5G equivalent of LTE's X2.
How it works
Xn carries Xn-C (control: handover prep, context transfer, status) and Xn-U (user: data forwarding during mobility). Xn Setup exchanges each gNB's served-cell info (PCI, NCGI, TAC, frequencies) so neighbours know how to reach each other.
Interview Q&A
Q: How is Xn different from X2?
A: Functionally similar (direct RAN-node link for mobility and data forwarding) but Xn connects gNBs in 5GS and carries 5G constructs — NG-RAN node IDs, NR cell identities, PDU-session/QoS-flow info — whereas X2 connects eNBs in EPS with E-RAB/bearer semantics.
NS07 PRACH & Multi-RAR Configuration 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
How a UE first grabs the network's attention (random access) and how the network answers many UEs at once.
How it works
The UE picks a preamble and sends it on PRACH; the gNB replies with a RAR (Msg2) granting a UL resource + timing advance; then Msg3 (RRC request) and Msg4 (contention resolution). Multi-RAR lets one response window carry several UEs' RARs efficiently under load.
Interview Q&A
Q: Walk the 4-step RACH procedure.
A: Msg1: UE sends a random-access preamble on PRACH. Msg2: gNB sends the Random Access Response with a timing-advance and UL grant. Msg3: UE sends its RRC connection/setup request on the granted resource. Msg4: gNB sends contention resolution confirming the UE. (2-step RACH collapses Msg1+3 and Msg2+4.)
NS08 PUCCH & PUSCH Formats 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
The uplink channels: PUSCH carries data, PUCCH carries control (ACK/NACK, CQI, scheduling requests) in several sized formats.
How it works
PUCCH F0/F2 are short (1–2 symbols) for a few UCI bits; F1/F3/F4 are long (up to 14 symbols) for more bits or coverage. The gNB picks the format by payload size and coverage need; PUSCH carries the actual data (and can piggyback UCI).
Interview Q&A
Q: Why are there multiple PUCCH formats?
A: They trade payload vs coverage vs latency. Short formats (F0/F2) give low-latency feedback for small UCI; long formats (F1/F3/F4) spread over more symbols for more bits or better cell-edge coverage. The scheduler chooses per the UCI size and the UE's link budget.
NS09 DCI Formats & Size Alignment 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Downlink Control Information is the scheduling grant on PDCCH; NR aligns DCI sizes so UEs can blind-decode them efficiently.
How it works
DCI 0_0/0_1 schedule UL (PUSCH), 1_0/1_1 schedule DL (PDSCH); 0_1/1_1 are the richer non-fallback formats. NR limits the number of distinct DCI sizes a UE must monitor (size alignment) to bound blind-decoding attempts.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is DCI and why does size alignment matter?
A: DCI is the PDCCH scheduling grant telling the UE where/how its data is (RB allocation, MCS, HARQ, etc.). The UE blind-decodes candidates, so 3GPP caps the number of different DCI sizes it must try — size alignment pads/aligns formats to stay within that budget and keep decoding feasible.
NS10 MU-MIMO Scheduling (UL/DL) & UE Grouping 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Serve multiple UEs on the same time/frequency by separating them in space with beamforming — multiplying cell capacity.
How it works
The scheduler groups UEs whose channels are spatially separable (low cross-correlation), builds precoders/beams per user, and transmits them simultaneously. Good grouping and accurate CSI keep inter-user interference low; bad pairing wastes the gain.
Interview Q&A
Q: MU-MIMO vs SU-MIMO?
A: SU-MIMO sends multiple layers to one UE. MU-MIMO sends layers to different UEs on the same resources, separated spatially by beamforming. MU multiplies capacity when UEs are spatially separable and CSI is good, but needs careful user grouping to limit inter-user interference.
NS11 Massive MIMO — Beamforming + DL Link Adaptation 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Use a large antenna array to form narrow, steerable beams toward each UE for coverage and capacity — the headline 5G feature on mid-band.
How it works
The array forms per-UE beams from CSI (SRS/CSI-RS); DLLA then adapts MCS/rank to the beamformed channel. Beamforming gain extends range and, with MU-MIMO, packs many users per cell.
Interview Q&A
Q: How does massive MIMO improve both coverage and capacity?
A: Coherently combining many antennas forms a high-gain, narrow beam steered at the UE — that array gain extends coverage. Because beams are spatially separated, the cell can serve many UEs simultaneously (MU-MIMO), multiplying capacity. Both depend on accurate CSI from SRS/CSI-RS.
NS12 SSB Beam & Precoder Cycling 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
The gNB sweeps its synchronization beams across the sector so UEs anywhere can find the cell, then refines to a best beam.
How it works
Multiple SSBs are sent in a burst, each on a different beam; the UE measures them and reports the best SSB index. Precoder/beam cycling during broadcast avoids leaving coverage holes between narrow beams.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why does NR sweep SSB beams?
A: On mid/high band the gNB uses narrow beams, so a single fixed broadcast beam would leave gaps. Sweeping several SSB beams across the sector lets a UE at any angle detect the cell, measure per-beam quality, and report the best SSB — the basis for initial access and beam management.
NS13 CSI-RS Framework & Measurements 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Reference signals the gNB sends so the UE can measure the downlink channel and report how to transmit to it.
How it works
From CSI-RS the UE derives CQI/PMI/RI/LI and beam (CRI) reports; the gNB uses them for link adaptation, precoding and beam selection. CSI-RS can also be used for mobility/beam measurements and tracking (TRS).
Interview Q&A
Q: What is CSI-RS used for?
A: It lets the UE measure the DL channel and feed back CSI — CQI (quality → MCS), PMI (precoder), RI (rank), and beam indicators. The gNB uses that to choose modulation, precoding and beams. It also supports beam management and fine time/frequency tracking.
NS14 Sounding (SRS) & DL CSI Acquisition 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
The uplink counterpart: the UE transmits a known signal so the gNB can measure the uplink channel — vital for TDD beamforming by reciprocity.
How it works
The UE sends SRS; in TDD the gNB uses channel reciprocity to estimate the DL channel from the UL sounding and compute DL precoders/beams — often more accurate than PMI feedback for massive MIMO.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why is SRS so important in TDD massive MIMO?
A: TDD uses the same frequency up and down, so by reciprocity the gNB can infer the DL channel from the UL SRS it measures directly — giving full-dimension CSI for beamforming without relying on limited PMI feedback. Good SRS coverage/scheduling is therefore key to MU-MIMO gains.
NS15 DL/UL Link Adaptation 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Continuously match the modulation and coding to the current channel so you send as fast as the link can reliably carry.
How it works
From CQI/HARQ feedback the gNB picks the MCS and rank, targeting a BLER operating point (~10% first-transmission). Good channel → higher-order modulation (up to 256/1024QAM); poor channel → robust low MCS.
Interview Q&A
Q: How does link adaptation choose MCS?
A: It maps the reported CQI (and adjusts with an outer loop from HARQ ACK/NACK) to an MCS that hits a target first-transmission BLER (~10%). If retransmissions rise, the outer loop backs off MCS; if the channel improves, it climbs — maximizing throughput at controlled error.
NS16 256QAM UL (higher-order modulation) 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Pack more bits per symbol on the uplink when the channel is clean, raising UL peak throughput.
How it works
With high SINR the UE can use 256QAM (8 bits/symbol) instead of 64QAM; it needs low EVM in the UE PA and good link quality, and the gNB must signal support and pick the higher MCS table.
Interview Q&A
Q: What conditions are needed to use 256QAM on the uplink?
A: High SINR and a clean, linear UE transmit path (low EVM), because dense constellations are error-sensitive. Both UE and gNB must support the 256QAM MCS table, and link adaptation only selects it near-cell where the error target can still be met.
NS17 Connected-Mode DRX (NR) 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Battery saving for connected 5G UEs — sleep between scheduling like LTE DRX but with NR numerology.
How it works
On-duration + inactivity timer + short/long DRX cycles govern when the UE monitors PDCCH; tuned per service to trade latency vs power (tighter for URLLC, relaxed for background).
Interview Q&A
Q: How would you tune DRX differently for URLLC vs eMBB background traffic?
A: URLLC needs short (or effectively no) DRX cycles and a long inactivity timer so latency stays low, at a power cost. Background/eMBB traffic can use long DRX cycles and short on-durations to maximise sleep, accepting higher scheduling latency.
NS18 Paging in RRC_IDLE 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
How the network reaches an idle UE when there's incoming data or a call.
How it works
The AMF pages the UE across its registration area; each cell sends a paging message at the UE's paging occasion (derived from its ID and DRX). The UE, monitoring only its occasions, wakes and responds with a service request.
Interview Q&A
Q: How does an idle UE receive a page without draining its battery?
A: It only wakes at its own paging occasions, computed from its identity and the paging DRX cycle, and monitors PDCCH for the paging RNTI then. The AMF pages the registration area; on a match the UE runs a service request to move to connected.
NS19 QoS Scheduler & Multi-DRB 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
5G maps traffic to QoS flows and radio bearers so different services get the treatment they need on one connection.
How it works
The SMF assigns each flow a 5QI; SDAP maps QoS flows → DRBs. The scheduler enforces per-DRB priority/GBR. Multi-DRB lets one PDU session carry, say, a GBR voice DRB and a best-effort data DRB simultaneously.
Interview Q&A
Q: How does 5G QoS differ from LTE bearers?
A: LTE is bearer-centric (EPS bearer = QCI). 5G is flow-centric: the SMF marks packets with a 5QI per QoS flow, and SDAP maps flows onto DRBs. This is finer-grained — many flows can share or split across DRBs within one PDU session, with the scheduler honouring each 5QI.
NS20 EPS Fallback (voice) 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
When VoNR isn't available, a 5G voice call is moved to LTE so the call still connects.
How it works
On an IMS voice setup in SA, if the gNB/network can't serve VoNR it triggers EPS fallback: the UE is handed/redirected to LTE, where the voice runs as VoLTE (QCI 1). It's the common early-5G voice solution.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is EPS fallback and when is it used?
A: When a UE in 5G SA tries an IMS voice call but the cell/network doesn't support VoNR (or QoS can't be met), the network moves the UE to LTE (handover or redirection) and the call proceeds as VoLTE. It's the interim voice solution until VoNR is deployed.
NS21 Voice over NR (VoNR) 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Native 5G voice — the call runs directly on NR without falling back to LTE.
How it works
Voice is IMS VoIP over a 5G QoS flow (5QI 1) on NR. It needs solid NR coverage and QoS support end-to-end; where that's missing the network uses EPS fallback instead.
Interview Q&A
Q: VoNR vs EPS fallback?
A: VoNR keeps the voice call natively on 5G NR (IMS over 5QI 1), giving lower latency and no interruption — but it requires mature NR coverage and QoS. EPS fallback punts the call to LTE/VoLTE when VoNR can't be guaranteed. Operators start with fallback and migrate to VoNR as coverage matures.
NS22 PHY Timing-Advance Manager 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Keep every UE's uplink arriving at the gNB aligned in time despite different distances.
How it works
The gNB measures each UE's UL arrival and sends Timing Advance commands so bursts land inside the cyclic prefix; a TA timer forces re-sync if the UE goes quiet. Larger cells need a wider TA range.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why is timing advance needed and what bounds cell size?
A: UEs are at different distances, so without TA their uplinks would arrive misaligned and smear across symbols. The gNB commands each UE's TA so signals land within the cyclic prefix. The maximum TA range (and CP length) bounds the achievable cell radius.
NS23 NETCONF / YANG for CU & DU (O1) 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
The standard way to configure and monitor the gNB's CU and DU — the management (O1) interface.
How it works
Management uses NETCONF over SSH with YANG data models to push config and stream telemetry/alarms to the SMO/EMS. It's the O-RAN O1 interface for FCAPS across CU/DU.
Interview Q&A
Q: What are NETCONF and YANG, and which O-RAN interface use them?
A: YANG is the data-model language describing a node's config/state; NETCONF is the protocol (over SSH) to get/edit that config and receive notifications. In O-RAN they realise the O1 interface between the SMO/EMS and the CU/DU (and the M-plane to the O-RU) for FCAPS.
NS24 NG-Flex — AMF Load Balancing 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Spread signalling load across a pool of core AMFs and survive one failing — the 5G version of LTE S1-Flex.
How it works
A gNB connects to several AMFs (an AMF set/pool) over NG; it selects one per UE by weight/GUAMI and can re-home UEs if an AMF fails, giving load balancing and redundancy.
Interview Q&A
Q: What does NG-Flex (AMF pooling) give you?
A: A many-to-many gNB↔AMF connection so UE signalling is distributed across an AMF pool by weight, and if one AMF fails its UEs can be served by others. It's the 5G analogue of LTE S1-Flex (MME pooling) — load balancing plus geo-redundancy.
NS25 Enhanced Cell-ID (E-CID) Positioning 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Locate a UE using cell and radio measurements the network already has — no extra positioning reference signals needed.
How it works
E-CID combines the serving cell identity with measurements like timing advance and neighbour RSRP/RSRQ/AoA to estimate location — coarser than OTDOA/multi-RTT but cheap and always available.
Interview Q&A
Q: How accurate is E-CID and when would you use it?
A: It's coarse (tens to hundreds of metres) because it leans on cell ID, TA and signal-strength/angle measurements rather than dedicated positioning signals. You use it as a low-cost, always-available baseline, escalating to OTDOA/multi-RTT/PRS when you need higher accuracy.

Interview Q&A
Q: Walk the SA registration + PDU session at a high level.
A: After RACH the UE does RRC setup, then sends a Registration Request (NAS) carried in the gNB's Initial UE Message to the AMF. The AMF authenticates via AUSF/UDM (5G-AKA), runs NAS security (SMC), sends Registration Accept inside Initial Context Setup, and the UE confirms with Registration Complete. Then the UE requests a PDU session; the AMF selects an SMF (N11) which programs the UPF (N4); on accept, user data flows UE↔gNB↔UPF (N3) to the data network (N6).

HO01 Handover — Intra-DU (F1AP internal, no core involvement) Handover click to expand +
▼ Visual sequence diagram
Solid→request · dashed→response/ack · amber→internal action. Step-by-step detail below.

Source & target cell both under the same gNB-DU (same CU) · fastest HO type · no Xn, no core path switch

Why it exists
When a UE moves between two cells served by the same DU, there's no need to involve a new DU, Xn, or the core. The CU decides the HO from measurement reports and just reconfigures the UE onto the target cell; the DU allocates target-cell L1/L2 resources internally. Same CU-UP, same F1-U/N3 tunnels → no path switch, so the interruption is minimal. On a Metro AAU carrying several cells per sector, this is the common everyday mobility path.

             target cell cfg, new C-RNTI, RACH resources)
CU   → UE :  RRCReconfiguration (reconfigurationWithSync → target)
UE   → tgt:  RACH  (preamble → RAR/Msg2)
UE   → CU :  RRCReconfigurationComplete  (on target cell)
CU   → DU :  F1AP UE Context Modification (release source cell)

Data stall after HO → PDCP not re-established / SN discontinuity.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why is intra-DU HO the fastest? No Xn/S1/NG signaling and no UPF path switch — the CU just reconfigures the UE and the DU allocates target resources internally, so only the RACH-to-target interruption applies.
Q: What single IE turns an RRCReconfiguration into a handover? reconfigurationWithSync (contains the target SpCell config + RACH); without it, it's an in-place reconfig with no cell change.
Q (PW): On a Metro AAU with multiple cells per sector, why do most HOs stay intra-DU? Because the whole sector's cells terminate on one DU, so mobility within the site never needs Xn or core path switch — keeping KPIs (interruption time, drop rate) low.

Source & target cell on different DUs but the same CU · CU orchestrates both DUs over F1 · no Xn, no core path switch

Why it exists
A UE moving between cells on two DUs of the same gNB (same CU). The CU stays anchored — it sets up a fresh UE context on the target DU (F1AP UE Context Setup), moves the UE, then releases the source DU's context. Because the CU-UP and its N3 tunnels don't move, there's still no core path switch; only the F1-U tunnel between CU-UP and the target DU is re-pointed.

Interview Q&A
Q: What's the key F1AP difference vs intra-DU HO? Inter-DU uses UE Context Setup on the target DU (new context) rather than UE Context Modification on the same DU.
Q: Does the core know about this HO? No — CU-UP is unchanged, so no NGAP Path Switch; only the internal F1-U tunnel to the target DU is re-pointed.
Q (PW): Why can two Metro sites still avoid Xn here? If both sectors' DUs share one CU (common in a centralized deployment), the CU handles it over F1 — Xn only appears when the target is a different gNB/CU.
HO03 Handover — Inter-CU / Xn-based (full XnAP flow) Handover click to expand +
▼ Visual sequence diagram
Solid→request · dashed→response/ack · amber→internal action. Step-by-step detail below.

Target is a different gNB/CU, and an Xn interface exists between source and target · core involved only for the path switch

Why it exists
When two gNBs have a direct Xn link, HO is done gNB-to-gNB without routing prep through the core (faster, less core load). The source CU asks the target CU to admit the UE, forwards buffered data over Xn during the switch, and only afterwards does the target tell the AMF to switch the UPF's downlink path. The 4G equivalent is X2-based HO.

Interview Q&A
Q: When is Xn HO chosen over N2 HO? When a direct Xn exists between the gNBs — it's faster and offloads the core; N2 is the fallback when there's no Xn.
Q: How is data loss avoided? SN Status Transfer + Xn-U data forwarding, with the UPF End Marker telling the target when the old path is drained.
Q (PW): Between two Metro gNBs on adjacent sites, which HO runs? If Xn is provisioned, Xn-based; the only core touch is the NGAP Path Switch at the end.
Deep Reference Notes
X2 (LTE) ↔ Xn (5G) message parity: HandoverRequest / HandoverRequestAck, SN Status Transfer (PDCP SN counts for lossless HO), and UE Context Release exist on both. Trigger types: normal HO is measurement-triggered (A3 event); a failed link triggers RLF → RRC Re-establishment instead (which needs a prepared context at the target, or it is rejected). Phases: Preparation (admit + reserve) → Execution (RRCReconfig + RACH) → Completion (Path Switch + release). Data forwarding: direct (over X2/Xn-U) or indirect (via S-GW/UPF). Rel-16 DAPS (Dual Active Protocol Stack) keeps the source link alive during execution for near-0 ms interruption.

HO06 Handover — Conditional (CHO, UE-autonomous, 5G NR Rel-16) Handover click to expand +
▼ Visual sequence diagram
Solid→request · dashed→response/ack · amber→internal action. Step-by-step detail below.

Network pre-configures candidate targets; the UE decides when to execute — no waiting for a network command at the worst moment

Why it exists
In a normal HO the network sends the command after the measurement report — but by then the serving radio may already have collapsed, so the command itself is lost → HO failure / drop. CHO (Rel-16) fixes this: the gNB prepares one or more candidate cells in advance and hands the UE execution conditions. The UE keeps serving traffic and, the instant a condition is met, executes the prepared HO autonomously. Big win for high-speed and mmWave (fast fading) where the command-to-execution window is fatal.
Call flow
UE → gNB : MeasurementReport (early, before serving degrades) gNB → T-cells : prepare candidates (Xn/F1 HO Request, as normal) gNB → UE : RRCReconfiguration { conditionalReconfiguration: condReconfigToAddModList[ candidate cell + execution condition CondEvent (A3/A5) ] } UE : keeps serving link, EVALUATES conditions each cell UE : when cond met (e.g. RSRP_tgt > thr AND RSRP_srv < thr for TTT) -> EXECUTE autonomously -> RACH on that target UE → T-gNB : RRCReconfigurationComplete T-gNB : PathSwitch (as normal) ; other prepared candidates are released

UE never executes CHO → execution-condition thresholds unreachable (A5 thr1/thr2 too strict), or candidate RSRP not crossing → check the measConfig + reported RSRP. Interview Q&A
Q: How does CHO reduce handover failures? The target is prepared and the command delivered early, while the serving link is still good; the UE then executes at the right moment on its own — so a late-collapsing serving cell can't kill the (already-received) command.
Q: Baseline HO vs CHO — who decides the execution instant? Baseline: the network, via the RRCReconfiguration it sends after the report. CHO: the UE, when its pre-loaded execution condition is met.
Q (PW): Where would CHO matter for Metro? On B41/n41 (higher band, faster fading) and any high-mobility corridor, where the normal command-to-execution gap is where drops happen.

In plain English
An Xn handover test proves that a phone moving from one 5G cell to another keeps its call and data when the two cells belong to different CUs joined by the Xn link. In the lab nobody walks around: two programmable attenuators do the "walking". The test makes the source cell fade and the target cell grow, the phone reports that the neighbour is better, and the two CUs hand it over. The automation then opens the captured signalling and checks, message by message, that the right information was exchanged. This card is how the integration suite (pw-pytest, ZT5) does it; the theory of the flow is in HO03, the counters that must match are in ZT2.

Level 3 — what an expert notices
1. Why exactly one UE. The filters select messages, not UEs. The AMF-UE-NGAP-ID check takes the first PDU Session Resource Setup in the capture and looks for a Path Switch with the same ID. With two UEs the first packet may belong to the UE that did not move, and a correct HO fails. If you extend the test to multi-UE, key every filter on the UE's F1AP/XnAP IDs.
2. Why NEA0. F1AP and XnAP carry RRC messages inside containers. With null ciphering Wireshark (plus the Lua post-dissector) can decode measId, PCI and CGI inside them; with NEA1/NEA2 the SRB payload is encrypted and every RRC-level assertion would fail with NO_PACKETS. A failed "measId 34" check on a ciphered setup is a test-environment problem, not a RAN bug.
3. Know what the filter really checks. The log line for TCM-47429 says "XnAP UEContextRelease", but the filter matches the F1AP UE Context Release Command from the source CU to its DU. That release is the consequence of the XnAP UE Context Release sent by the target after Path Switch Ack, so the check is valid — but when it fails, look at both interfaces.
4. Hard-coded radio facts. Neighbours are added with SSB ARFCN 643968; the bandwidth cases move the SSB (645696). If the neighbour's SSB does not match the real one, the UE never measures it, no A3 fires, and the test fails at "HandoverRequest NO_PACKETS" with a perfectly healthy Xn.
5. Negative tests are as valuable as positive ones. hoallowed=false must produce silence on Xn; handinAdmin=false must produce a correctly built HandoverPreparationFailure with the right cause and the right failure counters. Both catch real bugs (a CU that ignores its own policy, or one that rejects with a wrong cause).
Coverage note for the owner: the whole integration suite has no conditional-handover (CHO) test and no CMAS/ETWS test. HO06 (CHO) is theory only on this site; if CHO is on a roadmap, this Xn skeleton plus a condition-based reconfiguration check would be the natural starting point.
Interview Q&A
Q: How do you trigger a handover in an automated lab test without moving the UE?
A: With programmable attenuators on each cell's RF path. Start with the source strong and the target at maximum attenuation, then step the target down and the source up (here 2 dB every 200 ms on all four antennas). The UE sees the target become better than the serving cell by the A3 offset for the time-to-trigger, reports it, and the network runs the HO on its own — so the whole decision chain is tested, not just the execution.
Q: Which IEs would you check to prove an Xn handover really happened end to end?
A: Cause handover-desirable-for-radio-reasons in the XnAP HandoverRequest; SpCell-ID and SRB/DRB IDs in the target's F1AP UE Context Setup Request; the Target-to-Source transparent container (the RRC HO command) in the HO Ack; an SN Status Transfer; a Path Switch Request/Ack whose AMF-UE-NGAP-ID equals the source's (same UE context in the core); and the source releasing the UE with normal-release.
Q: Why must ciphering be NEA0 for this kind of test?
A: Because the checks decode RRC content (measId, neighbour PCI, CGI, HO command) carried inside F1AP/XnAP. With ciphering on, that payload is encrypted in the capture and the assertions cannot see it. It is a test-visibility requirement, not a product requirement.
Q: Give two negative Xn HO tests and their expected outcomes.
A: HO not allowed on the neighbour relation: no HandoverRequest is sent at all. Hand-in disabled on the target: the target answers with HandoverPreparationFailure, cause handover-target-not-allowed, and the source's prep-failure-target-not-allowed counter equals its hand-out attempts.
Q: The test failed with NO_PACKETS on the HandoverRequest filter although Xn is up. Where do you look?
A: At the radio trigger, not Xn: was the neighbour added with the correct SSB ARFCN, did the UE get a measConfig for it, did a MeasurementReport arrive, did the attenuators actually move, and is the UE still attached? Only if a report arrived and no request followed is it a CU decision problem (neighbour relation hoallowed, Xn TNL, admission).

So the integration suite checks two things: the CU's own hand-out / hand-in counters after an Xn handover, and a handover success rate computed from the signalling during a long, repeated inter-CU handover run. Level 3 — pitfalls and how to read a failing number
Required vs Notify are the right pair. HandoverRequired is the source's intent (every attempt, including ones later cancelled or rejected with HandoverPreparationFailure); HandoverNotify is sent by the target only when the UE has arrived. Their ratio is the end-to-end success rate as the core sees it. Counting HandoverCommand instead of Required would hide preparation failures.
Expected = 1 needs exactly one HO. In the Xn counter case a slow attenuation ramp can cause a ping-pong (source → target → source). The counters are then correctly 2, but a hard-coded expectation of 1 fails. Deriving the expectation from the capture (as TCM-47430 does) avoids this, at the price that a message missing from the capture lowers both sides and hides the error — cross-check with the UE log when numbers look low.
Counter timing. Counters are written per reporting period. Reading before the next counter XML is generated returns the previous period (often 0) — hence "after new xml generated" and a watch started before the traffic.
Loose throughput gates. A 0.1 pass fraction only proves data kept flowing across 20 swings, not HO interruption time; judge interruption from iperf per-second gaps or from UE logs. MRO-type issues (too-early / too-late / wrong-cell, LF11) are not measured by these tests.
No HO at all is its own verdict. "HO_Req=0" usually means the trigger never worked (neighbour/SSB config, attenuator server, UE detached), not a 0 % success rate — the suite correctly reports it as a different failure.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is the difference between HO preparation success and execution success?
A: Preparation is network-to-network: the target accepted and returned the HO command (Attempt → PrepSuccess). Execution is the UE part: after receiving the command the UE synchronises and completes on the target (ExeAttempt → Success). Prep failures point to admission or config; execution failures point to radio (RACH, RLF, T304).
Q: How does the inter-CU CI compute the handover success rate?
A: It captures NGAP/F1AP on both CUs during 20 attenuation swings with 8 UEs, counts HandoverRequired (source side) and HandoverNotify (target side) in both directions, and computes 100 × ΣNotify / ΣRequired rounded to 3 decimals. It passes at 85 % or more and fails separately if no HandoverRequired was seen.
Q: Why derive expected counter values from the packet capture instead of hard-coding them?
A: So the test stays correct when the radio produces more than one HO (ping-pong) and still checks that the counters match what really happened. The trade-off is that a capture gap affects both sides, so it complements, not replaces, a sanity check on absolute numbers.
Q: Hand-in is disabled on the target. Which counters should move?
A: Source: XnHandOutAttempt and XnHandOutPrepFailureTargetNotAllowed by the same amount, no PrepSuccess. Target: XnHandInAttempt and XnHandInPrepFailure by the same amount. The Xn message is HandoverPreparationFailure with cause handover-target-not-allowed.
Physical-layer channels in depth — PUCCH, PDCCH, PRACH, TDD
NS27 PUCCH Formats 0–4 in Depth — closed-loop power control & UCI on PUSCH 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
After the gNB sends a UE data, it needs to hear back quickly: "got it" or "resend" (HARQ-ACK), "I have data to send" (scheduling request) and "here is how good my channel looks" (CSI). That small but vital feedback travels on PUCCH. NR offers five PUCCH "envelopes" (formats 0–4): tiny fast ones for 1–2 bits, long robust ones for the cell edge, and bigger ones for CSI reports. The UE sets its PUCCH power from a formula plus small +/− corrections the gNB sends in every DL grant. And when the UE happens to be sending data on PUSCH at the same moment, the feedback simply rides inside the PUSCH instead. This card goes deeper than the overview in NS08.

  • Channel coding (TS 38.212 §6.3.1): 1–2 bits are sent uncoded (repetition/simplex), 3–11 bits use a Reed-Muller (32,K) block code, 12 bits and above use Polar coding with CRC (6-bit CRC for 12–19 bits, 11-bit CRC for 20+).
  • UCI types: HARQ-ACK, SR and CSI (CSI part 1 = RI/CRI/wideband CQI; part 2 = PMI and subband CQI, whose size depends on part 1).
  • Before RRC config the UE uses one of 16 common PUCCH resource sets signalled by pucch-ResourceCommon in SIB1 (TS 38.213 Table 9.2.1-1) — only F0 or F1.
  • After RRC config: up to 4 PUCCH resource sets per UL BWP. Set 0 carries ≤2 bits and can hold up to 32 resources; sets 1–3 hold up to 8 each and are chosen by payload size (maxPayloadSize). The 3-bit PUCCH Resource Indicator (PRI) in DCI 1_0/1_1 picks the resource inside the set (for set 0 with >8 resources the starting CCE of the PDCCH is also used).
  • Timing: the PDSCH-to-HARQ feedback timing indicator (K1) in the DL DCI tells which slot the ACK goes in.
Level 1 — PUCCH power control (TS 38.213 §7.2)
Transmit power in PUCCH occasion i:
P = min{ PCMAX, P0_PUCCH + 10·log10(2μ·MRB) + PL + ΔF_PUCCH(F) + ΔTF + g(i) }
  • P0_PUCCH = cell-level p0-nominal + optional UE-specific offset — the target received power per RB.
  • PL — the UE's DL path-loss estimate from the configured reference signal (SSB or CSI-RS). Note: for PUCCH the path loss is always fully compensated (no α factor, unlike PUSCH).
  • ΔF_PUCCH(F) — per-format offset (deltaF-PUCCH-f0…f4) so formats with different processing gain land at the right SINR.
  • ΔTF — adjusts for payload / number of symbols (more bits in same resource → more power).
  • g(i) — closed-loop state. TPC commands are accumulated only for PUCCH: 2-bit field mapping to −1, 0, +1, +3 dB. Sources: DCI 1_0/1_1 (per-UE) and DCI 2_2 (group TPC, TPC-PUCCH-RNTI). Up to two closed-loop states can be configured (twoPUCCH-PC-AdjustmentStates), e.g. one per beam/TRP.
  • g is reset when P0 is reconfigured; after random access it starts from the Msg2 TPC plus the power ramp-up the UE used on PRACH.
Level 1 — UCI multiplexed on PUSCH (TS 38.212 §6.3.2.4, TS 38.213 §9.3)
  • If a PUCCH carrying HARQ-ACK/CSI overlaps in time with a PUSCH of the same UE (and the processing timeline is met), the UE does not send PUCCH — it puts the UCI inside the PUSCH. NR (Rel-15) does not require simultaneous PUCCH+PUSCH on one carrier.
  • Number of REs for UCI is set by beta-offsets (semi-static, or dynamic via the 2-bit beta_offset indicator in DCI 0_1) and capped by the scaling α (0.5 / 0.65 / 0.8 / 1) so UCI never eats the whole PUSCH.
  • Mapping: HARQ-ACK goes on the first non-DMRS symbols after the first DMRS (best channel estimate), then CSI part 1, then CSI part 2, then UL-SCH data.
  • HARQ-ACK ≤2 bits punctures the data (the UE and gNB still rate-match data as if no ACK were there — robust against DCI misses); >2 bits rate-matches around it.
  • SR is never multiplexed on PUSCH — if the UE is already transmitting PUSCH it just sends a BSR MAC CE.
  • A PUSCH can also carry only UCI (aperiodic CSI with the UL-SCH indicator = 0 in DCI 0_1).
Level 2 — how to test and what to look at
  • Conformance (TS 38.141-1/-2, clause 8.3): PUCCH F0/F1 are tested for ACK missed-detection and NACK→ACK error; F1 also for DTX→ACK false detection; F2 for ACK missed detection and CSI BLER; F3/F4 for UCI BLER — each at a specified SNR under fading channels. Cross-check with UPERF01.
  • Lab/functional: sweep UE distance (or attenuator) and watch HARQ-ACK DTX rate on PUCCH (gNB expected ACK/NACK but detected nothing). A rising DTX rate at the edge before PUSCH BLER rises means PUCCH is your coverage limiter — try a long format (F1/F3) or more symbols.
  • Power-control check: in the UE log compare the PUCCH TX power against the formula; step the attenuator by 10 dB and confirm TX power follows PL 1:1; send TPC +3 dB bursts and confirm accumulation and clipping at PCMAX (power headroom report goes to ~0).
  • gNB side: received PUCCH SINR per UE should converge to the target; a persistent offset usually means wrong p0-nominal / ΔF or a UL path imbalance (RU RX gain, cable loss).
  • UCI-on-PUSCH: schedule continuous UL traffic plus DL traffic; the UE log should show HARQ-ACK carried on PUSCH and no PUCCH in those slots. Verify beta-offset changes move the UCI RE count and the DL HARQ DTX rate.
  • Messages to check: PUCCH-Config (resourceSetToAddModList, resourceToAddModList, format1/2/3/4 parameters, dl-DataToUL-ACK), PUCCH-ConfigCommon (pucch-ResourceCommon, p0-nominal), PUCCH-PowerControl, SchedulingRequestResourceConfig, CSI-ReportConfig, uci-OnPUSCH in PUSCH-Config.
Level 3 — design trade-offs and real pitfalls
  • PUCCH capacity: F0/F1 let many UEs share one PRB (12 cyclic shifts, times OCC for F1), so ACK/SR capacity is cheap. F2/F3 are one-UE-per-resource — CSI-heavy configs (many UEs, short CSI periodicity, subband CQI) eat PUCCH PRBs quickly. Vendors typically stagger CSI periodicity/offsets across UEs.
  • Band-edge placement: PUCCH is normally placed at both edges of the BWP with intra-slot frequency hopping for diversity, leaving a contiguous middle for PUSCH. Too many PUCCH PRBs reduces peak UL throughput; too few causes PUCCH resource blocking and limits connected UEs (see NS35).
  • TDD: many DL slots feed ACKs into few UL slots → large HARQ-ACK codebooks (Type-1 semi-static or Type-2 dynamic with DAI). Payload jumps from F0/F1 to F2/F3 and the link budget shrinks — a classic cause of DL throughput collapse at cell edge in DL-heavy patterns.
  • DAI mistakes: with the dynamic (Type-2) codebook, a missed DCI is detected by the counter DAI; a mismatch in gNB/UE codebook size makes the whole ACK payload undecodable — shows up as bursts of DTX for multiple HARQ processes at once.
  • Power-control windup: if the gNB keeps sending +TPC to a UE already at PCMAX, g(i) accumulates; when the UE moves closer it overshoots. Good implementations stop sending +TPC when PHR ≈ 0.
  • F0 threshold tuning: F0 is detected by correlation across cyclic shifts; the detection threshold trades DTX→ACK false alarms (dangerous — lost data never retransmitted at HARQ level) against missed ACKs. This is why DTX→ACK has its own conformance requirement.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why does NR have short and long PUCCH?
A: Short PUCCH (F0/F2, 1–2 symbols) gives very fast feedback at the end of a slot and suits self-contained TDD slots. Long PUCCH (F1/F3/F4, 4–14 symbols) accumulates more energy for cell-edge coverage. F0/F1 carry up to 2 bits, F2/F3/F4 carry more (CSI, big ACK codebooks).
Q: Which formats can multiplex several UEs in one PRB and how?
A: F0 by cyclic shifts, F1 by cyclic shifts plus time-domain orthogonal cover codes, and F4 by pre-DFT OCC (2 or 4 UEs). F2 and F3 are single-UE per resource.
Q: How is PUCCH TPC different from PUSCH TPC?
A: PUCCH TPC is always accumulated (−1/0/+1/+3 dB), comes in DL DCIs (1_0/1_1) or group DCI 2_2, and PUCCH path loss is fully compensated (no alpha). PUSCH TPC comes in UL DCI or DCI 2_2, can be accumulated or absolute, and PUSCH supports fractional path-loss compensation.
Q: What happens to UCI when PUCCH and PUSCH collide?
A: The UCI is multiplexed on PUSCH: HARQ-ACK next to the first DMRS, then CSI part 1/part 2, with RE count set by beta-offsets and capped by alpha. ACK ≤2 bits punctures data, larger ACK rate-matches. SR is dropped and replaced by BSR.
Q: You see high HARQ DTX at cell edge but PUSCH BLER is fine — what do you check?
A: PUCCH link budget: format and number of symbols, PUCCH received SINR vs target, p0-nominal and deltaF, whether the ACK codebook grew into F2/F3, TPC windup at Pcmax, and DAI/codebook mismatch.
NS28 PDCCH in Depth — CORESET duration, search spaces, dynamic aggregation level & DCI 0_1 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
PDCCH is the gNB's "notice board": it tells each UE where its data is and how to decode it (the DCI). The board lives in a small time-frequency region called a CORESET (1–3 OFDM symbols tall). Level 2 — dynamic aggregation level, testing and measuring
  • How vendors typically pick AL: map the UE's wideband CQI/SINR to the lowest AL that meets a PDCCH BLER target (commonly around 1%), then run an outer loop: if the gNB sends a DL grant and sees HARQ DTX (no ACK/NACK at all), the PDCCH was probably missed → raise the offset; successes slowly lower it. Common channels (SIB1, paging, RAR) use a fixed high AL (4–16) since they must reach the whole cell.
  • Trade-off: higher AL = more reliable but fewer UEs per slot. With 45 CCEs, only 2 UEs at AL16 fit, versus up to 45 at AL1. This is why cell-edge-heavy loads hit PDCCH blocking (no free CCEs for a candidate) before PDSCH PRBs run out.
  • KPIs/logs: PDCCH CCE utilisation, AL distribution histogram, PDCCH allocation-failure (blocking) counts, DL/UL HARQ DTX rate, scheduled UEs per slot. UE side: DCI decode logs showing RNTI, format, AL, CCE index.
  • Test ideas: (1) fix AL and sweep SNR to find where DTX starts — compare AL1 vs AL8 thresholds (~9 dB apart in theory: 10·log10(8)). (2) Load many UEs and check blocking vs CORESET duration 1→2 symbols. (3) Verify the UE never exceeds candidate/CCE limits when multiple search spaces coincide. (4) Change controlResourceSetZero and confirm UEs still read SIB1.
  • Conformance: PDCCH demodulation is a UE test (TS 38.101-4) — not a BS RF test; on the BS side you verify via functional/KPI tests.
  • RRC to check: PDCCH-Config (controlResourceSetToAddModList, searchSpacesToAddModList), PDCCH-ConfigCommon, ControlResourceSet (duration, frequencyDomainResources, cce-REG-MappingType, precoderGranularity, tci-StatesPDCCH), SearchSpace (nrofCandidates, dci-Formats).
Level 3 — expert detail and pitfalls
  • Beam management and PDCCH: each CORESET has a TCI state (which SSB/CSI-RS beam to assume). A wrong or stale TCI after beam change looks like sudden HARQ DTX bursts even though CQI is fine — check MAC CE "TCI State Indication for UE-specific PDCCH".
  • precoderGranularity: sameAsREG-bundle vs allContiguousRBs. Wideband precoding allows better channel estimation across the whole CORESET; a UE that doesn't interpolate correctly may show higher PDCCH miss rate.
  • CORESET#0 and SSB multiplexing: the MIB index picks one of the multiplexing patterns (1 = TDM, 2/3 = FDM with SSB). A wrong offset makes UEs camp but fail SIB1 decode — a classic "UE sees the cell but never attempts access".
  • DCI size budget pitfalls: adding a feature (e.g. extra DCI 2_x for power saving) can break the 3/4-size rule; the network then pads/truncates 0_0/1_0, and a UE implementation mismatch shows as a total DCI decode failure after reconfiguration.
  • PDCCH overbooking: for the PCell the standard allows USS overbooking with a defined drop rule; misunderstanding which candidates are dropped leads to "grant sent but UE never saw it".
  • Rel-16/17 extras (awareness): DCI 0_2/1_2 compact formats for URLLC, span-based monitoring (more occasions per slot), PDCCH repetition across TRPs, PDCCH-skipping for power saving.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is a CORESET and how big can it be?
A: A time-frequency region carrying PDCCH: 1–3 OFDM symbols in time and a bitmap of 6-PRB groups in frequency. Built from REGs (1 PRB × 1 symbol), REG bundles and CCEs (6 REGs). Up to 3 CORESETs per BWP in Rel-15; CORESET#0 is configured from the MIB.
Q: Common vs UE-specific search space?
A: CSS (types 0/0A/1/2/3) are monitored by all or groups of UEs for SIB, RAR, paging and group commands; USS is per UE for normal scheduling with its C-RNTI, using the hashing function to spread positions.
Q: How does dynamic aggregation level work and what is the trade-off?
A: The gNB picks AL (1–16) per UE from CQI/SINR to hit a PDCCH BLER target, with an outer loop driven by HARQ DTX. Higher AL is more robust but consumes more CCEs, raising PDCCH blocking and limiting UEs per slot.
Q: Name five fields of DCI 0_1 and what they do.
A: e.g. TDRA (K2 and PUSCH symbols), FDRA (PRBs), MCS (modulation/code rate), TPC (power), SRS resource indicator/precoding (UL MIMO), CSI request (aperiodic CSI), beta_offset indicator (UCI on PUSCH), UL-SCH indicator (UCI-only PUSCH).
Q: Why would you increase CORESET duration from 1 to 2 symbols?
A: To double the CCE pool when PDCCH blocking limits throughput or number of scheduled UEs (many edge UEs at high AL, narrow bandwidth). The cost is ~7% fewer PDSCH symbols per slot.

In plain English
PRACH is the UE's "knock on the door". Because the UE doesn't yet know its distance to the gNB, its knock can arrive late by the round-trip delay — so the PRACH burst carries a cyclic prefix and a silent guard time sized for the biggest cell you want to serve. NR gives two families: long preambles (length-839 sequence, formats 0–3) for big FR1 macro cells, very large radius or high speed, and short preambles (length-139, formats A/B/C) that fit inside a few OFDM symbols — ideal for TDD, small cells, beam sweeping and FR2.   • 64 preambles per RACH occasion per cell, built from cyclic shifts of one or more Zadoff-Chu roots (prach-RootSequenceIndex: 0–837 long, 0–137 short). Preambles per root = floor(L / NCS).

  • Restricted sets (long preambles only): unrestrictedSet, restrictedSetTypeA, restrictedSetTypeB. Level 2 — choosing and testing a format
  • Selection checklist: duplex mode (FDD → long formats easy; TDD → short or format 0 in a UL-rich pattern), required radius (ISD/2 plus margin, or terrain-limited distance for rural), speed profile (format 3 or restricted set), FR2/beam sweeping (short only), link budget (format 2 or B4 for more energy).
  • Radius sanity: don't forget both limits — CP/GT from the format and NCS. A format-1 cell with a small NCS will still fail for far UEs (their preamble shifts into the next preamble's zone → wrong preamble ID / wrong TA).
  • Conformance (TS 38.141-1/-2 clause 8.4): PRACH false-alarm probability ≤ 0.1% and detection probability ≥ 99% at a specified SNR, for AWGN and fading, with timing offsets; tests exist per format class. Cross-check FC10, UPERF01, PRACHDM01.
  • Lab tests: (1) emulate delay with a channel emulator or fibre spool: step round-trip delay up to the design radius and confirm detection + correct TA; beyond radius expect failures. (2) Frequency offset/Doppler test for format 3 or restricted sets. (3) Missed-Msg1 rate vs preambleReceivedTargetPower and ramping step. (4) Check prach-ConfigurationIndex against the TDD pattern — every occasion must fall in UL symbols.
  • KPIs/logs: RACH attempts vs Msg2 sent vs Msg3 received, detected TA distribution (reveals real cell radius and overshooting), preamble power, false detections with no UE present (noise/interference), RRC setup success.
Level 3 — expert detail and pitfalls
  • TA range vs format: the TA command in the RAR is 12 bits (0–3846) in units of 16·64·Tc/2μ; at 15 kHz that is ~2 ms of round trip (~300 km) — the preamble format and NCS, not TA signalling, are the real radius limits.
  • Overshooting cells: a TA histogram with a tail far beyond the ISD shows over-shooting (downtilt/terrain). With format 0 those UEs may produce ghost preamble IDs in neighbour zones.
  • Root sequence collisions: neighbour cells on the same root and same RACH occasion create false alarms and Msg2 for phantom preambles — plan roots like PCI (reuse distance).
  • Format 1/2 in TDD: needs 3+ ms of continuous UL — practically impossible in DL-heavy patterns (e.g. DDDSU). Large-radius TDD usually uses format 0 with a UL-favouring pattern or short C2 with a big guard period (see NS32 on TDD guard vs cell radius).
  • Interference: long formats occupy only ~1 MHz; a narrowband interferer there kills RACH while data looks fine — check RSSI per PRB in the PRACH region.
  • RACH capacity: msg1-FDM (1/2/4/8 occasions in frequency) and SSB-per-RO mapping define RACH capacity; too few ROs with many beams increases access delay.
Interview Q&A
Q: Long vs short PRACH preambles?
A: Long: ZC length 839, 1.25 or 5 kHz SCS, formats 0–3, FR1 only, large radius/coverage/high speed, ~1 MHz wide. Short: length 139, SCS 15–120 kHz, formats A1–C2, fit inside OFDM symbols, suited to TDD, small cells, beam sweeping and FR2.
Q: When would you use PRACH format 1?
A: For very large cell radius (up to ~100 km): its 684 µs CP and ~716 µs guard absorb the long round-trip delay — rural macro, sea coverage, air-to-ground. It costs 3 ms of UL time.
Q: What limits the cell radius for PRACH?
A: The format's CP/guard time and the cyclic-shift size N_CS (zero-correlation zone). Both must exceed round-trip delay plus delay spread; ~150 m of radius per µs.
Q: What are restricted sets and why only for long preambles?
A: Subsets of cyclic shifts that avoid aliasing from Doppler-induced correlation peaks at high speed. Long preambles have narrow 1.25 kHz SCS so Doppler is a large fraction of it; short preambles use wide SCS and don't need them.
Q: RACH success is poor only for far UEs — what do you check?
A: Format radius vs actual distance, N_CS (zeroCorrelationZoneConfig), TA histogram, preamble target power and ramping, UL interference in PRACH PRBs, and root reuse with neighbours.
NS32 NR TDD Patterns in Practice — DL/UL slot patterns, special-slot symbols, guard period vs cell radius 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
In TDD the same frequency is shared by downlink and uplink, taking turns in time — like a single-lane bridge with traffic lights. The TDD pattern is the traffic-light programme: how many slots go gNB→UE (D), how many UE→gNB (U), and a special slot (S) in between that contains a few DL symbols, an empty guard period (GP) and a few UL symbols. More D slots = more download speed; more U slots = better upload and faster feedback; a longer GP = bigger cell but wasted air time. Every cell in an area (and every operator in the band) must run the same pattern, time-aligned, or one gNB's downlink blasts straight into its neighbour's uplink receiver. TDD01 covers how the pattern is pushed to an O-RAN radio; this card is the design side.
Level 1 — how the pattern is defined (TS 38.331 / 38.213)

Using S = 10D:2G:2U instead of 6D:4G:4U adds 4 DL symbols per period — about +6% DL capacity on DDDSU — at the cost of GP (radius) and UL symbols (SRS/short PUCCH room). Some UEs/chipsets and schedulers only use S-slot DL symbols for PDSCH if the allocation fits the reduced length (PDSCH mapping type A needs enough symbols after DMRS); check that PDSCH is actually scheduled in S, not just PDCCH.
Guard period vs cell radius. Rule of thumb: dmax ≈ c·(TGP − Toffset − Tswitch)/2. At 30 kHz, 2 GP symbols (71.4 µs) minus ~13 µs offset gives roughly 8–9 km; 4 GP symbols roughly 19 km. At 15 kHz each symbol is twice as long, so radius per GP symbol doubles. PRACH format also caps radius (see NS07/NS29).
10 MHz TDD (e.g. refarmed LTE TDD blocks) is cramped: 24 PRBs at 30 kHz leaves little room for CORESET, SSB and PUCCH, so 15 kHz SCS is often chosen for narrow TDD carriers (52 PRBs, longer symbols, longer GP per symbol). 40 MHz at 30 kHz (106 PRBs) is a common mid-band private/regional allocation.
Test checklist:
  • Decode SIB1 (UE log / network scanner) and confirm periodicity, slot and symbol counts match the planned pattern; confirm dedicated/dynamic overrides are what you expect.
  • Spectrum analyser in time-domain (zero span / gated sweep) or a VSA: confirm DL bursts and the gap lengths; measure ON/OFF transient and OFF power (TS 38.141 transmitter OFF power).
  • Frame alignment: compare frame start against a GNSS 1PPS reference; the TDD cell phase synchronisation requirement is 3 µs between cells (i.e. ±1.5 µs to a common reference) in TS 38.133.
  • Throughput per pattern: run full-buffer DL and UL, compare with the table above; a mismatch often means S-slot symbols unused or UL slots partly used by PUCCH/PRACH.
  • Latency: ping RTT and HARQ feedback delay (K1) change with how often a U slot appears — DDDSU beats a 5 ms pattern.
  • Far-UE / attenuated test with a channel emulator adding delay: watch UL BLER and TA; UL degrades once TA eats into the GP.
Level 3 — expert pitfalls
  • Co-existence with neighbours: operators in adjacent TDD blocks must agree the same pattern and frame start, otherwise a strong DL next door lands in your UL — seen as raised UL noise floor (RSSI per PRB) only in certain symbols. Look at UL interference per symbol, not averaged.
  • LTE TDD co-existence: aligning NR with an LTE TDD configuration requires a matching NR pattern plus a frame offset; the offset must also be set in the radio (TDD01 shows the M-plane side). A wrong offset gives interference that looks like a hardware fault.
  • Remote interference / atmospheric ducting: far-away gNBs' DL can arrive tens to hundreds of km later and hit the first UL symbols. Symptom: early UL symbols noisier at night/in certain weather. Mitigation is a bigger GP or Rel-16 remote interference management (RIM reference signals).
  • PRACH placement: in unpaired spectrum PRACH occasions must fall in UL (or valid flexible) symbols; picking a PRACH configuration index whose occasions land in D slots silently leaves no valid RACH occasions — cell looks fine, nobody can attach.
  • NTA,offset: 25600·Tc (~13 µs) for FR1 TDD, 39936·Tc when LTE-NR co-existence is configured, 0 for FDD. A mismatch between what the UE applies and what the gNB receiver expects shows up as a constant UL timing error.
  • UL-heavy patterns cost DL capacity and SSB/CORESET opportunities; check that the SSB burst still fits in D slots for the planned beam count.
Interview Q&A
Q: What does DDDSU mean and what is its periodicity at 30 kHz?
A: Three DL slots, one special slot, one UL slot. At 30 kHz a slot is 0.5 ms, so the pattern repeats every 2.5 ms (four times per 10 ms frame).
Q: Why would you move symbols from the guard period into the DL part of the special slot?
A: To gain DL throughput (a few percent). The price is a shorter GP, which limits cell radius and robustness to remote interference, so it suits small/medium urban cells, not rural macro.
Q: How do you estimate the maximum cell radius from the GP?
A: GP must cover round-trip delay plus the TA offset and switching time: d ≈ c(T_GP − T_offset − T_switch)/2. At 30 kHz that is roughly 5.4 km per GP symbol after the offset is paid.
Q: Why must neighbouring TDD cells use the same pattern and be time-synchronised?
A: Otherwise one cell transmits DL while the neighbour receives UL on the same or adjacent frequency; the high-power DL desensitises the UL receiver. Hence GNSS/PTP sync to within a few microseconds.
Q: Where does the UE learn the TDD pattern?
A: From tdd-UL-DL-ConfigurationCommon in SIB1 (ServingCellConfigCommonSIB), optionally refined per UE in RRC (tdd-UL-DL-ConfigurationDedicated) and dynamically with DCI format 2_0 slot format indication for flexible symbols.

NS30 Cell Barring & Unified Access Control — access identities, access categories, SIB1 uac-BarringInfo 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Sometimes the network must say "not now". Cell barring is the blunt tool: the MIB tells every UE "don't camp here" (e.g. during maintenance or for a test cell). Unified Access Control (UAC) is the fine-grained tool for overload: each access attempt is labelled with who the user is (access identity, e.g. normal subscriber, emergency services, operator staff) and what they want (access category, e.g. paging response, emergency call, voice, data). SIB1 then says, per category, "let only 30% through and make the rest wait". Think of a stadium gate: VIPs and ambulances go straight in, everyone else rolls dice to get in and retries after a random wait. In LTE this needed several separate mechanisms (ACB, SSAC, EAB, ACDC); NR unifies them.

Level 1 — UAC in SIB1 and the barring check (TS 38.331 §5.3.14)
  • uac-BarringInfo in SIB1: uac-BarringForCommon (list of access category → barring-info-set index), optional uac-BarringPerPLMN-List, and uac-BarringInfoSetList (up to 8 sets).
  • Each UAC-BarringInfoSet: uac-BarringFactor p00…p95 (probability of passing), uac-BarringTime s4…s512, and uac-BarringForAccessIdentity — a 7-bit bitmap for identities 1, 2, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15.
  • Algorithm: category 0 → allowed. If no barring info for that category → allowed. If the UE has a valid special access identity whose bit is 0 → allowed. Otherwise draw rand ∈ [0,1): rand < barringFactor → allowed; else barred and start T390 for that category with duration (0.7 + 0.6·rand) × uac-BarringTime.
  • While T390 runs, further attempts of that category are barred; on expiry RRC tells NAS "barring alleviated" and the attempt can be retried.
  • Interaction with RRC reject: after RRCReject with waitTime, T302 runs and all categories except 0 and 2 are considered barred.
  • Category 1 has its own assistance info (uac-AccessCategory1-SelectionAssistanceInfo) so delay-tolerant devices can be throttled separately.
Level 2 — configuring and testing
  • Cell barring test: set cellBarred = barred with intraFreqReselection allowed → idle UEs reselect to another cell on the same frequency; set notAllowed → they leave the frequency. Verify with UE logs (MIB decode, cell excluded, reselection) and that the barred cell shows zero RRC setup attempts.
  • Reserved-for-operator test: a normal SIM should not camp; a test SIM configured with access class/identity 11 or 15 should camp (lab SIMs are often set this way — check the SIM before blaming the gNB).
  • UAC statistical test: configure barring for category 7 with factor p50 and barring time s16; generate repeated MO data attempts from many UEs (or one UE with scripted attempts) and check ~50% pass on first try; measure the retry spread (0.7–1.3 × 16 s).
  • Exemption test: same config, category 0 (page the UE) should always go through; a UE with access identity 1/2 and its bitmap bit 0 should pass.
  • SIB change: modify uac-BarringInfo on the fly — UEs must pick it up via the SI change procedure (short message on paging); check the time until barring takes effect.
  • KPIs: RRC setup attempts per establishment cause, RRC setup success ratio, RRC reject counts with waitTime, paging success. Barring works before RRC setup, so barred attempts are invisible at the gNB — you only see fewer attempts. Use UE-side logs or a UE simulator to count barred attempts.
  • Caution: in a live or shared lab, never test barring on a cell that may carry real emergency traffic.
Level 3 — design notes and pitfalls
  • Overload strategy: vendors typically combine several layers — UAC in SIB1 (pre-RRC), RRC reject with waitTime (at Msg4), AMF overload start (NGAP) leading to reject/back-off, and admission control on RRC-connected users. UAC is the only one that avoids RACH load altogether.
  • Automatic UAC: some implementations raise barring automatically from load measurements (RACH load, CPU, connected UEs). Too aggressive → "no service" complaints; too slow → RACH storm after a power outage or large event. A slow recovery ramp avoids oscillation.
  • Per-PLMN in RAN sharing: uac-BarringPerPLMN-List lets each sharing operator throttle its own subscribers without hurting the other.
  • Slice-based barring: operator-defined categories (32–63) can map to a specific S-NSSAI/DNN, giving a way to throttle one slice's access — relevant to 5G slicing tests.
  • Common confusion: LTE access classes (AC 0–9 normal, 10 emergency, 11–15 special) vs NR access identities — NR removed the random "normal access class" 0–9; randomness now comes from the per-attempt draw.
  • Emergency calls on a barred cell: MIB barring blocks camping for everyone; a UE needing emergency calls must find another cell. With UAC, emergency is category 2 and can still be barred unless identity bits exempt it — design choices must follow regulator rules.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is the difference between cell barring and UAC?
A: Cell barring (MIB cellBarred, SIB1 reservation) stops UEs camping on a cell at all. UAC keeps the cell usable but probabilistically blocks access attempts per access category, with exemptions for special access identities, to control overload.
Q: Explain access identity vs access category.
A: Access identity describes the user/subscription (0 normal, 1 MPS, 2 MCS, 11–15 special classes). Access category describes the attempt type (0 MT/paging, 2 emergency, 3 signalling, 4 voice, 5 video, 6 SMS, 7 data, 8 RRC signalling, 32–63 operator-defined). Barring is configured per category, with per-identity exemption bits.
Q: Walk through the UAC barring check.
A: Category 0 is allowed. If SIB1 has barring info for the category and no valid identity is exempt, the UE draws rand; if rand < barringFactor it proceeds, otherwise it is barred and starts T390 = (0.7+0.6·rand)·barringTime before retrying.
Q: What does intraFreqReselection = notAllowed do?
A: When the cell is barred, the UE may not reselect to other cells on the same frequency and treats the frequency as barred for up to 300 s, pushing it to another frequency or RAT.
Q: Why can't you see UAC-barred attempts in gNB counters?
A: The check happens in the UE before any RACH/RRC setup, so the gNB just sees fewer attempts. You need UE logs or a UE simulator to measure barred attempts.
NS31 5G → 4G Mobility — idle reselection, inter-RAT handover over N26 & A2/B1 redirection 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
When a 5G SA user walks out of NR coverage, the network must hand them to LTE before the link breaks. There are three ways: in idle the UE decides itself (reselection using priorities broadcast in SIB5); in connected mode the gNB can do a proper handover through the cores, which keeps the IP session alive — this needs the N26 interface between the 5G AMF and the 4G MME; or the gNB can simply release the UE with a note "go to this LTE frequency" (redirection), which is simpler but causes a short gap. LF17 covers 4G↔5G interworking from the LTE side and NS20 covers EPS fallback for voice; this card focuses on the 5G→4G direction for coverage loss.
Level 1 — idle mode: NR → LTE reselection (TS 38.304 §5.2.4, TS 38.331 SIB5)
  • SIB5 lists LTE carriers (carrierFreqListEUTRA): EARFCN, cellReselectionPriority, threshX-High, threshX-Low, q-RxLevMin, q-QualMin, allowed bandwidth, PCI exclusion list; plus t-ReselectionEUTRA.
  • LTE lower priority (the usual design, NR preferred): reselect when serving NR Srxlev < threshServingLowP and the LTE cell Srxlev > threshX-LowP for TreselectionEUTRA.
  • Measurement trigger: the UE only has to measure lower-priority inter-RAT carriers when serving Srxlev ≤ s-NonIntraSearchP (and quality ≤ s-NonIntraSearchQ, if configured).
  • Dedicated priorities can be given in RRCRelease (cellReselectionPriorities, valid for T320) — e.g. to steer a UE after release.
  • Reverse direction: the LTE side broadcasts NR carriers in SIB24 — keep the two sets of thresholds consistent to avoid ping-pong (see Level 3).

Level 2 — how to test it
  • Setup: NR cell + overlapping LTE cell, both cores with N26 (or a combined core emulator); UE with an ongoing ping or file transfer. Attenuate NR gradually (or drive out of coverage).
  • Checks for HO: RRCReconfiguration with measConfig containing an LTE measObject + B1/B2 reportConfig (+ gap config); MeasurementReport; NGAP HandoverRequired (target eNB ID, TAI, cause, source-to-target container); MobilityFromNRCommand; on LTE: RACH + RRCConnectionReconfigurationComplete; S1AP Handover Notify; UE context release on the NR side.
  • Measure: HO preparation and execution success, user-plane interruption (ping gap), IP address unchanged, packet loss with/without data forwarding, QoS flow → EPS bearer mapping (e.g. 5QI 9 → QCI 9 default bearer).
  • Without N26: expect the gNB to use redirection. Verify RRCRelease redirectedCarrierInfo EARFCN, LTE attach/TAU, whether the PDN session is kept (depends on "interworking without N26" support) and the service gap.
  • Idle: release the UE to idle on NR, lower NR RSRP below threshServingLow and confirm reselection to LTE after Treselection; then raise NR and confirm return (via LTE SIB24 priorities).
  • KPIs (TS 28.552 inter-system mobility measurements): inter-RAT HO preparation attempts/successes/failures by cause, execution success, redirections to E-UTRA, and — on LTE — incoming inter-RAT HO success. Watch drop rate at the NR edge before/after tuning.
  • Common failure causes: missing LTE neighbour relation (ANR off), wrong EARFCN/PCI, AMF can't resolve the target MME (TAI mapping), N26 not configured, UE capability lacks the LTE band, measurement gaps not configured so B1 never fires.
Level 3 — design trade-offs and pitfalls
  • Threshold design: set the A2 that starts measurement early enough to allow gaps + TTT + HO preparation before the NR radio link fails. Too early → UEs leave 5G unnecessarily (low 5G time share); too late → radio link failure and RRC re-establishment attempts that can't succeed on LTE.
  • Ping-pong loops: NR→LTE in connected mode by B1/B2, then LTE→NR idle reselection by high NR priority in SIB24 as soon as the UE goes idle — and back. Set the LTE-side NR threshold (threshX-High) above the NR→LTE trigger level with a few dB of hysteresis.
  • Uplink-limited edge: NR TDD mid-band often runs out of UL before DL; basing the trigger only on DL RSRP misses this. Vendors typically add UL-based triggers (PHR, UL BLER, SINR) or use a B2 RSRP threshold that anticipates the UL limit.
  • Redirection vs HO: redirection is simple and robust (no core dependency) but interrupts real-time sessions; HO keeps sessions but depends on N26, neighbour config and correct TAI→MME mapping. Many networks use HO as primary and blind redirection as safety net.
  • RRC re-establishment is not inter-RAT: after NR RLF the UE can only re-establish on NR; if it finds only LTE it goes idle and reselects, losing the connection. Hence the importance of a timely A2 redirection.
  • Voice: in a network without VoNR the same 5G→4G machinery is triggered by IMS voice setup (EPS fallback, NS20) instead of coverage.
Interview Q&A
Q: What are the three ways to move a UE from 5G SA to LTE?
A: Idle reselection (SIB5 priorities/thresholds), connected-mode inter-RAT handover via MobilityFromNRCommand (needs N26 between AMF and MME), and RRC release with redirection to an LTE carrier (measurement-based or blind A2).
Q: Why is N26 important?
A: N26 lets the AMF and MME exchange UE mobility and session context, enabling seamless inter-system handover and idle mobility with IP preservation. Without it the network uses redirection and the UE re-registers on EPC, with a longer interruption.
Q: B1 vs B2 — which would you use and why?
A: B1 triggers when the LTE neighbour exceeds a threshold — simple, used after an A2 has confirmed NR is weak. B2 combines "NR below thr1" and "LTE above thr2" in one event, preventing HO to LTE while NR is still good.
Q: What RRC message carries the 5G→4G handover command?
A: MobilityFromNRCommand with targetRAT-Type = eutra; its targetRAT-MessageContainer holds the LTE RRCConnectionReconfiguration (with mobilityControlInfo) built by the target eNB.
Q: UEs keep bouncing between NR and LTE — how do you fix it?
A: Compare the NR→LTE triggers (A2/B1/B2 thresholds) with the LTE→NR idle reselection priorities/thresholds in SIB24 and connected-mode B1 on LTE; add hysteresis so the return threshold is several dB above the leave threshold, and consider UL-based triggers.

  • Averaging window (AW): the period over which GFBR/MFBR are measured for GBR flows (default 2000 ms for standardized GBR 5QIs; can be signalled). The scheduler meets GFBR over the window, not in every slot.
  • MDBV: for delay-critical GBR a packet is only counted as a "PDB miss" if the burst stayed within MDBV and the flow within GFBR. The RAN uses MDBV to dimension pre-allocation (configured grant, reserved PRBs).

Test ideas:
  • Establish a 5QI 65 flow and a best-effort flow on the same UE and on different UEs; fill the cell with full-buffer 5QI 9 traffic and check the MC flow keeps its GFBR and latency (per-5QI DL delay counters, e.g. 28.552 DRB.RlcSduDelayDl per QoS class).
  • Admission: request GBR beyond remaining capacity; expect rejection or pre-emption of a lower-ARP flow (check NGAP failure causes and QoS-flow release counters).
  • Notification control: degrade the radio so GFBR cannot be met; the gNB should send a notification (PDU Session Resource Notify) if notification control was requested.
  • Dynamic 5QI: signal a non-standard 5QI with explicit descriptors and confirm the gNB honours the given PDB/priority rather than falling back to a default profile.
Level 3 — expert detail and pitfalls
  • Priority level vs scheduler weight: 23.501 priority is relative within one UE and between UEs, but many schedulers map it to a weight, not strict priority. 5QI 69 (priority 5) signalling should never starve behind 5QI 65 voice; test mixed MC signalling + voice under load.
  • Delay-critical GBR needs radio features, not just labels: hitting 5–10 ms PDB usually needs short UL periodicities (SR, configured grant type 1/2), mini-slot or short TDD periods, fewer HARQ retransmissions, possibly PDCP duplication. With a 5 ms TDD pattern the frame structure alone can consume most of a 5 ms budget.
  • GBR over a cell edge: GFBR guarantees break when MCS drops; admission control should reserve PRBs using a conservative spectral-efficiency estimate. Watch for "admitted then failing" flows — a common KPI trap.
  • LTE/5G interworking: LTE uses QCI 65, 66, 67, 69, 70 with the same meanings; on 5G→4G mobility (NS31) the mapping must survive, otherwise MC sessions drop at the boundary.
  • Counters: make sure per-5QI counters exist for non-default 5QIs — some deployments only aggregate "GBR" vs "non-GBR", hiding a failing MC flow inside averages.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is special about delay-critical GBR 5QIs compared with normal GBR?
A: They carry an MDBV and very short PDBs (5–30 ms); a packet delayed beyond the PDB counts as lost if the burst was within MDBV and GFBR. The RAN must dimension for bursts, not just average rate.
Q: Which 5QIs are used for MCPTT?
A: 5QI 65 (GBR, priority 7, 75 ms, 10^-2) for the push-to-talk voice user plane and 5QI 69 (non-GBR, priority 5, 60 ms, 10^-6) for its signalling. 5QI 67 carries MC video, 5QI 70 MC data.
Q: What is the averaging window?
A: The time over which GFBR and MFBR are evaluated for a GBR flow, 2000 ms by default for standardized GBR 5QIs. It lets the scheduler meet the rate on average rather than per slot.
Q: 5QI priority vs ARP — which decides what?
A: 5QI priority level guides scheduling order when resources are contended; ARP decides whether a flow is admitted and whether it can pre-empt or be pre-empted. A flow can have high ARP but a moderate 5QI priority.
Q: How would you verify a mission-critical flow in the lab?
A: Check NGAP QoS parameters and RRC DRB/LCP config, then load the cell with best-effort traffic and measure per-5QI latency, throughput vs GFBR and loss; also test admission/pre-emption by exceeding capacity.

Level 2 — building the KPIs (TS 28.554 style) with a worked example
Accessibility is a product of legs, because a UE must pass all of them:
Access SR = RRC setup SR × NG signalling setup SR × PDU session (or QoS flow) setup SR
Retainability in 28.554 is time-normalised: abnormally released active QoS flows / total QoS-flow session time (drops per hour). Many operators also report a percentage drop rate = abnormal / (normal + abnormal) releases — know which one a report uses.
Mobility: HO SR = HO executions successful / HO preparations attempted (or split into preparation SR × execution SR).
Integrity: UE throughput = volume / active transmission time, excluding the last slot of each burst so small tails do not dilute the rate. Latency from DRB delay counters.
Utilisation: mean PRB usage = RRU.PrbUsed / RRU.PrbAvail.

How to verify counters in the lab:
  • Controlled test: with a UE simulator, drive exactly N attaches, M handovers, K forced drops (e.g. cut RF), then compare PM deltas with the trace. Every mismatch is a bug or a definition misunderstanding.
  • Negative cases: RACH failure, RRC Reject, Msg5 missing, PDU session reject from core, HO preparation failure, HO timer expiry — check each lands in the right counter and cause.
  • Check the trigger point: "attempt" on RRC Setup Request vs on RRC Setup sent changes the SR when Msg3/Msg4 fail.
  • Check granularity and timestamps (15-min boundaries, time zone, missed periods after a restart) and that PM files are valid against the XML schema (TS 32.435 / 28.550 file format).
Level 3 — traps an expert looks for
  • Split gNB: in CU/DU deployments some counters live in CU-CP (RRC, NGAP, HO), some in DU (PRB, MAC, CQI), some in CU-UP (PDCP volume, delay). Aggregating per cell needs consistent cell IDs across nodes; double counting across CU-CP and DU is common.
  • Repeated attempts: a UE retrying RRC setup 3 times inflates attempts; some KPI definitions de-duplicate by UE within a window.
  • Re-establishment: a successful re-establishment may or may not count as a drop depending on definition — clarify before comparing vendors.
  • Throughput dilution: dividing total volume by the full 15 minutes gives cell throughput, not user experience; 28.552 UE throughput uses active time only.
  • Inter-system: 5G→4G redirection/handover (NS31) must be counted as a mobility event, not as a drop. Missing this makes retainability look bad after a coverage-driven LTE fallback.
  • Small denominators: a 50% HO SR from 2 attempts is noise. Always report attempts alongside rates.
Interview Q&A
Q: Which 3GPP specs define 5G counters and KPIs?
A: TS 28.552 defines the NR/5GC performance measurements (counters); TS 28.554 defines the end-to-end KPIs built from them; TS 28.550/32.435 cover PM collection and file format.
Q: How is 5G accessibility usually computed?
A: As a product of stage success rates: RRC connection setup SR × UE-associated NG signalling SR × PDU session / QoS flow setup SR.
Q: What does 28.554 retainability measure?
A: Abnormal releases of active QoS flows normalised by session time (drops per unit time), rather than a simple drop percentage.
Q: Why exclude the last slot when calculating UE throughput?
A: The last slot of a burst is often partly filled; including it would lower the measured rate for small bursts and not reflect the rate the user experienced while data was flowing.
Q: How do you validate a new counter?
A: Generate a known number of events with a UE simulator or test UEs (including failure cases), then compare PM deltas with signalling traces for the same period.
NS35 gNB Scale & Resilience — multi-cell DU, 512+ connected UEs, split 2 vs 7.2x, failure recovery 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
A modern gNB is software spread over several boxes (or containers): a CU that handles signalling and encryption, a DU that runs the fast scheduler, and an RU at the antenna. Two questions matter in the field: how big can it get (how many cells, how many connected phones before something runs out) and what happens when a piece fails. Like a restaurant: seats (memory), waiters (scheduler CPU) and the order window (PDCCH) each cap how many customers you can serve, and you need a plan for when the chef goes home sick. This card stays generic — no particular vendor platform. See CM08 for split 7.2 hardware and NS24 for AMF redundancy.

Level 2 — splits and failure recovery, and how to test them
Split options (TR 38.801): 3GPP standardized option 2 — F1 between CU (RRC/PDCP/SDAP) and DU (RLC/MAC/PHY) — and E1 between CU-CP and CU-UP. O-RAN standardized 7.2x (low PHY — FFT/iFFT, CP, possibly precoding — in the RU). Option 2 tolerates ms-level transport and lets one CU pool many DUs; 7.2x needs tight, synchronised fronthaul (eCPRI, PTP) but keeps RUs simple and multi-vendor. Option 6 (MAC/PHY, nFAPI) is used in some small cells; option 8 is classic CPRI.

Test ideas:
  • Capacity soak: ramp a UE emulator to the target connected UEs (e.g. 512, 1024) with a realistic traffic mix; record CPU per core, slot-deadline misses, PDCCH blocking rate, PUCCH/SRS resource exhaustion, RRC.ConnMax.
  • Attach storm: power-cycle the cell with all UEs camped; measure time to re-register all UEs and RACH/RRC failure counters during the storm.
  • Kill tests: kill DU, CU-CP, CU-UP processes/pods one at a time; time to cell on-air, time to first successful attach, UE recovery rate; confirm correct alarms and that PM counters are not lost or double-counted around the restart.
  • Transport faults: add delay/loss on F1 and fronthaul; find the point where F1 SCTP heartbeats fail or late U-plane packets are dropped by the RU (on-time/late counters).
Level 3 — cloud-native specifics and pitfalls
  • Stateless vs stateful: container orchestration restarts pods quickly, but UE contexts live in memory. Unless state is checkpointed or replicated to a standby, a restart is a cell-wide drop. Hot standby with state sync trades memory/network for recovery time.
  • Real-time constraints: the DU L1/L2 needs pinned, isolated cores, huge pages, a real-time kernel and SR-IOV/DPDK NICs; noisy neighbours or a CPU frequency change show up as sporadic slot deadline misses and BLER spikes rather than obvious errors (see CM29).
  • Liveness vs readiness: a DU that is "alive" but has lost PTP lock must not stay on air; health checks must include sync state and fronthaul status, not just process presence.
  • Scaling out is not free: adding CU-UP instances needs E1 load balancing; adding cells to a DU may need more PUCCH/PRACH planning and re-balancing of L1 accelerator queues.
  • SCTP: use multi-homing for F1/NG/E1 where supported; single-path SCTP with default timers can take tens of seconds to declare a path dead.
Interview Q&A
Q: What usually limits the number of connected UEs in a cell before memory does?
A: Control-channel resources: PDCCH CCEs for DCIs and PUCCH/SRS resources for SR, CSI and HARQ. Memory and CPU matter too, but PDCCH/PUCCH run out first for active UEs.
Q: Why is split option 2 easier on transport than 7.2x?
A: F1 carries PDCP PDUs and signalling, so bandwidth scales with user traffic and latency tolerance is in milliseconds. 7.2x carries frequency-domain IQ per antenna/layer, needs PTP sync and roughly 100 µs class latency.
Q: What happens to UEs when a DU restarts?
A: The cell goes off air; UEs declare RLF, re-establishment fails because the context is gone, and they perform new RRC setup / registration when the cell returns — an attach storm to plan for.
Q: Which procedures bring a restarted CU back into service?
A: F1 Setup (DU→CU), E1 Setup between CU-CP and CU-UP, and NG Setup towards the AMF; resets (F1/E1/NG Reset) clear stale UE associations.
Q: How would you test gNB resilience in a lab?
A: Load it with a UE emulator, then kill each component in turn and measure time to on-air, UE recovery rate, alarms and counter integrity; also inject transport delay/loss on F1 and fronthaul.
NS36 NTN Basics for RAN Engineers — large delay, TA pre-compensation, Doppler, K_offset, HARQ disabling (Rel-17) 5G SA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN) put the cell on a satellite (or high-altitude platform). Standard NR assumes a tower a few km away; a satellite is 600 km (LEO) to 36 000 km (GEO) away and a LEO satellite races past at about 7.5 km/s. That breaks three NR assumptions: timing advance is huge, Doppler shift is huge, and HARQ acknowledgements arrive far too late. Rel-17 fixes these mostly by making the UE smarter — it knows its own GNSS position and the satellite's orbit, so it pre-corrects its own timing and frequency. Even if you never test a satellite, the same ideas appear in very large terrestrial cells and in lab tests with channel emulators adding big delays. NS22 covers normal timing advance.

Level 2 — the Rel-17 mechanisms and how to test them
  • SIB19 carries NTN configuration: satellite ephemeris (position/velocity or orbital elements), epoch time and validity duration, common TA parameters, Koffset, and reference location / distance thresholds for mobility.
  • TA pre-compensation: the UE applies TTA = (NTA + NTA,UE-specific + NTA,common + NTA,offset)·Tc. The UE-specific part comes from its own GNSS position and the ephemeris (service link); the common part is broadcast (feeder-link delay up to a reference point). The UE keeps updating it autonomously as the satellite moves. GNSS capability is therefore mandatory for Rel-17 NR-NTN UEs.
  • Frequency pre-compensation: the UE pre-corrects UL frequency for the service-link Doppler; the network handles the feeder link.
  • Scheduling offsets: Koffset is added to timing relationships (PUSCH after DCI (K2), HARQ-ACK timing, RAR grant, CSI reference) so the UE is not asked to transmit before it could possibly receive the grant; kmac covers MAC CE action timing when the DL/UL frame alignment differs at the gNB.
  • HARQ: up to 32 HARQ processes (from 16) and HARQ feedback disabling per DL process (plus an UL mode where the gNB need not wait for retransmission timing). With feedback disabled, reliability relies on blind repetitions and RLC ARQ.
  • Timers: RA response window, contention resolution timer, and several MAC/RLC/PDCP timers get offsets or longer ranges to cover the RTT.
  • Mobility: conditional handover with location-based (distance to reference, condEvent D1) and time-based triggers, because RSRP differences between beams are small at a satellite.
Test ideas (terrestrial lab): use a channel emulator with large static delay plus time-varying delay and Doppler profiles; verify SIB19 decoding, UE TA/frequency pre-compensation (check the gNB-measured residual TA stays small), RACH success rate vs delay, throughput with HARQ feedback enabled vs disabled, and RLF/timer behaviour when the ephemeris validity expires.
Level 3 — why HARQ breaks, and NTN-like terrestrial configs
HARQ stall math: stop-and-wait HARQ needs enough processes to cover the round trip. At 15 kHz (1 ms slots) a 26 ms LEO RTT needs ~26+ processes to keep the pipe full; with 16 the link is idle over a third of the time. At GEO (~541 ms) even 32 processes give only a few percent duty cycle — hence feedback disabling.
Large terrestrial cells meet milder versions of the same problems: PRACH format 0 (long sequence, 1.25 kHz SCS) supports roughly 14–15 km, formats 1 and 2 around 100 km (NS29/NS07); TA range and GP (NS32) must match. Air-to-ground or maritime links behave like "slow NTN" — large TA, higher Doppler — and use the same test tooling.
  • Ephemeris accuracy: stale or wrong ephemeris causes UL timing errors the gNB sees as large TA commands or failed Msg3 decoding. Always check epoch/validity when UL fails only after a while.
  • Feeder-link switches (gateway change) shift the common TA and can look like a mass timing jump for every UE in the cell.
  • IoT-NTN (NB-IoT/LTE-M over satellite) was specified in parallel in Rel-17 with the same principles; Rel-18 adds coverage enhancements and NR-NTN above 10 GHz.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why can't a normal NR UE work over a LEO satellite?
A: The RTT (~26 ms) exceeds the TA range and timing assumptions, Doppler (tens of kHz) exceeds what the receiver tracks, and 16 HARQ processes cannot cover the round trip. Rel-17 adds TA/frequency pre-compensation, K_offset, more HARQ processes and HARQ feedback disabling.
Q: How does the UE know how much TA to apply before RACH?
A: It computes the service-link delay from its GNSS position and the satellite ephemeris in SIB19, and adds the broadcast common TA (feeder link). The gNB then only corrects a small residual.
Q: What is K_offset?
A: A cell-specific (and optionally UE-specific) extra slot offset added to scheduling timings such as K2 and HARQ-ACK timing, so the UE is never scheduled to transmit before it could have received the command over the long link.
Q: Why disable HARQ feedback in NTN?
A: With RTT of tens to hundreds of ms, stop-and-wait HARQ would leave the link idle; disabling feedback lets the gNB keep sending new data while reliability is handled by repetitions and RLC ARQ.
Q: Transparent vs regenerative payload?
A: Transparent: the satellite relays the signal and the gNB is on the ground, so the feeder link adds delay. Regenerative: the gNB (or part of it) is on the satellite, shortening the HARQ loop. Rel-17 NR-NTN covers transparent.
