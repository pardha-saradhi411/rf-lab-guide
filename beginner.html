M01 The Decibel & RF Power — dB, dBm, dBc, dBFS, link budget Beginner click to expand▾

dB, dBm, dBc, dBFS — the language of RF

Why RF uses logarithmic scale (dB):
RF signals span 13 orders of magnitude in power — from a 20W PA (+43 dBm) to a sensitivity threshold of −99 dBm. That is a ratio of 10^14. Working in linear watts is impossible. dB compresses this: every ×10 power = +10 dB, every ×2 = +3 dB. Adding gains in dB = multiplying in linear. Chain gain = sum of all block gains in dB.
dBm, dBc, dBFS — reference points:
dBm = power relative to 1 mW. 0 dBm = 1 mW. +30 dBm = 1W. −99 dBm = your RX sensitivity.
dBc = power relative to carrier. Used for ACLR (adjacent channel power vs carrier) and spurious. ACLR limit −45 dBc means adjacent channel must be 45 dB below the wanted signal.
dBFS = power relative to ADC full scale. 0 dBFS = ADC clipping. −12 dBFS = typical operating point. Converted to dBm via ADC_ADJUST in hardware.json.
Quick mental maths — dB rules to memorise:
+3 dB = ×2 power · −3 dB = ÷2 · +10 dB = ×10 · −10 dB = ÷10
+6 dB = ×4 · +20 dB = ×100 · +30 dB = ×1000
0 dBm = 1 mW · +10 dBm = 10 mW · +30 dBm = 1 W · +43 dBm = 20 W
−30 dBm = 1 μW · −60 dBm = 1 nW · −99 dBm ≈ your sensitivity floor
Why RF lives in logarithms
A base station may transmit 40 W; the signal it receives from a phone at the cell edge can be around 10⁻¹³ W — a ratio of ~100,000,000,000,000 to 1. You cannot reason about that range in linear watts. RF uses a logarithmic scale, the decibel, which compresses that range and turns multiplication into addition.
dB — the decibel is a RATIO
dB = 10 × log₁₀(P_out / P_in)

Build everything else by combining: +6 dB = ×4 · +13 dB = ×20 · +20 dB = ×100 · +30 dB = ×1000
dBm — absolute power (referenced to 1 mW)
dBm = 10 × log₁₀(Power_mW / 1 mW)

Practical questions you will face
Q: Your EXA shows -18.7 dBm. Cable loss is 2.0 dB. What is the power at the SMA?
A: -18.7 + 2.0 = -16.7 dBm. The cable ate 2 dB. Set ExtGain = +2.0 dB on EXA and it shows -16.7 dBm directly.
Q: B3 rated power is -15.7 dBm. B1 is -16.7 dBm. Why is B3 stronger?
A: B3 (1842 MHz) band combiner has less insertion loss than B1 (2140 MHz) because loss increases with frequency in passive components. So B3 arrives at the SMA with less cable/combiner loss, appearing stronger.
Q: ACLR measures -55 dBc. What does that mean in watts if carrier is -16.7 dBm?
A: Carrier: -16.7 dBm = 21.4 μW. Adjacent channel leakage: -16.7 - 55 = -71.7 dBm = 0.068 nW. The adjacent channel gets only 1/355,000 of the wanted power. That's a good ACLR.
Q: Someone says 'I need 3 dB more power'. What does that mean?
A: They need to double the output power. 3 dB = factor of 2. So if current power is 20W, they need 40W.
M02 The 50-Ohm World — impedance, reflection, VSWR, return loss Beginner click to expand▾

50 Ω impedance — why it matters for every connector and cable

Why 50 Ω is the RF standard:
75 Ω minimises cable loss (optimal for coax). 33 Ω maximises power transfer. 50 Ω is the engineering compromise — acceptable loss, acceptable power handling, rugged connectors. The entire RF industry standardised on 50 Ω so equipment from different manufacturers connects without impedance mismatch.
What happens when impedance is not matched (VSWR):
When source and load impedances differ, part of the signal reflects back. VSWR (Voltage Standing Wave Ratio) measures this. VSWR = 1.0 = perfect match, zero reflection. VSWR = 2.0 = 11% power reflected. A poorly torqued SMA connector can have VSWR > 2.0, meaning you lose 11% of your signal before it even enters the RU. In your sensitivity tests, a VSWR of 2.0 at the cable-to-RU connection costs you about 0.5 dB sensitivity.
Return loss — how VNA shows mismatch:
Return Loss (dB) = −20·log(reflection coefficient Γ). RL = 20 dB means Γ = 0.1 = 1% power reflected = excellent. RL = 10 dB means Γ = 0.316 = 10% reflected = poor. Your VNA S11 measurement shows return loss. Target: S11 < −20 dB (RL > 20 dB) for all cables and connectors in your test setup.
Why 50 ohms?
At RF, a cable is a transmission line with characteristic impedance Z₀ set by its geometry, not its length. The industry standardised on 50Ω: coax handles maximum power near 30Ω and minimum loss near 77Ω — 50Ω is the compromise. Rule: keep the entire path 50Ω end to end.
Reflection coefficient (Gamma)
Γ = (ZL − Z0) / (ZL + Z0) Matched (ZL=50Ω): Γ = 0 Open (ZL=∞): Γ = +1 Short (ZL=0): Γ = −1
Return loss and VSWR — the two numbers you live by
Return Loss (dB) = −20 × log₁₀|Γ| ← bigger is better VSWR = (1 + |Γ|) / (1 − |Γ|) ← near 1 is better

Keying a PA into an open port (Γ = +1, 100% reflected) returns all power into the PA — can permanently damage it. This is why VSWR-trip protection exists and why you NEVER connect a PA to an unterminated port.
VNA calibration standards: Load = perfect 50Ω (Γ=0), Open/Short = perfect reflectors (Γ=±1). The SOLT cal removes systematic errors so what you measure is the DUT, not your cables.

Why 50Ω — the complete history and physics
A coaxial cable has two competing requirements:
• Maximum power transfer needs Z ≈ 30Ω (outer/inner diameter ratio = 1.5)
• Minimum attenuation needs Z ≈ 77Ω (outer/inner ratio = 3.6)
• Maximum voltage breakdown needs Z ≈ 60Ω
• 50Ω = geometric mean of 30 and 77 = compromise that's 'good enough' for all three.

TV systems use 75Ω (minimize loss in long cable runs). Radar uses 50Ω (maximize power). Your EXA, EXM, VNA, all cables, all connectors, all RU ports = 50Ω.

Transmission line effects — when does it matter?
A lumped circuit model works when length ≪ λ/10.
At 2.14GHz (B1 DL): λ = c/f = 3×10⁸/2.14×10⁹ = 14cm. λ/10 = 1.4cm
At 758MHz (B28 DL): λ = 39.6cm. λ/10 = 3.96cm
Rule: Any cable, trace, or connector longer than λ/10 must be treated as a transmission line.
In your lab: ALL cables are transmission lines. A 1m cable at 2GHz = 6.7λ long!

Consequence: Impedance seen at one end depends on length AND frequency. A shorted stub of exactly λ/4 looks like an OPEN CIRCUIT. Used deliberately in RF filters.
VSWR measurement — what to do in your lab
When to measure: When channel power is lower than expected, sensitivity is worse, ACLR is high
How: VNA → S11 measurement → log magnitude display
Your target: Return loss > 20dB (VSWR < 1.2) for all cables in signal path
Common culprits: Bent SMA pin, overtightened connector (>15 in-lb), corrosion, wrong connector type

Real lab example: Unit 26050509 — B1 power deficit traced to cable with VSWR 2.5 (12dB return loss). Replacing cable restored 1.8dB of missing power.
Smith chart — read it without fear
Smith chart maps complex impedance Z = R + jX onto a circle.
• Center: 50+j0Ω = perfect match = VSWR 1:1
• Right edge: Open circuit (Z=∞)
• Left edge: Short circuit (Z=0)
• Upper half: Inductive (positive jX)
• Lower half: Capacitive (negative jX)

Your VNA shows Smith chart in Cal Recall menu. After SOLT calibration, a good 50Ω load should land exactly at center.
Interview Q&A
Q: Your cable shows VSWR of 2.0. How much power is reflected?
A: VSWR=2.0 → Γ=0.33 → Reflected fraction = |Γ|² = 0.11 = 11%. For B1 at -16.7dBm, reflected power = -16.7 - 9.5 = -26.2dBm.
Q: Why does a λ/4 open stub act as a short circuit?
A: At the open end, voltage is maximum, current is zero. After travelling λ/4, the wave has shifted 90° in phase. At the input, current is maximum and voltage is zero — that's a short circuit. This is used in bandstop filters and bias tees.
Q: Why do you always check cable connections before blaming the DUT?
A: A bad connection adds return loss that looks like reduced DUT output. 1dB of extra cable loss = 1dB deficit in channel power. Always VSWR-check cables first, then troubleshoot the DUT.
M03 The RF Signal Chain — how TX and RX work: every stage from baseband to antenna Beginner–Intermediate click to expand▾

RF chain gain cascade — how gains and losses combine

Gain cascade in dB — just add:
The beauty of dB: total chain gain = sum of all individual gains and losses. Example DL chain: DAC output 0 dBm → PA gain +43 dB → duplexer loss −0.5 dB → coupler loss −30 dB → BOB SMA = +12.5 dBm. For UL: antenna receives −97.5 dBm → duplexer −0.5 dB → LNA +20 dB → mixer −7 dB → ADC input = +15 dBm... wait, that cannot be right — AGC reduces gain to keep ADC safe. Real chain includes AGC which maintains ADC at −12 dBFS regardless of input.
Noise Figure in a cascade — Friis:
Unlike gain, noise figures do NOT simply add. First stage NF dominates. NF_total = NF1 + (NF2−1)/G1 + (NF3−1)/(G1×G2) + ... This is why LNA is first active component and why cable before LNA directly adds to NF. See NCH card for full numerical calculation showing your 5.75 dB system NF.
P1dB — compression point:
At high input powers, the PA gain compresses — output power no longer tracks input linearly. P1dB is the input power where gain drops by 1 dB from its small-signal value. Operating near P1dB causes ACLR failures. Your PA is backed off from P1dB to operate in its linear region — this is why DPD is needed, it allows operating closer to P1dB while maintaining linearity.

Why two power levels exist — BOB vs Antenna
The BOB SMA (Ball On Board test point) gives access to the pre-PA signal. This is where you connect the EXA for all RCT measurements.

BOB level B1: -16.7 dBm = the digital power set by BBU at the FHGW input
Antenna level B1: ~+36.5 dBm = after the GaN PA amplifies by ~53dB

Critical: The RCT tests ACLR, EVM, Channel Power at the BOB SMA level. DPD correction already happened in baseband before the DAC. So if ACLR is bad at the BOB, it's a DPD/CFR problem, not a PA problem.
RX path — where sensitivity is determined
UE transmits → Antenna → Duplexer (TX/RX isolation) → LNA (first amp, sets NF) → Down-conversion mixer → IF filter → ADC → FHGW → eCPRI → BBU

The LNA is critical: It's the first active component in the RX path. Its noise figure determines the system noise figure (Friis formula). A 2dB NF LNA makes the whole system NF ~2.07dB.
A damaged LNA (NF=10dB) degrades system NF by ~8dB → 8dB worse sensitivity → 8dB less UL coverage.

Your sensitivity test: Injects known power into RU RX port via EXM SG. Reduces power until BLER hits 5%. That level = reference sensitivity.
CFR vs DPD — why you need both
Problem: LTE/OFDM signals have very high PAPR (peak-to-average power ratio = ~12dB). This means the PA must be large enough for peaks, but spends most time at low efficiency.

CFR (Crest Factor Reduction): Clips peaks to reduce PAPR from 12dB to ~8.3dB. This DOES add distortion, but only to peak samples (rare). Sets PAPR = 8.3 ± 1.0 dB. Check: PreChecks verify PAPR register.

DPD (Digital Pre-Distortion): The clipped signal still has some distortion + PA adds its own. DPD applies the mathematical INVERSE of PA's transfer function. PA input is already 'pre-distorted' so that PA output is clean.

If DPD not converged: ACLR degrades 10-20dB. Check: dpd-app-orx --debug → ALIGNED status → dpd_convolution_alignment = ALIGNED.

Interview Q&A
Q: Why does the RCT measure at BOB SMA instead of the antenna port?
A: Repeatable: cable loss is calibrated. Safe: antenna RF would damage EXA input. Practical: you can't access the RF space outside the unit. The BOB gives electrical access to the signal before PA.
Q: DPD is showing MISALIGNED. What do you do?
A: 1. Check observation path cable (OBS port on RFFE → ADC). 2. Check CFR register (0x534020004 = 0xd7). 3. Run dpd-app-orx --debug to see alignment status. 4. If RFFE issue, may need JTAG re-flash (like the RFFE2 LB fault you solved).
Q: What is the 9.9765ms delay for B41 TDD?
A: B41 is TDD — the same frequency is used for DL and UL in alternating time slots. The BBU frame starts at t=0. The DL slot starts at t=9.9765ms into the frame. The EXM trigger must be delayed by exactly 9.9765ms to capture the DL burst.
M04 Radio Performance Metrics — RSSI, RSRP, SINR, EIRP, TRP, Sensitivity explained Intermediate click to expand▾

Key radio metrics — RSSI, RSRP, RSRQ, SINR explained

RSSI vs RSRP vs RSRQ — what each measures:
RSSI (Received Signal Strength Indicator): total wideband power received — signal + interference + noise. Measured in dBm. Your CLI get_rssi reads this.
RSRP (Reference Signal Received Power): power of LTE reference signals (CRS) per resource element. More precise than RSSI. Used for handover decisions. Range: −44 to −140 dBm.
RSRQ (Reference Signal Received Quality): RSRP / RSSI × N_RB. Includes interference quality. Range: −3 to −19.5 dB.
SINR (Signal to Interference+Noise Ratio): signal power / (interference + noise). The quality metric the scheduler uses.
Dynamic range — why it matters for your tests:
Dynamic range = max receivable signal − sensitivity floor = ~0 dBm − (−99 dBm) = 99 dB. Your ADC has 74 dB of instantaneous dynamic range. The AGC extends this by adjusting gain — a close UE at −40 dBm and a far UE at −99 dBm both need to be handled cleanly. If AGC saturates, all RSSI measurements are wrong.
EVM — Error Vector Magnitude (TX quality metric):
EVM measures how far each received IQ symbol is from its ideal constellation point, as a percentage of the ideal signal amplitude. EVM = |error vector| / |reference vector| × 100%. Limits: QPSK < 17.5%, 16QAM < 12.5%, 64QAM < 8%, 256QAM < 3.5%. EVM failures caused by: phase noise (LO quality), IQ imbalance, nonlinearity (DPD failure), clipping (CFR too aggressive).
RSSI
Total power in the receive band at the radio input, in dBm. Wideband: it sums wanted signal + noise + interference. High RSSI alone does NOT mean good signal — it could be mostly interference. Used for coverage monitoring and AGC control.
Sensitivity
Sensitivity = −174 + 10×log₁₀(BW_Hz) + NF + required_SNR Example: BW=10 MHz → −174+70+NF4+SNR1 = −99 dBm
Minimum input power still meeting BLER ≤ 5% (RCT test 7.2). Set by the chain's noise figure. 3GPP Wide Area BS REFSENS limit: −69 dBm (you should easily achieve −97 to −100 dBm).

EIRP and TRP
EIRP = TX power (dBm) + Antenna gain (dBi) − Losses TRP = EIRP integrated over the full sphere
Your AAU beamforming gain: ~+12 dB for B1/B3/B41 (12-element arrays) · ~+6 dB for B8/B28 (6-element arrays). So per-PA output 38.4 dBm → TRP 44.4 dBm → with beamforming 50.4 dBm. Three different power numbers — don't confuse them.
The metric hierarchy — from raw to useful
RSSI = everything the antenna sees (signal + noise + interference). Broadest.
RSRP = power of ONE reference signal subcarrier. Signal quality, ignores interference.
RSRQ = RSRP/RSSI per RB. Tells you how much of RSSI is actually signal.
SINR = signal vs (noise + interference). Best predictor of throughput.

Think of it like listening in a noisy room:
• RSSI = total sound level (decibels from sound meter)
• RSRP = how loud the speaker you want is
• SINR = how clearly you hear them above the background noise

M05 Modulation & OFDM — IQ constellations, QPSK to 256QAM, resource grid, EVM Intermediate click to expand▾

IQ modulation, OFDM, constellations — how radio carries data

IQ — In-phase and Quadrature:
A single RF carrier can carry two independent data streams: I (in-phase, cosine) and Q (quadrature, sine). They are orthogonal — they do not interfere with each other. A QAM constellation is a grid of I/Q amplitude combinations. QPSK uses 4 points (±1, ±1 for I and Q). 16QAM uses 16 points. 64QAM uses 64 points. More points = more bits per symbol = higher throughput = but needs higher SNR to distinguish points.
OFDM — how LTE uses it:
OFDM (Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing) divides the channel into many narrow subcarriers (15 kHz each in LTE). Each subcarrier carries its own QAM symbol. 1 Resource Block = 12 subcarriers × 180 kHz. 10 MHz channel = 50 RBs = 600 subcarriers. OFDM is robust against multipath because the cyclic prefix (4.69 μs) is longer than most delay spreads. The IFFT in the FHGW converts your frequency-domain subcarrier assignments into the time-domain OFDM signal.
IQ imbalance — what goes wrong:
If the I and Q paths have amplitude or phase mismatch, the constellation rotates and the points blur. Amplitude imbalance: all points shift toward I or Q axis. Phase imbalance: constellation rotates. Both increase EVM and cause ACLR to worsen. In your RU: IQ imbalance manifests as asymmetric constellation in the EXA modulation analysis view — left/right clusters at different sizes, or constellation tilted off axis.
IQ — why two numbers?
A signal has both amplitude and phase. The clean way to carry both is two numbers: I (in-phase) and Q (quadrature, 90° offset). Any modulated signal is I + jQ — a point on a 2D plane. The DAC, ADC, and all digital processing work in IQ.

OFDM and the resource grid
LTE/5G split the channel into many narrow 15 kHz subcarriers, each a low-rate QAM symbol. One subcarrier × one symbol = one Resource Element (RE). 12 subcarriers × 7 symbols = one Resource Block (RB). The test "prb: 273" means 273 RBs are allocated (= 100 MHz 5G NR channel).
EVM — test 6.5.2
Error Vector Magnitude = distance from where a symbol SHOULD be to where it LANDED, as % of ideal magnitude. A PA driven too hard (Module 6) raises EVM. The EXM measures this via the modulation analysis mode (sa_set_modulation_analysis_mode).
IQ playback — how Rx tests work
Receiver tests need a known, repeatable uplink signal. A waveform is pre-computed as an IQ file (.bin / .wfm) and a signal generator plays it into the radio. In RuAuto: PRACH_idx158_100MHZ.wfm for PRACH tests, PUCCH format waveforms for PUCCH tests. The EXM does double duty — generator for Rx tests, analyzer for Tx tests.

A perfect constellation = neat, crisp handwriting — every letter exactly where it should be. Phase noise = someone writing on a vibrating bus — the letters are in the right place but wobbly. IQ imbalance = writing with the paper tilted — everything is skewed in one direction. PA compression = writing with a blunt pencil — all the letters are squished together toward the centre. The EXA shows you the handwriting and you diagnose what is wrong with the pen.

Why OFDM beats single-carrier in multipath
Multipath: signal bounces off walls/buildings, arrives at multiple times. With single-carrier at 150 Mbps, symbol duration = 6.7ns. A 500ns delay spread means 75 symbols of interference — devastating.

With OFDM at 15kHz subcarrier spacing: symbol = 66.7μs. 500ns delay spread = 0.75% of one symbol. Cyclic prefix (5.21μs) absorbs it completely — no ISI.

Trade-off: OFDM needs very accurate frequency synchronisation. Frequency offset > subcarrier_spacing/2 = inter-carrier interference (ICI). This is why the 0.05ppm frequency error spec is so tight.

Interview Q&A
Q: What is the cyclic prefix and why is 5.21μs chosen?
A: CP is a copy of the end of each OFDM symbol prepended to the start. It absorbs multipath delay spread. 5.21μs was chosen to handle typical urban multipath (500ns to 5μs delay spread) with margin. Rural cells with longer paths use extended CP (16.67μs).
Q: Your EVM test shows much higher EVM on outer subcarriers than center. What's wrong?
A: Frequency-dependent gain/phase error across the channel bandwidth. Common causes: cable resonance, filter roll-off at band edges, equalizer not covering full BW. Check: EXA → Modulation Analysis → Subcarrier EVM display. Shows EVM per subcarrier frequency.
Q: Why does 64QAM need better SINR than QPSK?
A: 64QAM has 64 symbols in the same IQ plane, minimum distance = 0.155 (normalized). QPSK has 4 symbols, minimum distance = 0.707. For the same noise power, 64QAM has 13dB less margin before symbols overlap and errors occur. SINR must be 13dB+ higher for 64QAM to work.
Deep Reference Notes
OFDM orthogonality: subcarriers spaced exactly 1/Tu (15 kHz in LTE) so each sits at the nulls of its neighbours — TX uses an IFFT, RX an FFT. Cyclic prefix copies the symbol tail to its head so multipath (delay spread) stays within the CP and causes no ISI: normal CP ≈ 4.7 µs, extended ≈ 16.7 µs (CP must exceed channel delay spread — see ETU 5 µs in PROP02). SC-FDMA (DFT-spread OFDM) is used on the uplink: a DFT precoder before the IFFT gives it single-carrier-like envelope → ~2–3 dB lower PAPR than OFDM → the UE PA runs more efficiently at cell edge. Grid: 1 RE = 1 subcarrier × 1 symbol; 1 RB = 12 subcarriers × 1 slot.

M06 Downlink TX Chain — CFR, DPD and the GaN PA: how the signal becomes clean RF power Intermediate click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRSTDL TX chain in one sentence: CFR clips the signal peaks so the PA does not have to handle massive spikes, then DPD pre-distorts the signal so the PA's natural nonlinearity is cancelled out — together they let you run the PA close to full power while still meeting the ACLR limit.

DL TX chain — CFR, DPD, power scaling, common failures

CFR — why PAPR must be reduced before PA:
OFDM signals have high PAPR (Peak to Average Power Ratio) — typically 10–12 dB. This means the PA must handle peaks 10–12 dB above the average power. To handle the peaks without clipping, the PA would need to be backed off by 10–12 dB from its P1dB — operating at only 1/10th to 1/100th of its capacity. CFR clips these rare high peaks and smooths them, reducing PAPR to ~8 dB. This lets the PA operate 3–4 dB closer to P1dB, dramatically improving efficiency.
DPD convergence — the 60-second rule:
DPD adapts its pre-distortion polynomial coefficients by comparing the PA output (via observation path) to the ideal signal. This adaptation takes 30–60 seconds after power-on. During convergence: ACLR may be 5–10 dB worse than final value, EVM is elevated, power may fluctuate ±0.5 dB. Always wait for DPD to converge before measuring ACLR, EVM, or doing TX gain calibration. Check DPD status register or simply wait 60 seconds after enabling TX.
Common TX failures and root causes:
ACLR fail: DPD not converged (wait 60s), DPD model mismatch after hardware change, PA operating beyond P1dB (check rated power), CFR not working.
EVM fail: IQ imbalance, LO phase noise, clipping from over-driven input, DPD coefficients diverged.
Power low: tx_calibration_eeprom.json missing (fault 12), wrong TX_GAIN register, PA bias wrong, damaged PA.
The real FHGW DL chain order
Digital 2CC → DL Power Scaling → Gain → CFR → Gain → DPD → Per-channel Gain → DAC
The power_monitor reads dBFS at taps along the way. DPD is ONE link in this chain — not a standalone box.
PA compression and P1dB
Amplifiers are linear only up to a point. P1dB marks where gain has dropped 1 dB from linear. Past P1dB: compression, distortion, spectral regrowth (= ACLR fails), constellation smearing (= EVM fails).
PAPR — why OFDM is hard on PAs
OFDM sums hundreds of subcarriers. When they momentarily align in phase, the instantaneous power peaks ~10 dB above average (PAPR ≈ 10 dB). To prevent clipping, the average must run far below P1dB — hurting efficiency. This is why power_monitor shows DL around −21 dBFS, leaving headroom for peaks.
CFR — Crest Factor Reduction
CFR deliberately clips signal peaks (then applies a filter so it doesn't splatter) to reduce PAPR before the PA. On your FHGW: register 0xb0290000, filter_length 511, threshold 8.3. Two working points: 0xD7 (occupancy > 50%) and 0x128 (occupancy ≤ 50%). Less PAPR = PA can run with less back-off = more efficient.
DPD — Digital Pre-Distortion
DPD pre-warps the signal with the mathematical inverse of the PA's nonlinearity. The PA then "undoes" the pre-distortion → the cascade behaves linearly even though the PA itself is nonlinear. A feedback (observation) path adapts the model. CFR + DPD together let you run the PA hot (efficient) while still passing ACLR/EVM conformance tests.
If DPD fails or is miscalibrated: sudden ACLR degradation of 10–20 dB. PreCheck: DPD Gain Register and DPD Input Power + PAPR must both pass before any RF test begins.

GaN PA deep dive — why it's non-linear and what you do about it
A GaN transistor in your PA behaves linearly only up to ~3dB below compression.
Above that, gain starts dropping AND phase starts shifting — AM-AM and AM-PM distortion.

AM-AM: High-amplitude signals get less gain than low-amplitude. Constellation symbols near edges get compressed inward. EVM degrades on 64QAM symbols.
AM-PM: Phase rotation depends on amplitude. Constellation gets a spiral shape. ACLR explodes because spectral regrowth.

DPD fixes both: Before the signal enters the PA, DPD applies the inverse transfer function. High-amplitude symbols are pre-emphasized so after PA compression they come out at the right level. Phase is pre-rotated so after PA AM-PM rotation, it ends up correct.

Q: Why does PAPR matter for PA efficiency?
A: PAPR = 8.3dB means the PA must be designed for peaks 8.3dB above average. But most of the time the signal is at average power where the PA is very inefficient (Class AB efficiency at back-off ≈ efficiency × 10^(-PAPR/10) reduction). Lower PAPR = more efficient = less heat = longer PA life.

M07 ACLR — Adjacent Channel Leakage Ratio: why it matters, how to measure, pass/fail Intermediate click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRST ACLR in one sentence: When you transmit, your signal should stay neatly inside your assigned frequency channel. ACLR measures how much leaks into the neighbouring channel. Think of it as "staying in your lane" — the 3GPP limit says your leakage must be 45 dB weaker than your main signal. Failure means your PA is nonlinear and DPD is not fixing it properly.

ACLR — Adjacent Channel Leakage Ratio measurement setup

What ACLR measures and the limit:
ACLR = power in adjacent channel / power in wanted channel, in dBc. 3GPP limit for LTE base station: −45 dBc for adjacent channel (offset = channel BW), −50 dBc for alternate channel (offset = 2× channel BW). Measured on EXA using the ACLR/ACP measurement mode. A value of −42 dBc = FAIL (limit is −45 dBc). This means the PA is leaking 3 dB too much power into adjacent spectrum.
EXA setup for ACLR measurement:
Center freq = band center (B1 = 2140 MHz). Span = 5× channel BW minimum. RBW = 100 kHz. Atten = 20–30 dB (preamp OFF — signal is strong). Input = BOB SMA + 30 dB pad. Trigger = frame trigger or free-run. Use EXA ACLR function (not manual integration). Important: EXA automatically integrates power in each channel using the correct LTE RRC filter — do not try to measure ACLR manually from the spectrum trace.
TDD gating for B41 ACLR:
B41 is TDD — the PA transmits only during DL subframes. EXA must be gated to measure only during TX active periods. Without gating, the ACLR measurement includes the noise floor during UL periods, making ACLR appear 5–10 dB better than it actually is. Set EXA gate delay = DL subframe start offset, gate length = DL subframe duration. Use the TDD frame structure from tc_cadence.json to find the correct timing.
ACLR — test 6.6.2
Adjacent Channel Leakage Ratio = wanted-channel power / adjacent-channel leakage power, in dBc. The direct measure of spectral regrowth from PA nonlinearity and DPD performance. In RuAuto: sa_set_ACP_mode. Higher dBc = better (less leakage).

Interview Q&A
Q: ACLR upper is −58 dBc but ACLR lower is −48 dBc. What could cause asymmetry?
A: Asymmetric ACLR = problem at one specific frequency offset, not general DPD failure. Common causes: 1) Partial blockage or resonance at that frequency. 2) Mixer spur at that offset. 3) Adjacent carrier from another band at that frequency. 4) Cable resonance. Check SEM for the asymmetric side.

M08 PRACH — Random Access: how a UE knocks on the cell door, ZC sequences, timing advance Advanced click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRSTPRACH in one sentence: When a phone wants to join the network, it shouts a special code (Zadoff-Chu preamble) — the base station must hear this code even when the phone is at the cell edge, which is exactly what your sensitivity test verifies.

PRACH — Zadoff-Chu preambles, detection threshold, timing advance

What PRACH is and when it is sent:
PRACH (Physical Random Access Channel) is the UE's first transmission when connecting to the network. The UE sends a PRACH preamble to request access. The gNB must detect this preamble even at very low power — this is your sensitivity test. PRACH uses Zadoff-Chu sequences (length 839 for long preamble, 139 for short) chosen for their perfect correlation properties — a Zadoff-Chu sequence correlated with itself gives a sharp peak, making it easy to detect.
PRACH detector — how detection works:
The RU receives the wideband UL signal and runs a correlator against each of the 64 possible Zadoff-Chu root sequences. If the correlation peak exceeds a threshold (noise floor × peak_multiplier α), a PRACH is declared detected. The threshold α trades off: low α = sensitive (detect weak UEs, but more false alarms), high α = robust (few false alarms, but misses weak UEs). Your DSA sensitivity test verifies that at the stated threshold power, the detection rate exceeds 99%.
Timing Advance — what PRACH timing measures:
When a PRACH is detected, the RU measures the arrival time of the correlation peak. The offset from the expected time = propagation delay from the UE. The gNB sends a Timing Advance command telling the UE to advance its transmission timing by this amount. This ensures UL signals from all UEs arrive at the RU synchronised to within the cyclic prefix window (4.69 μs). TA range: 0 to 667 μs = ~100 km cell radius.
What PRACH is for
PRACH (Physical Random Access Channel) is how a UE first reaches the network with no uplink timing. At power-on, or to re-sync, the UE sends a random-access preamble (a "signature"). The base station detects it and replies with a timing-advance (TA) command — telling the UE how early to transmit so data arrives at exactly the right time.
Preamble structure
Preamble = Cyclic Prefix (CP) + Zadoff-Chu (ZC) sequence + Guard interval
Preamble formats 0–3 differ in CP/sequence/guard lengths — for different cell radii. Format 4 is TDD-only (rides in the UpPTS field of the special subframe). FHGW limitation: only PRACH config index 4, Frame Config 2 / SSF 6.
Zadoff-Chu sequences
ZC sequences have constant amplitude (important — not all amplitude variation means signal content), a single sharp autocorrelation peak, and low cross-correlation. 64 preamble signatures are made from one root by choosing a cyclic shift. The shift granularity is set by Ncs (defines the Zero-Correlation Zone).

Allowable delay per UE = Ncs × FFT_size / 839. Bigger cells need larger allowable delay → more roots → more inter-root noise. This is a fundamental cell-size vs. capacity trade-off.
The PRACH detector (FlexRAN-style)
1. Remove CP → FFT → extract PRACH subcarriers 2. Multiply by conjugate of root ZC (correlation) 3. IFFT → power-delay profile 4. Noise estimator: Noise_est = mean(noise vector from correlation) 5. Peak multiplier: detected if peak / Noise_est exceeds threshold 6. Peak position → timing advance
In RuAuto — test_Rx_PRACH
EXM plays a PRACH IQ waveform (e.g. PRACH_idx158_100MHZ.wfm) into the RU UL port. The test sweeps input power down using binary search or sensitivity (stepped coarse→fine) to find the detection threshold against PRACH_KPI. Output: miss-detection %, false-alarm %, timing advance avg/std, SNR. FHGW KPI: base_dbm (e.g. −87 dBm) + DSA per-band offset.
PRACH complete deep dive — from math to your test
PRACH uses Zadoff-Chu (ZC) sequences — a mathematical sequence with perfect autocorrelation.
For root sequence u, length N_ZC=839:
x_u(n) = e^{-jπu·n(n+1)/839}, n = 0..838

Why ZC? |R(τ)| = 1 for any cyclic shift τ. This means the BS can detect PRACH preamble AND measure timing advance (TA) simultaneously. Different cyclic shifts = different preamble IDs. Up to 64 preambles from one root sequence.

PRACH timing advance — what ZCZ means
ZCZ = Zero Correlation Zone = number of cyclic shifts the BS can use without false detection.
ZCZ = 8 means 8 unique cyclic shifts available.
Each cyclic shift = different timing advance capability. More ZCZ = can detect more UEs with different distances.

Timing advance from PRACH:
TA = cyclic_shift × (1/BW) × 1000 μs
At 10MHz, 1 TA unit = 0.52μs = 156m round trip = 78m one-way
Max TA = 1282 units = 100km range (macro cell coverage limit)
B41 TDD PRACH — the timing challenge
In B41 TDD, PRACH can only be sent in UL slots.
Frame structure: 10ms, typical config has UL slots at positions 9.9765ms from frame start.
Your EXM must inject the PRACH waveform with EXACTLY 9.9765ms delay from the frame trigger.

If delay is wrong: PRACH arrives in DL slot or guard slot → RU ignores it → 100% miss detection → test FAILS
Check: In tc_cadence.json: "ext_trig_delay": 9.9765e-3
Interview Q&A
Q: PRACH test shows 100% miss detection at all power levels. What's wrong?
A: 100% miss at ALL power levels (including high power like -40 dBm) means the signal is never arriving in the right slot. For FDD: check waveform file matches the configured BW. For TDD B41: trigger delay wrong (should be 9.9765ms). Also check: RU UL path (LNA, cable). Run sensitivity test first — if that also fails, UL path is broken.
Q: Why does PRACH use root sequence 32 in your tests?
A: PID32 (Preamble Index/Root 32) is the configured root for your test setup. The ZC sequence root determines which 64 preambles are available. Root 32 is assigned to avoid collision with neighboring cells in production. In lab: any root works — 32 is standard in PW test setup.
Q: PRACH sensitivity is measured at −79 dBm. Limit is −82 dBm. What's the verdict?
A: FAIL. Sensitivity is WORSE (less negative = weaker signal) than the limit. The RU needed a stronger signal than specified to achieve <1% miss detection. Root cause: check LNA NF, cable loss, DSA register, OBS path. For certification, this is a hard fail.
