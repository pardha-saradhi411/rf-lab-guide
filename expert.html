M14 RX Receive Chain Deep Dive — ADC calibration, dBFS↔dBm, real dynamic range measurements Advanced click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRSTRSSI in one sentence: RSSI is the radio's report of how strong a signal it received — but the raw number is in ADC counts (dBFS), and you need a calibration constant (ADC_ADJUST) to convert it to the real-world power in dBm that you can compare to your EXM injection.

RSSI and receive chain — dBFS to dBm, ADC calibration, dynamic range

dBFS to dBm conversion — the complete path:
get_rssi N → returns value in dBFS (e.g. −12.5 dBFS). ADC_ADJUST stored in hardware.json per band per ADC channel. Conversion: P_dBm = dBFS + ADC_ADJUST. If ADC_ADJUST_B1_ADC0 = −85: P_dBm = −12.5 + (−85) = −97.5 dBm at RU antenna port. This matches EXM injected −95 dBm minus cable loss −2.5 dB = −97.5 dBm. Agreement within ±1 dB = calibration good.

RSSI failures — diagnosis by which number is wrong:
All three (EXM, CLI, DU) off → EXM not calibrated, or cable loss not subtracted.
CLI wrong, DU correct → ADC_ADJUST wrong for that channel.
CLI correct, DU wrong → FHGW processing chain issue.
One column CLI wrong, others correct → hardware fault on that column (LNA, cable, connector).
All columns drift same direction as unit warms → temperature compensation not configured.
The real RSSI calibration method
Inject a CW tone at band centre at 3 known power levels (e.g. −40.5, −60.5, −70.5 dBm). Read RSSI at each. Fit an approximate straight line. Compute the average offset. Write that offset per-ADC into /etc/configs/hardware.json (the ADC_ADJUST vector — one entry per ADC 0–7). Then automation sweeps the input over [−40 dBm : 1 dB : −120 dBm] and records corrected RSSI.
Band-to-ADC mapping (real hardware)
B1 uses ADCs 0, 1, 4, 5. B3 uses ADCs 2, 3, 6, 7. Each ADC is calibrated separately with its own ADC_ADJUST offset. This is why get_rssi returns 8 values — one per ADC, and which one matters depends on your band and flavor.

PLAIN ENGLISH FIRSTUL RCT in one sentence: The uplink RCT test injects a known signal from the EXM into the RU receive port and checks that the RU correctly reports the signal power — if it reports wrong, something is wrong with the receive chain or calibration.

UL RCT test — ul-rct-test pipeline, 4-stage process, what it verifies

What the UL embedded RCT tests:
The UL RCT (uplink Radio Conformance Test) verifies that the RU's receive chain is performing correctly end-to-end — from RF input at the antenna port to decoded data at the DU. It uses EXM to inject a known uplink signal and measures: (1) PRACH detection rate (sensitivity), (2) RSSI accuracy (calibration), (3) SINR under various conditions, (4) multi-path robustness (EPA/ETU if PropSim connected).
4-stage UL test pipeline:
Stage 1 — Sync: RU acquires timing from DU. GPS/PTP lock verified for TDD.
Stage 2 — RSSI calibration: EXM injects known power, CLI and DU readings verified vs EXM.
Stage 3 — Sensitivity: EXM injects at DSA-corrected threshold per band/BW/FRC. PRACH detection rate measured. Must exceed 99% in 100 attempts.
Stage 4 — Dynamic range: EXM sweeps from −60 to −110 dBm. Verifies RSSI tracks linearly across the dynamic range window.
ul-rct-test command and parameters:
ul-rct-test --band B1 --bw 10M --frc A1 --iterations 100 --dsafile tc_cadence.json
Key parameters: band (B1/B3/B8/B28/B41), BW (5M/10M/15M/20M), FRC (A1/A2/A3/A5), iterations (100 minimum for statistical validity), dsafile (points to your calibrated DSA table). Results saved to rct_results_YYYYMMDD.json with per-column pass/fail, RSSI error, and detection count.
What UL RCT embedded is
Validates the entire uplink receive path — antenna to BLER — against 3GPP FRCs. "Embedded" means it runs entirely on the FHGW: no DU, no external test PC, one CLI command. A signal generator still injects the FRC waveform; everything else is on-RU. Catches receive-chain regressions at box level, fast, before DU integration.

M16 TDD Frame Structure & PHY — LTE/NR slot layout, HARQ timing, how BBU timing affects RCT Advanced click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRSTTDD in one sentence: Band 41 cannot transmit and receive at the same time — it takes turns, transmitting in some time slots and receiving in others, which means your EXA measurements must be gated to only the transmit slots, otherwise the results will be wrong.

TDD frame structure — special subframes, DL/UL ratio, B41 timing

TDD vs FDD — the fundamental difference:
FDD (B1/B3/B8/B28): separate frequencies for DL and UL. They can transmit simultaneously. Duplexer separates them at the antenna. TDD (B41): DL and UL share the same frequency, separated in time. A T/R switch connects either the PA (during DL) or LNA (during UL) to the antenna. PA and LNA never operate simultaneously — no duplexer needed. This is why B41 has better NF (2.0 dB) — the T/R switch has lower loss than a duplexer.
TDD frame structure — subframe types:
LTE TDD uses 10ms radio frames divided into 10 subframes (1ms each). Subframe types: D=Downlink, U=Uplink, S=Special. Special subframe has 3 parts: DwPTS (DL pilot), GP (guard period for switching), UpPTS (UL pilot). Common config 2: DSUDD DSUDD = repeating pattern. Config 5: DSUUU DSUUU = more UL. Your B41 config is set in the DU — check which TDD config is active before EXA gating setup.
Timing impact on measurements:
B41 ACLR must be measured with EXA gating on DL subframes only. PRACH arrives in UL subframes only. The guard period (GP) must be large enough for signal propagation delay plus switching transient — typically 3–14 OFDM symbols. PTP synchronisation accuracy must be ±1.5 μs for TDD — a drift > 1.5 μs causes DL/UL collision (TX and RX active simultaneously) which can damage the T/R switch and the LNA.
TDD vs FDD — the key difference
FDD (B1/B3/B8/B28): separate paired UL/DL frequencies, transmit and receive simultaneously. TDD (B41): UL and DL share the same frequency, alternating in time slots. TDD works in unpaired spectrum. The price: more complex scheduling, more stringent timing requirements.
TDD frame structure — what you must know for B41
PW products use two combinations: (UL/DL config 2, SSF config 7) and (UL/DL config 3, SSF config 8). The special subframe splits into: DwPTS (downlink data) + Guard Period (propagation delay protection) + UpPTS (SRS/PRACH or extra guard).

A01 RF Fundamentals Reference — signals, frequency, wavelength, power: the complete picture Beginner click to expand▾

RF fundamentals — signals, power, frequency, wavelength

Frequency, wavelength, and why band choice matters:
f = 1/T (Hz). λ = c/f where c = 3×10⁸ m/s. B1 at 2140 MHz: λ = 14 cm. B28 at 758 MHz: λ = 40 cm. Lower frequency = longer wavelength = better building penetration = wider coverage. Higher frequency = shorter wavelength = more bandwidth available = higher capacity. This is why B28 (700 MHz) is used for coverage (rural, indoors) and B41 (2545 MHz) for capacity (dense urban).
Power — amplitude, dBm, and what numbers mean in RF:
Signal power in RF is always in dBm (decibels relative to 1 mW). 0 dBm = 1 mW. +30 dBm = 1 W. +43 dBm = 20 W (your PA). −99 dBm = your RX sensitivity. The 142 dB difference between PA output and sensitivity threshold is the dynamic range challenge that every part of the RF chain must handle.
Sine wave parameters — amplitude, phase, frequency:
A sine wave is fully described by: amplitude (power level in dBm), frequency (carrier), and phase (timing offset vs reference). Beamforming works by controlling phase per column. ACLR measures spectral purity. EVM measures how accurately amplitude and phase are reproduced for each symbol. All three physical parameters appear as measurement metrics in your RCT.
1.1 What is RF?
RF stands for Radio Frequency — electromagnetic waves that carry energy and information through space without any physical conductor. The RF spectrum spans from roughly 3 Hz to 300 GHz. Below 20 kHz is audio; above 300 GHz is infrared/optical. Everything in between is RF.
An electromagnetic wave has two components oscillating perpendicular to each other and perpendicular to the direction of travel: the electric field (E) and the magnetic field (H). These cannot exist independently — changing E creates H and vice versa (Maxwell's equations). The wave travels at the speed of light c = 3×10⁸ m/s in free space.

1.3 Impedance — why everything is 50 Ω
All RF equipment uses 50 Ω impedance as the standard interface. This is a compromise: 30 Ω gives minimum loss in a coaxial cable, 77 Ω gives maximum power handling. 50 Ω is the geometric mean — chosen by military in the 1940s and it stuck. Every connector, cable, port on your EXA, EXM, and VNA is 50 Ω.
When the impedance of connected devices doesn't match (source impedance ≠ load impedance), some power is reflected back rather than transmitted. This is the cause of return loss, VSWR, and all the reflections you see in VNA measurements.
Reflection coefficient: Γ = (ZL − Z0) / (ZL + Z0). If ZL = Z0 = 50 Ω: Γ = 0 (no reflection). If ZL = open (∞): Γ = +1 (all reflected). If ZL = short (0): Γ = −1 (all reflected, 180° phase shift).
Return loss: RL = −20·log₁₀|Γ| dB. Higher return loss = better match. Target: >10 dB for antennas, >20 dB for connectors and cables.

2.1 Thermal noise — the fundamental floor
Every resistor at temperature T generates random voltage fluctuations due to thermal agitation of electrons. This is Johnson-Nyquist noise. The available noise power is:
P_noise = k · T · B
Where k = Boltzmann's constant = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K, T = temperature in Kelvin (290 K = 17°C = room temp), B = bandwidth in Hz.
At 290 K: P_noise = kTB = −174 dBm/Hz (the "kTB" number every RF engineer memorizes).

2.2 Noise figure (NF)
A real amplifier or receiver adds noise on top of the input thermal noise. Noise Figure (NF) quantifies how much worse the SNR gets as the signal passes through a component.
NF = SNR_in (dB) − SNR_out (dB)
NF = 0 dB would be a perfect, noiseless component. Real LNAs: NF = 1–3 dB. Mixers: 5–8 dB. Long cables at high frequency: significant NF contribution.
Effective noise figure (Friis formula for cascaded stages):
NF_total = NF₁ + (NF₂−1)/G₁ + (NF₃−1)/(G₁·G₂) + ...
Where NF values are linear (not dB) and G is gain (linear). The first stage (LNA) dominates. This is why the LNA quality matters most for receiver sensitivity — everything after it is "noise that follows the first gain stage."
Practical example for your RU receiver (B3, 10 MHz):
Thermal noise floor: −174 + 70 = −104 dBm
LNA NF = 3 dB → receiver noise floor = −104 + 3 = −101 dBm
Required SNR for LTE QPSK (1 RB): ~−1 dB
Implementation margin (filter losses, ADC, etc.): ~3 dB
REFSENS ≈ −101 − 1 + 3 = −99 dBm → 3GPP specifies −69 dBm (30 dB margin for real deployments)
2.3 Dynamic range
Dynamic range is the ratio between the strongest signal a receiver can handle and the weakest it can detect. It is limited at the bottom by the noise floor and at the top by non-linearity (compression or clipping).
For a spectrum analyzer (your EXA):
Bottom limit: Noise floor = kTB + NF of analyzer front end. Set by thermal noise and RBW.
Top limit: Mixer 1 dB compression point (P1dB). Above this, gain compresses, measurements are invalid.
Spurious-free dynamic range (SFDR): Range where the fundamental signal is visible above noise AND no third-order intermodulation products exceed the noise floor. SFDR = (2/3) · (IIP3 − kTBF).
For your RU receiver: Must handle UL signals from −120 dBm (cell edge UE) to −30 dBm (nearby UE at full power) — a 90 dB range. The AGC system adjusts LNA gain and attenuators to keep the ADC input within its linear range across this entire range.
When you do ACS/blocking tests, you're deliberately testing the edge of this dynamic range. A strong blocking signal in the adjacent channel partially saturates the front end, raising the effective noise floor and reducing the wanted signal's SNR below the required level.
The full derivation — why kTB = -174 dBm/Hz
Boltzmann's constant: k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K
Reference temperature: T = 290K (17°C) — IEEE standard
Thermal noise power density: N₀ = kT = 1.38×10⁻²³ × 290 = 4.0×10⁻²¹ W/Hz
In dBm/Hz: 10×log₁₀(4.0×10⁻²¹ / 10⁻³) = 10×log₁₀(4.0×10⁻¹⁸) = -173.97 ≈ -174 dBm/Hz

This is the MINIMUM noise in any resistor at room temperature. You CANNOT go below this. Every amplifier, ADC, and cable adds MORE noise on top.

A03 S-Parameters & Filters — S11/S21/S22, what VNA measures, filter types in your RF path Beginner–Intermediate click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRSTS-parameters in one sentence: S-parameters are the VNA's way of measuring how a component handles RF — S11 tells you how much signal bounces back (is the impedance matched?), and S21 tells you how much gets through (what is the gain or loss?).

S-parameters, filters, VNA — advanced RF measurements

S-parameters — what S11/S21/S12/S22 measure:
A 2-port network has 4 S-parameters. S11: input reflection (VSWR at Port 1). S21: forward transmission (gain or loss from Port 1 to Port 2). S12: reverse transmission (isolation). S22: output reflection. For a cable: S11 < −20 dB (good match), S21 = −loss dB (e.g. −2.5 dB). For your duplexer: S21_TX = −0.5 dB (TX insertion loss), S21_RX = −0.5 dB (RX insertion loss), S12_TX_to_RX < −50 dB (TX-to-RX isolation).
Filter specs — what each number means:
Insertion loss: how much the filter attenuates in the passband (bad, adds NF). Rejection: how much it attenuates out-of-band (good, blocks blockers). Passband ripple: variation of insertion loss across the passband. Group delay variation: frequency-dependent delay causing phase distortion. Your B1 pre-BPF: insertion loss 0.3 dB (passband 2110–2170 MHz), rejection > 60 dB (at B3 and B41 frequencies).
VNA usage in your lab:
S11 (cable/connector quality): both ports, check S11 < −20 dB at band frequencies. S21 (cable loss): Port 1 → cable → Port 2, magnitude gives loss, phase gives electrical length. S21 (column phase cal): Port 1 → BOB SMA → RFFE → Port 2, phase difference between columns = beamforming correction needed. Always SOLT cal first. See VNA2-7 cards for detailed procedures.

3.2 What the E5071C screen is showing (your filter)
Your E5071C screen throughout the videos shows S21 of what appears to be a bandpass filter or duplexer. Let's decode what you're looking at:
The passband (left side, wiggly trace): The frequency range where the filter lets signals through. The ripple you see is intentional — it's the equiripple characteristic of a Chebyshev filter design. The amplitude varies between the ripple peaks and dips (typically 0.1 to 1 dB ripple). Insertion loss at the center of the passband tells you how much signal you lose just from passing through the filter (typically 0.5–2 dB for a good diplexer).
The rolloff (middle, sharp drop): The filter transition band — where it goes from passing to blocking. A steep rolloff means better selectivity — the filter can separate closely-spaced signals. Chebyshev designs sacrifice passband flatness for steeper rolloff. Elliptic (Cauer) filters add transmission zeros in the stopband for even sharper rolloff.
The stopband (right side, flat floor): How much the filter blocks unwanted signals. Your trace shows ~50–60 dB rejection. This is how much isolation the duplexer provides between TX and RX bands — absolutely critical. If the TX signal at +43 dBm gets −50 dB isolation → only −7 dBm reaches the RX input — still easily detectable but the duplexer filter would reject it further. A real duplexer needs >50 dB between TX and RX port.

3.4 VNA calibration in detail — why and how
A VNA works by sweeping a source signal across a frequency range, injecting it into the DUT, and measuring what comes back (S11) and what comes out (S21). The problem: the cables, connectors, and adapters between the VNA port and your DUT have their own S-parameters (loss, reflections). These systematic errors contaminate your measurement.
The three systematic errors the SOLT cal corrects:
Directivity error: The VNA's directional coupler isn't perfect — some of the incident signal leaks into the reflected wave measurement even with a perfect match at the DUT.
Source match error: The VNA source isn't perfectly matched to 50 Ω — some reflected signal from the DUT bounces back off the source and re-enters the DUT.
Frequency response error: The test cables, connectors, and adapters have their own insertion loss and phase shift which varies with frequency.
SOLT procedure step by step:
Short: Connect a short circuit (0 Ω) to Port 1 at the calibration reference plane. The VNA measures: all power should reflect back with 180° phase shift (Γ = −1). Any deviation = directivity and source match errors at that frequency.
Open: Connect an open circuit (no connection). All power reflects, 0° phase shift (Γ = +1). The known parasitic capacitance of the open is stored in the cal kit — the VNA uses it to distinguish directivity from source match.
Load: Connect a precision 50 Ω termination. Should produce Γ = 0 (perfect match, no reflection). Any measured signal = raw directivity error of the coupler. This establishes the error floor.
Thru (2-port cal only): Connect Port 1 directly to Port 2. Now the VNA can measure transmission errors — cable loss, adapter insertion loss, phase delay.
After these four measurements the VNA solves 12 error terms (for full 2-port cal) and removes them from subsequent DUT measurements. Your calibration reference plane is now at the tip of the cable/connector where you connected the Short/Open/Load — everything between VNA port and that tip is removed from the measurement.
If you move or flex the cable after calibration, the cal is invalidated. The cable's S-parameters change with flex. Always recalibrate if the setup changes. Check the cal status indicator — 'C' = active, 'Cc' = interpolated (acceptable but not ideal), no 'C' = no cal, results are uncorrected and unreliable.
3.5 Time domain analysis on the E5071C
One of the most powerful E5071C features is converting the frequency sweep into a time-domain response. The math is an inverse FFT (IFFT) of the swept S-parameter data. The result shows when reflections arrive at the measurement port — which tells you where they come from.
A signal travels at roughly 2×10⁸ m/s in typical coaxial cable (velocity factor ~0.67). So a reflection arriving at t = 5 ns came from a point 5 ns × 2×10⁸ m/s / 2 = 50 cm away from the calibration plane (divide by 2 because signal makes a round trip).
Spike at t=0: Reflection at the calibration plane itself — connector interface, impedance step at the DUT port.
Spike at t=2 ns: Reflection 20 cm down the cable — could be a kinked cable, damaged connector, or moisture ingress.
Spike at t=10 ns: Reflection 1 m from the port — cable end, unterminated stub, or mismatch at the antenna.
For your RU antenna port verification: do a 1-port S11 measurement in time domain. You should see a small reflection at t=0 (connector), then ideally nothing else. Any additional spike between the connector and the antenna location = impedance discontinuity in the feed path that's causing reflections and reducing radiated power.
A04 LTE/NR Air Interface — OFDM, resource grid, FDD vs TDD, physical channels (PDSCH/PUSCH) Intermediate click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRSTLTE air interface in one sentence: LTE divides the channel into thousands of tiny 15 kHz subcarriers (OFDM), assigns groups of them to each user (resource blocks), and switches between FDD (separate frequencies for up/down) or TDD (same frequency, different time slots) depending on the band.

LTE air interface — OFDM, resource blocks, FDD, TDD

Resource block structure — from subcarrier to throughput:
1 subcarrier = 15 kHz. 1 Resource Block (RB) = 12 subcarriers = 180 kHz. 1 slot = 7 symbols (normal CP) = 0.5 ms. 1 subframe = 2 slots = 1 ms. Channel bandwidth to RBs: 5 MHz→25RB, 10 MHz→50RB, 15 MHz→75RB, 20 MHz→100RB. Peak throughput (100RB, 64QAM, MIMO 4×4): ~300 Mbps. Your sensitivity test uses all RBs at QPSK 1/3 — lowest throughput, best coverage.
FDD bands (B1/B3/B8/B28) vs TDD band (B41):
FDD: separate DL and UL frequencies. B1: DL 2110–2170 MHz, UL 1920–1980 MHz (190 MHz duplex gap). Duplexer separates TX and RX. Can transmit and receive simultaneously. TDD B41: DL and UL share 2496–2690 MHz. T/R switch alternates TX and RX. DL/UL ratio configurable (e.g. 3DL:1UL). Needs PTP timing synchronisation (±1.5 μs). Asymmetric — can give more DL capacity on demand.
OFDM benefits and the PAPR cost:
Benefits: flat fading per subcarrier (simple equalisation), flexible resource allocation (schedule per RB per UE), robust against multipath (cyclic prefix). PAPR cost: OFDM sum of many subcarriers creates random peaks 10–12 dB above average. PA must handle these peaks without clipping → either back off PA (lower efficiency) or use CFR+DPD (maintain efficiency). Your AAU uses CFR+DPD to enable PA operation near P1dB without ACLR violations.
4.1 Why OFDM?
A wideband channel (20 MHz of LTE) has frequency-selective fading — some frequencies fade more than others depending on the multipath environment. In a single-carrier system, a deep fade at one frequency wipes out a chunk of your data. OFDM (Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing) slices the wideband channel into N narrow subcarriers, each so narrow (15 kHz in LTE) that it experiences flat fading. A fade at one subcarrier only kills data on that one subcarrier — easy to correct with FEC coding.
OFDM mechanics: N subcarriers spaced Δf = 15 kHz apart. Each subcarrier carries one OFDM symbol over duration T_symbol = 1/Δf = 66.7 µs (plus cyclic prefix). All subcarriers transmitted simultaneously — their orthogonality ensures zero ICI (inter-carrier interference) at the exact sampling points.

A05 TX Chain Mastery — PA linearity, EVM, ACLR, DPD: the complete transmit picture Intermediate click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRST TX chain in one sentence: The transmit chain takes a tiny digital signal from the computer and amplifies it up to a powerful radio wave that shoots out the antenna. Like a whisper going into a microphone, through an amplifier, and out a loudspeaker — except the "loudspeaker" is a 20-watt radio wave.

TX chain — complete downlink signal path

The TX chain converts baseband IQ data from the BBU into a high-power RF signal at the antenna. Every stage serves a specific purpose. Understanding each block explains every TX test you run — power, ACLR, EVM, DPD convergence.

FHGW — what it does before the RF chain:
The Fronthaul Gateway receives compressed IQ data from the DU via eCPRI over 10GbE. It performs BFP (Block Floating Point) decompression — expanding 9-bit compressed samples back to 16-bit IQ. Then IFFT converts frequency-domain subcarriers to time-domain OFDM symbols. Cyclic prefix is added (4.69 μs normal CP) to protect against multipath. CFR (Crest Factor Reduction) clips and filters the waveform to reduce PAPR from ~12 dB down to ~8 dB — this is critical because the PA cannot handle 12 dB PAPR efficiently.
DPD — Digital Pre-Distortion (most important TX block):
The PA is nonlinear — it compresses near saturation, generating harmonics and intermodulation products that cause ACLR failures. DPD pre-distorts the input signal with the inverse of the PA's nonlinearity so the output is linear. It works by sampling a small portion of the PA output (the observation path), comparing it to the ideal signal, and continuously updating its pre-distortion coefficients. DPD needs 30–60 seconds to converge after power-on or any power level change. Always wait for DPD convergence before measuring ACLR or EVM — measuring before convergence gives wrong results. When DPD works correctly: ACLR improves by 10–15 dB compared to no DPD.
PA — Power Amplifier (the dominant TX block):
The PA is the final amplifier that brings the signal to full transmit power. Your AAU uses GaN (Gallium Nitride) PAs — chosen for high power density and efficiency at RF frequencies. Key PA specs:
• Output power: +43 dBm (20W) per column at antenna port
• Efficiency: ~35% (Class AB operation) — means 65% of DC power becomes heat
• ACLR limit: −45 dBc — signal power in adjacent channel must be 45 dB below wanted signal
• EVM limit: 3% for 64QAM
• Protection: thermal shutdown at 85°C junction temp, VSWR protection, over-current protection

The PA is why your RU gets hot — at 20W output and 35% efficiency, the PA dissipates about 37W of heat per column = 148W total for 4 columns.
BOB SMA — how you measure TX without destroying EXA:
The BOB (Break-Out Board) SMA port is a directional coupler output that taps −30 dB of the main TX signal. If antenna port has +43 dBm, BOB SMA has +43 − 30 = +13 dBm — still too high for EXA (max input −10 dBm safely). You add a 30 dB attenuator pad between BOB SMA and EXA input. Final EXA input = +13 − 30 = −17 dBm — safe range. Your rated power targets at BOB SMA already account for the coupler ratio — B1 target −16.7 dBm at BOB SMA = +43.3 dBm at antenna = about 21W.
ACLR — what it measures and why it matters:
ACLR (Adjacent Channel Leakage Ratio) measures how much of the TX signal power spills into adjacent frequency channels. Limit is −45 dBc (45 dB below the wanted signal power). ACLR failure means your PA is generating spectral regrowth — nonlinear distortion products spreading into adjacent channels. This would interfere with other operators' spectrum. Root causes of ACLR failure: DPD not converged, DPD model wrong (e.g. after PA replacement), CFR not working, PA operating beyond its P1dB compression point. Always measure ACLR at rated power — measuring at reduced power can give passing results even when the real operating point would fail.
🎯 Analogy: DPD is like tuning a hearing aid

A hearing aid takes distorted sound (the PA's nonlinear output) and pre-processes the input so the output sounds correct. When you first put it in, the aid doesn't know your specific ear — it takes a minute to adapt its settings to match your hearing profile. DPD is the same: when TX first enables, it doesn't know the PA's exact nonlinearity today (which varies with temperature, ageing, power level). The ORX path samples the PA output and the DPD engine runs an adaptive algorithm, updating its coefficients every few milliseconds until the error is below 1%. Measuring ACLR before this converges is like measuring eye prescription before the patient has focused — the number is meaningless.

A06 RX Chain Mastery — LNA, mixer, ADC, AGC: why each stage determines your sensitivity Intermediate click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRST RX chain in one sentence: The receive chain catches a faint radio signal from a distant phone (weaker than a whisper) and carefully amplifies it without adding too much noise — so the computer can understand what the phone said. The LNA (Low Noise Amplifier) is the most critical part: it is the first thing that touches the signal and its quality determines everything that follows.

RX chain — complete uplink signal path

The RX chain converts a weak RF signal from the UE antenna into a digital baseband stream that the DU can process. Every component in the chain adds noise (increasing NF) or adds gain (amplifying both signal and noise). The art of RX chain design is minimising noise before the first amplifier.

LNA — why it is the most critical component:
The LNA (Low Noise Amplifier) is the first active component the signal reaches after passing through the duplexer and pre-BPF. Its NF of 2 dB and gain of +20 dB (×100 linear) set the entire system noise figure. Everything before the LNA (cable, duplexer, pre-BPF) adds its full loss as NF. Everything after the LNA has its NF contribution divided by 100 — making post-LNA components almost irrelevant to sensitivity. This is why: a 7 dB NF mixer after the LNA only contributes 0.07 dB to system NF, but a 0.1 dB longer cable before the LNA degrades sensitivity by exactly 0.1 dB. Your LNA is a GaAs pHEMT — chosen for low noise at microwave frequencies.
VGA/AGC — keeping the ADC in range:
The Variable Gain Amplifier with Automatic Gain Control adjusts the signal level dynamically to keep the ADC operating in its optimal range (~−12 dBFS). A nearby UE (strong signal) causes the AGC to reduce gain to prevent ADC clipping. A distant UE (weak signal) causes AGC to increase gain to stay above the quantisation noise floor. The AGC settles in <10 μs — fast enough to track fast fading. If AGC fails or saturates, the ADC clips → RSSI reading wraps around → completely wrong power measurement → all RSSI-based tests fail.
ADC — where the signal becomes digital:
The 12-bit ADC samples the analog baseband IQ signal at 122.88 MSPS (mega samples per second). It outputs dBFS readings — power relative to ADC full scale. The ADC_ADJUST calibration constant converts dBFS to absolute dBm at the RU antenna port. The 12-bit resolution gives 74 dB of dynamic range (SQNR = 6.02×12 + 1.76 = 74 dB). This means the ADC can cleanly handle signals from full scale (0 dBFS) down to −74 dBFS — a 74 dB window that the VGA/AGC keeps the signal centred in.
Two places to read RSSI — CLI vs DU, must agree within 2 dB:
CLI (get_rssi N): reads raw dBFS from ADC N, adds ADC_ADJUST → dBm at RU port. Fast, direct, per-column.
DU RSSI (testmac rssi): reads after FFT processing in the baseband — averages across subcarriers, includes interference. Both must agree within ±2 dB. If they disagree: CLI accurate but DU off → FHGW/baseband path issue. Both off from EXM → ADC_ADJUST wrong. CLI varies per column → individual column hardware fault.

A07 Massive MIMO & Beamforming — spatial multiplexing, beam weights, OTA radiation patterns Advanced click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRSTBeamforming in one sentence: By applying a slightly different timing (phase shift) to each of the four antenna columns, the signals add up strongly in one direction and cancel out in others — like four people clapping in sync to make the sound louder in one spot.

Beamforming — how phase shifts steer the beam

Analog vs digital beamforming:
Analog beamforming: one phase shifter per antenna element in the RF domain. Simple, low power, but can only form one beam at a time. Your AAU uses this for horizontal beam steering.
Digital beamforming: separate ADC/DAC per element, phase applied in baseband. Multiple simultaneous beams possible. Enables MU-MIMO. Your AAU uses digital BF for vertical tilt (elevation).
Hybrid: combination — analog in azimuth (wide sweep), digital in elevation (fine control).
Why element spacing = λ/2:
λ/2 spacing prevents grating lobes — spurious beams at other angles that would waste power and cause interference. With λ/2 spacing, the maximum steerable angle without grating lobes is ±90° (boresight to endfire). Wider spacing allows finer steering resolution but at the cost of grating lobes. Your 4-column array with λ/2 spacing gives about ±90° steering range and ~20° beam half-power width.
VNA phase calibration — what it corrects:
Each column's TX path (from digital beamformer output to antenna element) has slightly different electrical length due to cable/trace length variation and component tolerances. If Column B is 5° longer than Column A, the beam will point 2° off from commanded angle. VNA measures S21 phase per column → computes Δφ vs Column A → corrects the PE44820DS digital phase shifter register per column. Target: all columns within ±2° of each other.
7.1 Antenna fundamentals
Antenna gain: An antenna doesn't amplify — it redistributes power. Gain G (in dBi) describes how much more power is radiated in the peak direction compared to an isotropic antenna that radiates equally in all directions. A 17 dBi antenna radiates 50× more power in its peak direction than an isotropic antenna — by concentrating energy spatially rather than amplifying.
EIRP = TX power (dBm) + Antenna gain (dBi)
Example: Your RU TX = +43 dBm (20 W) per port. Antenna array gain = 18 dBi (64 elements). EIRP = 43 + 18 = +61 dBm (1.26 kW equivalent isotropic). This is the number that 3GPP tests under TS 38.141-2 §6.3.
Beamwidth: The angular range over which the antenna gain is within 3 dB of peak. Narrower beamwidth = higher gain but less spatial coverage. A 64-element massive MIMO array: horizontal beamwidth ~5°, vertical beamwidth ~8°. The beam must be steered electronically to serve different users.
7.2 Massive MIMO — from theory to your Metro AAU
Traditional macro base stations use 2–8 antennas. Massive MIMO uses 32, 64, 128, or 192 antenna elements. The advantages are profound:
Array gain: N antenna elements coherently combined → N× power (in linear terms) = 10·log10(N) dB gain. 64 elements: +18 dB. This 18 dB extra gain vs. a single antenna can be traded for coverage, capacity, or reduced TX power (for energy efficiency).
Spatial multiplexing: Use the many degrees of freedom to serve multiple users simultaneously in the same time-frequency resource. With 64 antennas, the base station can create 8–16 independent spatial beams, each carrying data for a different user — multiplying spectral efficiency by 8–16×.
Interference rejection: Steer nulls toward interfering sources. The many antennas allow precise spatial filtering — useful in dense deployments where neighboring cells cause significant interference.
Your Metro AAU has this full 2D array architecture. The antenna elements are arranged in rows and columns on a flat panel. Horizontal steering controls azimuth (left-right). Vertical steering controls elevation (tilt — crucial for controlling which floors of a building or which cells receive the beam).
7.3 Beamforming — the physics
Beamforming works by adjusting the phase (and optionally amplitude) of the signal fed to each antenna element so that the radiated waves from all elements add constructively in the desired direction and destructively in other directions.
Simple 2-element example: Two antennas separated by λ/2. If both antennas transmit at the same phase (0° difference), the waves add constructively broadside (perpendicular to the array) and cancel end-fire (along the array axis). To steer the beam to angle θ, introduce a phase difference Δφ = π·sin(θ) between the elements. For a 64-element array, this creates very fine angular resolution.
Analog beamforming: Phase shifters in the RF path (before or after the PA). Fast switching, simple, but can only form one beam at a time. Used in FR2 (mmWave) due to the large number of elements and cost of having a full radio chain per element.
Digital beamforming: Each element has its own full radio chain (DAC, PA, LNA, ADC). Phase and amplitude weights applied in the digital baseband. Can form multiple simultaneous beams. Used in FR1 massive MIMO. More expensive but full flexibility.
Hybrid beamforming (your Metro AAU): Combination of analog sub-arrays and digital chains. Fewer digital chains than elements — cost/performance tradeoff. Typical: 8 digital chains driving 64 antenna elements via analog phase shifters (8 elements per chain).
7.4 Why OTA testing is unavoidable for AAS
In a traditional macro base station, you can unplug the antenna and connect a cable to a specific TRX port. You measure one PA at a time. The antenna gain is known separately. The conducted power is the reference.
In your Metro AAU, the antennas are permanently integrated. There is no connector where you can isolate "the antenna" from "the PA." The antenna elements couple to each other (mutual coupling) — the radiation pattern of element N depends on what all other elements N−1, N+1, etc. are doing. You cannot characterize the antenna pattern from element measurements alone.
The only correct way to characterize the full system — antenna + PA + beamforming weights all working together — is to measure the radiated power in the far field. This is exactly what OTA testing does. The CATR creates a far-field equivalent plane wave in a compact space, and the probe antenna measures what the RU actually radiates at each beam angle.
7.5 The CATR — compact antenna test range explained
The far-field distance for an antenna of physical size D at wavelength λ is:
Far field distance = 2D²/λ (Fraunhofer distance)
For a 30 cm massive MIMO panel at 2 GHz (λ = 15 cm): far field distance = 2 × 0.3² / 0.15 = 1.2 m. That's manageable. But at 3.5 GHz for a 50 cm panel: 2 × 0.5² / 0.086 = 5.8 m. And at 28 GHz for a 10 cm mmWave module: 2 × 0.1² / 0.011 = 1.8 m. So for FR2 the chamber can be compact; for FR1 massive MIMO panels, 5–10m far-field distances would require a very large chamber.
The CATR solves this by using a parabolic reflector to convert the spherical wave from a feed antenna into a plane wave over a short distance. The feed antenna sits at the focal point of the reflector. The plane wave exists in the "quiet zone" — the region where the wave is sufficiently flat and uniform for measurement. The DUT sits in the quiet zone, and a probe antenna measures the received signal level at various DUT orientations controlled by a precision turntable.
What happens during TRP measurement:
Step 1: Position DUT at reference orientation (beam peak pointing toward probe). Measure EIRP = reference power.
Step 2: Rotate DUT through N_θ × N_φ grid of angles (typically 5° or 10° steps). Measure EIRP at each angle.
Step 3: TRP = discrete integral of EIRP × sin(θ) over all measured angles (the sin(θ) is the solid angle weighting factor for spherical integration).
Step 4: Compare measured TRP against declared TRP. Pass if within ±specification window.
A08 Propagation Models — AWGN, fading (EPA/EVA/ETU), PropSim: how channels are emulated in lab Intermediate click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRSTChannel models in one sentence: In your lab, the cable gives a perfect connection (AWGN) — but in the real world, signals bounce off buildings and arrive at different times (multipath), which is what EPA/EVA/ETU channel models simulate to test how the RU handles messy real conditions.

Propagation channel models — AWGN, EPA, EVA, ETU in your tests

AWGN — your standard lab test condition:
AWGN (Additive White Gaussian Noise) is the simplest channel model — single path, flat fading, no multipath. Your lab bench with direct cable connection from EXM to RU is AWGN. All DSA sensitivity values in your table are AWGN performance. Real channels have multipath, but AWGN gives the best achievable sensitivity that sets the baseline. 3GPP defines sensitivity tests under AWGN — this is what you verify.
EPA/EVA/ETU — multipath channel models:
EPA (Extended Pedestrian A): 7 taps, 0–410 ns delay, 5 km/h Doppler. Represents pedestrian indoor/suburban. EVA (Extended Vehicular A): 9 taps, 0–2.51 μs delay, 70 km/h. Urban micro. ETU (Extended Typical Urban): 9 taps, 0–5 μs delay, high delay spread. Dense urban macro. Each model has delay profiles and Doppler spread specified by 3GPP. Sensitivity degrades 1–3 dB vs AWGN under these conditions due to multipath combining losses.
PropSim — when you need it:
PropSim is a hardware channel emulator that sits between EXM and RU. It applies the EPA/EVA/ETU delay profiles in real hardware, including realistic Doppler. Required for full conformance testing when AWGN sensitivity alone is not sufficient. Your bench (AWGN only) is sufficient for RCT smoke tests and hardware validation. PropSim is used for type approval / 3GPP conformance certification.
8.1 Why channel models exist
In the real world, a UE's signal travels to the base station via many different physical paths: direct line-of-sight, reflections off buildings, diffraction over rooftops, ground reflection, etc. Each path has different delay (due to different path length) and different amplitude (due to different path loss and reflection coefficient). The received signal is the sum of all these copies.
These paths change as the UE moves, causing the channel to fade — sometimes coherently constructive (signal peaks), sometimes destructive (signal dips). At a UE speed of 70 km/h on a 1.8 GHz channel, a complete fade cycle (UE moves λ/2 = 8.3 cm) takes 8.3 cm / (70/3.6 m/s) = 4.3 ms — very fast.
3GPP defined standardized channel models so that every manufacturer tests under the same conditions. A channel emulator (PropSim in your setup) implements these models as real-time digital filters with random phase evolution at the Doppler rate. This makes lab testing reproducible — anyone, anywhere, with the same PropSim model gets the same fading statistics.
8.2 AWGN — the baseline
Additive White Gaussian Noise — no multipath, no fading. Just thermal noise added to a clean signal. This represents the absolute best-case propagation — as if the UE were in line-of-sight with no reflectors.
AWGN sensitivity is always the best (lowest input power at which throughput passes). If the RU fails AWGN sensitivity, it has a fundamental receiver problem (NF too high, hardware fault). Fading models always give worse sensitivity than AWGN because multipath adds ICI and ISI.
All your PUCCH format 1 vectors test AWGN first (along with EPA5 and EVA70). AWGN is the reference — it's the point where you first verify the receiver is working at all before stressing it with fading.

8.4 PropSim — what the channel emulator actually does
PropSim is a hardware channel emulator. It takes the UL digital waveform from a signal generator (or from the waveform file loaded into the tester), applies the mathematical multipath channel model in real time, and outputs the faded signal to the RU's antenna port through the attenuated test cable (cable_loss = −77 dB in your config).
How it works internally:
Input: Baseband IQ samples (or RF signal). PropSim upconverts if needed.
Channel filter: For each tap (delay), applies a fractional delay filter and multiplies by a complex fading coefficient. The fading coefficient evolves according to the Doppler spectrum corresponding to the velocity setting (Jakes model for isotropic scattering).
Summation: All tap outputs are summed to create the faded received signal.
Noise addition: AWGN is added at a level corresponding to the desired SNR/received power.
Output: The faded, noisy signal is transmitted to the RU via the calibrated cable (cable_loss compensated by PropSim output power).
The ext_delay = 0.009997485 seconds in your config (≈10 ms) is the additional timing offset applied to compensate for the round-trip delay of the test cables and PropSim internal processing. This keeps the UL frame timing aligned with what the RU expects based on the DL reference it receives from the tester.

10.1 EVM failure diagnosis — systematic approach
EVM fail is one of the most common test failures and often the hardest to root cause because multiple independent faults can cause similar EVM degradation. Use this systematic approach:
Step 1 — Characterize the failure pattern:
Uniform across all RBs? → LO phase noise, DAC clock jitter, IQ imbalance (gain or phase mismatch between I and Q paths), or PA compression (check if EVM improves when you reduce TX power by 3 dB).
Worse at band edges (high/low RB index)? → Filter group delay distortion. The band-select filter's group delay is typically highest at the passband edges. This introduces frequency-dependent phase error across the channel bandwidth — different subcarriers arrive with different delays, distorting the constellation.
Worse at specific RBs in the middle? → Filter notch (narrow-band amplitude dip), stuck DAC bit (creates a spurious tone at a specific frequency), or digital clipping if a specific frequency resource hits a processing path with lower precision.
Only fails on one antenna port? → Hardware fault in that specific TX chain (DAC, up-converter, PA, or connector). Compare EVM across all antenna ports simultaneously using multi-port ETM 3.1.
Fails only after warm-up? → Thermal drift in oscillator reference, PA thermal compression, or temperature-dependent IQ imbalance. Let the unit thermally stabilize for 30+ minutes before measurement.
Step 2 — Reduce TX power: Reduce TX power 3 dB, measure EVM again. If EVM improves significantly (2+ dB), PA compression was the dominant cause. If EVM doesn't improve, PA compression is not the cause — look at signal generation (DAC, IQ modulator, LO).
Step 3 — Check DPD: If the RU has DPD, disable it (if test mode allows). If EVM gets dramatically better with DPD off, the DPD model is miscalibrated and creating pre-distortion that doesn't match the PA. If EVM gets dramatically worse, DPD was working and the underlying PA is very nonlinear.
10.2 ACLR failure — diagnosis tree
Is ACLR bad on BOTH sides (±1 channel) equally?
→ PA nonlinearity or DPD failure. PA-generated IMD products are symmetric around the carrier.
→ Test: reduce power 1 dB at a time. PA-caused ACLR improves at ~2–3 dB per 1 dB power reduction (third-order slope). DPD failure: ACLR typically steps by a large amount (10+ dB) when DPD is toggled.
Is ACLR bad on only ONE side?
→ Asymmetric clipping or filter phase asymmetry. Check the digital baseband filter coefficients are symmetric. Also check if there is a frequency offset (carrier frequency error) that causes asymmetric ACLR presentation.
→ Could also be a spur (spurious emission) landing in the adjacent channel — use the EXA with narrow RBW to inspect the adjacent channel spectrum for discrete tones vs. continuous spectral regrowth.
Difference between ACLR failure and SEM failure:
ACLR measures power in a defined adjacent channel bandwidth (e.g. 5 MHz wide). A single strong spurious tone would cause ACLR fail.
SEM checks the full spectrum shape against the mask. Broadband spectral regrowth (PA IMD) causes SEM fail across a wide frequency range. A single spur causes a localized SEM spike.
10.3 REFSENS failure — diagnostic flowchart
Check 1 — Cable loss calibration: Measure S21 of the full path from tester output to RU antenna port with the VNA. If cable_loss entry in your config doesn't match measured S21, the wrong signal level is being delivered. Correct the config value and retest.
Check 2 — Duplexer RX filter: Measure S21 of the duplexer RX port. If the passband insertion loss has increased (aging, moisture, thermal shock), you're losing extra dB before the LNA. Even 1 dB extra IL = 1 dB worse REFSENS.
Check 3 — LNA noise figure: Measure NF directly with a noise figure meter or cold-source method. If LNA NF has degraded (ESD damage, electromigration), REFSENS will be directly affected. NF increases 1 dB → REFSENS degrades 1 dB.
Check 4 — LO phase noise: Connect spectrum analyzer to the LO test point (if accessible). Increased phase noise degrades SNR of the received signal even at optimum input level. Phase noise close to the carrier (within the channel BW) directly adds to the noise floor.
Check 5 — AGC calibration: Verify the AGC sets the ADC input level correctly at the REFSENS signal level. If AGC sets too much attenuation, the ADC is underdriven and quantization noise becomes significant. If AGC sets too little, a small blocker that the ACS test introduces can partially clip the ADC.
10.4 RSSI accuracy at band edge — why it's the hardest
RSSI is defined as the total received power across the declared channel bandwidth. The problem at band edge (fb and ft): the RX filter doesn't have a perfectly flat response — it has rolloff at the band edges. This means the effective integration bandwidth for RSSI is slightly narrower than the declared channel bandwidth.
If the filter's −3 dB point is at the channel edge, roughly half the power in the edge subcarriers is attenuated. The RU's RSSI algorithm doesn't know the filter is cutting off — it integrates over what the ADC sees, which is the filtered version. Result: RSSI reads low by 0.5–1 dB at band edge vs. the true signal level.
The fix: Calibration tables that correct RSSI based on frequency. The RU stores a calibration offset per band edge, derived during factory calibration. If this calibration table is wrong (wrong duplexer type fitted, calibration not run, EEPROM corruption), RSSI_Accuracy_fb fails. Check: compare RSSI at fm (mid-band, should be accurate) vs fb (band edge, may be offset). If the error is constant across temperatures → calibration table error. If error varies with temperature → temperature compensation table fault.
10.5 B28 power anomaly between firmware builds — your specific case
The B28 TX power difference observed between 2025.4.X and 2026.2.1 firmware builds follows a recognizable pattern in multi-band RU development. When a new firmware build changes PA bias, DAC full-scale register values, or DPD model initialization, band-specific power offsets can appear.
Root cause categories — ordered by likelihood:
DAC full-scale setting change: If the firmware changed the DAC's full-scale register (VREF or digital gain coefficient) for the B28 PA chain, output power changes proportionally. A 1-LSB change in a 12-bit DAC VREF register = ~0.024 dB. But register writes in groups of 4 = ~0.1 dB steps. Check: read DAC configuration registers via I2C/SPI debug interface and compare 2025.4.X vs 2026.2.1 register dumps.
PA bias voltage change: If firmware changed the PA drain voltage or gate bias for B28 (often stored as EEPROM cal values), quiescent current changes → output power changes. More bias → more gain → more output power. This is exactly what PWK is investigating with their "DAC setting changes impacting B28 power."
DPD model initialization difference: If the new firmware initializes DPD with different coefficients (or a different iteration count before declaring convergence), the pre-distortion depth differs. DPD reduces the effective PA gain → changes output power. Check: compare output power with DPD disabled across both firmware builds. If power difference disappears → DPD initialization is the cause.
Band switching/gain table update: Modern multi-band RUs have frequency-specific gain tables that set the target output power per band. If this table was updated for B28 specifically (either intentionally or as a bug), B28 power changes while other bands don't. Check: compare output power across B1, B3, B8, B28, B41. Only B28 affected → band-specific config. All bands affected proportionally → common path (LO, reference clock, ADC/DAC VREF).
Never accept "it's within tolerance" without understanding the root cause. A B28 power change that stays within ±1.5 dB today might be a symptom of a temperature-dependent change that exceeds tolerance at extreme temperatures. Insist on root cause identification before closing the GRDEV ticket.

A12 Phase noise — what it is, why it matters, how to measure and fix it Advanced click to expand▾
12.1 What phase noise actually is
An ideal oscillator produces a perfect sine wave at exactly frequency f₀. Real oscillators produce a signal whose frequency wanders slightly from moment to moment, and whose phase drifts randomly. This random phase jitter is phase noise. On a spectrum analyzer, instead of a perfect single spike at f₀, you see a skirt of energy spreading out from the carrier on both sides — the "noise floor" rises as you approach the carrier frequency.
Phase noise is measured as L(f) — the single-sideband phase noise density at offset f from the carrier, expressed in dBc/Hz (dB below carrier per Hz of bandwidth). A good oscillator might have L(1 kHz) = −80 dBc/Hz and L(1 MHz) = −140 dBc/Hz. The phase noise decreases (improves) as you move further from the carrier.
12.2 Why phase noise destroys OFDM EVM
OFDM subcarriers are spaced 15 kHz apart in LTE. They rely on perfect orthogonality — each subcarrier is sampled at a point where all other subcarriers have zero contribution (by design). Phase noise breaks this orthogonality in two ways:
Common Phase Error (CPE): Low-frequency phase noise (offsets much less than subcarrier spacing) causes the entire OFDM symbol to rotate by the same angle — all subcarriers get the same phase error. This is detectable and correctable using pilot subcarriers. LTE uses CRS for this correction.
Inter-Carrier Interference (ICI): Phase noise at offsets comparable to and greater than the subcarrier spacing causes each subcarrier to be contaminated by energy from neighboring subcarriers. This ICI cannot be corrected — it looks like additive noise on every subcarrier and directly degrades EVM.
The ICI power from phase noise that falls within the measurement BW is approximately:
ICI_power ≈ ∫ L(f) · df over the subcarrier BW
For LTE 15 kHz subcarrier spacing: phase noise at offsets 7.5–22.5 kHz from the LO directly becomes ICI noise in the adjacent subcarriers. A phase noise of −80 dBc/Hz at 10 kHz offset contributes roughly −80 + 10·log10(15000) = −80 + 41.8 = −38 dBc of ICI — potentially limiting EVM to ~1.3% even with a perfect PA.
12.3 How to diagnose phase noise as the EVM cause
If EVM fails and the following symptoms are present, phase noise is likely the cause:
EVM is uniformly bad across ALL subcarriers and ALL resource blocks (not just edges or specific RBs)
Reducing TX power 6 dB does NOT improve EVM (PA compression is not the cause)
EVM is worse at higher modulation orders (256QAM fails while 64QAM marginal) — because higher-order constellations are more sensitive to phase errors
EVM is temperature-dependent — PLL VCO phase noise typically worsens by 0.1–0.3 dB/°C above 40°C
EVM is worse at higher carrier frequencies — phase noise from the LO gets multiplied up: if your reference oscillator has phase noise L(f), and it's multiplied by N to reach the carrier frequency, the phase noise becomes L(f) + 20·log10(N) dB worse
Measurement method: Use the EXA's Phase Noise measurement application (Mode → Phase Noise). Sweep offset from 100 Hz to 40 MHz. Compare against the spec limits for your LO. If phase noise at 10–100 kHz offsets is worse than −85 dBc/Hz, it will impact LTE EVM.
Phase noise in your measurements — why it matters
Phase noise appears as sidebands around your carrier. On EXA it shows as the 'skirt' around the carrier peak.

For EVM: Phase noise causes symbol rotation in the constellation → EVM increases, especially at high modulation orders. 64QAM is much more sensitive to phase noise than QPSK.

For ACLR: Phase noise contributes to spectral regrowth. Very close-in phase noise (within channel BW) appears as EVM. Far-out phase noise appears as noise floor in adjacent channels.

A13 5G Beam Management — how gNB finds the best beam: CSI-RS, SSB, SRS, PMI/RI feedback Advanced click to expand▾
PLAIN ENGLISH FIRST5G beam management in one sentence: The gNB sweeps a beam across 64 directions (SSB), the UE picks the strongest one and says "that one", then finer beams (CSI-RS) narrow it down further — and for TDD like B41, the gNB can also use the UE's uplink signal (SRS) to figure out the best direction without the UE having to report anything.

5G NR beam management — SSB, CSI-RS, SRS lifecycle

SSB — initial beam discovery:
SSB (Synchronisation Signal Block) contains PSS+SSS+PBCH. Transmitted in bursts of up to 64 beams sweeping different directions. Period: 20 ms. UE measures RSRP per beam index and reports the best beam. gNB selects the TX beam with highest reported RSRP. This is beam acquisition — coarse, done at initial access. The PRACH preamble is sent in the direction of the best SSB beam.
CSI-RS — beam refinement after access:
CSI-RS (Channel State Information Reference Signal) is a reference signal the gNB transmits in a specific beam direction for the UE to measure. Finer granularity than SSB — can test many more beam directions. UE reports CRI (CSI-RS Resource Indicator) and RSRP per beam. gNB refines the TX beam from the SSB-selected direction to the optimal CSI-RS direction. Repeated periodically to track UE movement.
SRS — uplink beam management:
SRS (Sounding Reference Signal) is transmitted by the UE and received by the gNB. The gNB measures the UL channel quality from each of the 4 RX columns. For TDD: UL and DL channels are reciprocal — gNB uses SRS measurements to select the best RX beam and derive the optimal DL TX beam without UE feedback. For FDD: SRS used for scheduling but DL beamforming still relies on CSI feedback since channels are different frequencies.
13.1 Why 5G NR needs explicit beam management
In LTE, beamforming was relatively simple — the gNB would use feedback from the UE (PMI — Precoding Matrix Indicator) to choose a downlink precoding vector. In 5G NR with 64 or 192 antenna elements and 3D beamforming, the beam space is enormous. You need a systematic procedure to find the best beam pair between gNB and UE, track it as the UE moves, and recover when the beam fails. This is the beam management framework.

3GPP defines beam management as three procedures:
P1 — Initial beam sweep (SSB-based): The gNB transmits SSBs in multiple directions (up to 64 SSB beams in FR1). The UE measures RSRP for each SSB beam and reports the best beam index. This establishes the initial beam pair link (BPL). In your OTA chamber, this is the procedure that defines which gNB beam direction the EIRP is measured in.
P2 — Beam refinement (CSI-RS-based): After initial beam is established, gNB sends CSI-RS in a narrower set of directions around the best SSB beam for finer angular resolution. UE reports best CSI-RS beam → gNB refines its TX beam to maximize EIRP toward the UE.
P3 — UE-side beam sweeping: UE sweeps its own RX beams while the gNB holds a fixed beam. Measures which UE RX beam gives best SINR. Both endpoints are now optimized. This complete beam pair is the one used for data transmission.
13.4 TDD channel reciprocity — why B41 is fundamentally different
In TDD systems (B41), the UL and DL use exactly the same frequency. This means the wireless channel experienced by a DL signal is the same as the channel experienced by a UL signal (they're at the same frequency and travel the same path in both directions within the channel coherence time).
The gNB exploits this: it measures the UL channel (using SRS from UE) and uses that channel knowledge directly for DL beamforming without waiting for UE feedback. This is called reciprocity-based beamforming. It requires the gNB's TX and RX hardware paths to be calibrated so that the RF chain itself doesn't introduce phase and amplitude differences between TX and RX — otherwise the "reciprocity" assumption breaks down.
This calibration is done with dedicated RF calibration signals measured internally or via over-the-air calibration between antenna elements. If this calibration drifts (temperature change, component aging), the DL beamforming quality degrades — EVM and EIRP in the intended direction drop, and interference in other directions increases. This is why your B41 validation pays particular attention to EVM and ACLR across temperature, and why firmware builds that change calibration procedures can affect B41 performance without affecting FDD bands.

O-RAN functional split — where your RU sits in the architecture

O-RAN 7.2x split — what each node does:
O-RAN split 7.2x divides the baseband processing between O-DU and O-RU. O-DU handles: MAC scheduling, RLC, High-PHY (channel coding, rate matching, scrambling, resource mapping). O-RU handles: Low-PHY (IFFT/FFT, CP add/remove, precoding/beamforming, PRACH extraction). The FHGW in your system is the O-RU's Low-PHY processor — it receives compressed IQ from the O-DU via eCPRI and generates the final OFDM waveforms for the RFFE.
eCPRI fronthaul — what travels over the fiber:
eCPRI (evolved Common Public Radio Interface) carries IQ data between O-DU and FHGW over Ethernet (10GbE SFP+). The data is BFP compressed: 16-bit IQ samples are compressed to 9-bit mantissa + 4-bit shared exponent per resource block, reducing bandwidth by ~50%. Without compression, a 20 MHz 4T4R system would need: 4×2×12×7×20×16×2 = ~430 Mbps — too much for 10GbE. With BFP compression: ~200 Mbps — fits comfortably.
Why O-RAN matters for your testing:
O-RAN's open interfaces mean the O-RU (your AAU) must interoperate with any compliant O-DU, not just PW's own DU. Your RCT framework tests the O-RU in isolation using PW's own FHGW+DU stack. Conformance testing against 3rd-party DUs requires the O-RU to follow the exact eCPRI timing and BFP compression specs. Any deviation shows up as frame timing errors, RSSI discrepancies between CLI and DU, or PRACH detection failures.
15.1 Traditional RAN vs Open RAN
Traditional (monolithic) RAN: The baseband unit (BBU) and radio unit (RU) are tightly coupled, often from the same vendor. The interface between them is proprietary — you can't mix Nokia BBU with Ericsson RU. This worked but locked operators into single-vendor ecosystems.
Open RAN (O-RAN): The network functions are disaggregated into standardized logical units with open, interoperable interfaces. The O-RAN Alliance defines these interfaces so that any O-RU (your Metro AAU) can connect to any O-DU (Distributed Unit) regardless of vendor.

15.3 Open Fronthaul — what flows between O-DU and O-RU
The Open Fronthaul (eCPRI or O-RAN Fronthaul) carries IQ samples between the O-DU and O-RU over an Ethernet/IP network (10G or 25G fiber). This is fundamentally different from traditional CPRI which carried raw IQ at high, constant bandwidth.
Key fronthaul parameters that affect RF testing:
IQ compression: To reduce fronthaul bandwidth, IQ samples are compressed (BFP — Block Floating Point compression, typically 14-bit → 9-bit). Compression artifacts add quantization noise to the TX signal — this shows up as a small EVM degradation even with a perfect PA. Your EVM spec must account for this compression noise budget.
Timing: The O-RU must transmit each subframe at the correct time. Fronthaul latency and timing jitter can cause frame timing errors. This directly affects test_Tx_time_offset results — if fronthaul latency varies, the subframe timing varies, and time offset measurements will show variation.
Beamforming control: The O-DU sends beamforming weights (antenna port weights) to the O-RU for each beam. Errors in weight delivery cause beam pointing errors → OTA EIRP in the wrong direction → OTA tests fail even though the conducted RF chain is fine.
15.4 What 6G looks like from an RF perspective
You'll be testing 6G hardware before many engineers even understand what 5G NR conformance means. Here's what's coming:
Upper mid-band (7–24 GHz): New spectrum between sub-6 GHz and mmWave. Better propagation than mmWave, wider bandwidth than sub-6. Will require new OTA test methodologies — CATR at 15 GHz needs a different chamber than at 3.5 GHz.
Extreme massive MIMO (>256 elements): More antenna elements → higher beamforming gain → more complex beam management → more beam states to test in conformance.
AI/ML-native air interface: Beam prediction using AI, channel estimation using neural networks, adaptive modulation driven by ML. New KPIs and new test methods required — you'll need to test the AI model's beam prediction accuracy, not just the RF parameters.
Integrated sensing and communication (ISAC): The gNB simultaneously communicates with UEs and senses the environment (radar mode). New RF tests: radar range resolution, velocity resolution, interference between comm and sensing waveforms.

Expert RF topics — PLL/LO, mixers, filters in depth

PLL/LO — how the carrier frequency is generated:
PLL (Phase-Locked Loop) generates the Local Oscillator frequency by locking a VCO (Voltage Controlled Oscillator) to a reference (GPS-locked 10 MHz). The PLL compares the VCO output (divided by N) to the reference and adjusts the VCO until they match. Result: stable carrier frequency with close-in phase noise determined by the reference and loop bandwidth, and far-out noise determined by the VCO. GPS lock gives frequency accuracy of ±50 ppb — essential for TDD synchronisation and carrier frequency accuracy.
Mixer operation — frequency translation:
A mixer multiplies two signals: RF input × LO → outputs at sum (RF+LO) and difference (RF−LO) frequencies. For downconversion: RF = 2140 MHz, LO = 2140 MHz → IF = 0 Hz (direct conversion IQ). For upconversion: IF = 0 + LO = 2140 MHz. The mixer's IIP3 (3rd order intercept point) determines how well it handles strong blockers without generating intermodulation products. IIP3 must be > 20 dB above the strongest input signal to stay in the linear region.
Filter types in your RX chain and what they do:
Pre-BPF (before LNA): rejects out-of-band blockers (strong signals from other systems) before they can compress the LNA. Adds 0.3 dB NF. Worth it because a compressed LNA has much worse NF than 0.3 dB.
Post-BPF (after LNA): rejects image frequency and wideband noise. NF contribution negligible after LNA gain.
Duplexer: specially designed to pass only the RX band while heavily attenuating the TX band (>50 dB isolation) — prevents the high-power TX signal from going directly into the LNA input.
PLL (Phase-Locked Loop) — the LO source
Every mixer in your RU needs a Local Oscillator (LO) at exactly the carrier frequency. The LO is generated by a PLL: a feedback system that locks a Voltage-Controlled Oscillator (VCO) to a precise reference frequency (usually a 10 MHz TCXO or OCXO).
PLL structure: Reference → Phase Detector → Loop Filter → VCO → ÷N divider → Phase Detector (feedback) VCO output frequency = N × Reference frequency To get 1842.5 MHz with 10 MHz reference: N = 184.25 (fractional-N PLL) Phase noise performance: VCO noise + 20·log(N) dB + reference noise
The ÷N multiplication degrades phase noise by 20·log(N) dB. For N=184: 20·log(184) = +45 dB of phase noise degradation vs the reference. This is why crystal oscillator quality matters so much at GHz frequencies — noise is multiplied up.
Your EXA can measure LO phase noise: Mode → Phase Noise Application. Sweep offset from 100 Hz to 40 MHz. A result worse than −85 dBc/Hz at 10 kHz offset will cause measurable LTE EVM degradation.
Mixer theory — frequency translation
A mixer multiplies two signals: RF × LO. The output contains sum and difference frequencies: f_out = f_RF ± f_LO. For downconversion: f_IF = f_RF − f_LO. For direct conversion (zero-IF, used in your RU): f_LO = f_RF, so f_IF = 0 (baseband).
The image frequency problem: in a superhet (non-zero IF) architecture, there exists an image at f_RF_image = f_LO − f_IF that also mixes down to f_IF. An image-reject filter before the mixer must suppress this. In direct conversion (zero-IF), the image is the negative frequency component — handled by the IQ architecture (using I and Q channels to cancel the image mathematically).
Key mixer specs: Conversion gain (how much gain/loss at the IF output), IIP3 (linearity — two-tone IMD), LO-to-RF isolation (how much LO leaks to the RF port — causes DC offset in zero-IF), noise figure (typically 5–10 dB for passive mixers).
IQ imbalance — source of EVM floor
Zero-IF downconversion produces I and Q baseband signals. For perfect IQ demodulation, the I and Q paths must have exactly equal gain and exactly 90° phase difference. In practice there's always some mismatch:
Gain imbalance (ε): I and Q paths have different amplification Phase imbalance (φ): The 90° LO phase split is not exactly 90° Image rejection ratio (IRR) = 20·log₁₀(√((1+ε²+2ε·cosφ) / (1+ε²−2ε·cosφ))) 1° phase error + 0.1 dB gain error → IRR ≈ 40 dB → EVM floor ~1% 3° phase error + 0.5 dB gain error → IRR ≈ 30 dB → EVM floor ~3%
IQ imbalance shows up as EVM that is uniform across all RBs, temperature-dependent, and doesn't improve when you reduce TX power. The calibration firmware in your FHGW measures and corrects IQ imbalance during the calibration routine. If calibration has not run recently, IQ imbalance can be the hidden cause of marginal EVM.

SFDR — Spurious-Free Dynamic Range
SFDR is the range between the signal level and the highest spurious/intermodulation product in the output. It determines how strong a signal can be while still being able to see weak signals in its presence.
SFDR = (2/3) × (IIP3 − Pnoise) For a 12-bit ADC: SFDR ≈ 6.02 × 12 − 1.76 = 70 dB (theoretical) Real ADCs: SFDR typically 3–10 dB below theoretical due to harmonics, clock jitter
SFDR matters most in receiver tests: ACS (adjacent channel selectivity) and blocking tests inject a strong interferer alongside a weak wanted signal. If the receiver's SFDR is insufficient, the interferer creates intermodulation products that land in the wanted channel and cause BLER degradation even though the wanted signal is at the correct level.
🎯 Analogy: PLL is like cruise control in a car

You set the target speed (the reference frequency). The cruise control continuously compares actual speed (VCO output ÷N) to target speed (10 MHz GPS reference). If too fast → eases off throttle (reduces Vtune). If too slow → adds throttle (increases Vtune). The loop filter determines how aggressively it reacts — too aggressive = oscillation, too slow = drifts. A GPS-locked PLL maintains frequency to within ±50 ppb — a 2140 MHz carrier drifts by less than 107 Hz. This is why GPS loss immediately impacts B41 TDD performance.

EXP3 PA Classes A/B/AB/C/F — efficiency vs linearity trade-off in your GaN PA Advanced click to expand▾

PA classes — efficiency vs linearity trade-off

Class A — most linear, least efficient:
Bias point: mid-supply. Conducts for full 360° of input cycle. Gain is perfectly linear (constant). Efficiency: theoretically 25–50%, practically 15–30%. No crossover distortion. Used where linearity is critical and efficiency is secondary — e.g. small-signal driver stages. Your AAU does NOT use pure Class A — too much heat for 20W output.
Class AB — your AAU PA operating class:
Bias point: slightly above cutoff. Conducts for 180–360° of cycle. Combines best features of A and B — reasonable linearity, reasonable efficiency (~35–45% with DPD). The slight nonlinearity at crossover is corrected by DPD. This is the standard choice for cellular base station PAs. GaN technology (your PA material) enables higher efficiency than older GaAs at same output power.
Dynamic Vdd — how supply modulation improves efficiency:
Class AB PA efficiency is highest at peak power and drops at lower power levels. OFDM signals spend most of their time at low-to-medium power levels (high PAPR means peaks are rare). Dynamic Vdd reduces the PA supply voltage during low-power periods, so the PA dissipates less heat even though it's conducting. At 6 dB below peak power, Dynamic Vdd can improve efficiency from 35% to 55% — reducing heat by 30%. This is why your AAU's Dynamic Vdd configuration in the PA registers directly affects both efficiency and junction temperature.
Why PA class matters for your work
The PA in your Metro AAU RFFE is a GaN (Gallium Nitride) transistor biased and operated in a specific class that trades linearity against efficiency. Your PA efficiency test plan (Module 11 / PPT2) directly measures this. Understanding PA classes explains WHY DPD is necessary and WHY dynamic Vdd saves power.

Test equipment uses Class A amplifiers for their excellent linearity. The 50% max efficiency is acceptable for instruments that run from wall power. The GaN PA in your RU runs at 40+ V drain, delivers 20 W RF, and must be as efficient as possible — that's why Doherty at Class AB is used, and why DPD linearizes the nonlinearity.

EXP5 PRACH Detector Internals — noise estimator, correlation peak, TA calculation from ZC root Expert click to expand▾

PRACH detector internals — noise estimator, peak multiplier, false alarm

How PRACH detection decides pass/fail:
The PRACH detector correlates the received UL signal against each of the 64 possible Zadoff-Chu root sequences. For each root, the correlator produces a power profile over the cyclic shift dimension. The peak power is compared to a detection threshold: threshold = measured_noise_floor × peak_multiplier_α. If peak > threshold: PRACH detected. The noise floor is measured from non-PRACH resource blocks in the same subframe.
Peak multiplier α — the key parameter:
α is set by the RU configuration. Low α (e.g. 4 = 6 dB above noise): detects weak UEs, but more false alarms (random noise peaks exceed threshold). High α (e.g. 16 = 12 dB above noise): robust against false alarms, but misses UEs at or below threshold power. Your sensitivity test uses a specific α — the DSA power level is set so that at the rated sensitivity, the signal peak is just above threshold at the standard α value. This is why the sensitivity number is not "noise floor + NF" alone — it includes the peak_multiplier overhead.
False alarm rate — why it matters in deployment:
A false PRACH alarm causes the gNB to send a Random Access Response (RAR) to a nonexistent UE, wasting resources. High false alarm rate degrades cell capacity. Too sensitive α → many false alarms. In your lab: false alarms show up as PRACH detected count exceeding the number of EXM injections (more detections than preambles sent). Normal: detection count = injections × detection_rate. If detection count > injections: false alarm problem — α too low.
The full PRACH detection pipeline
Stage 1 — Front end: Remove CP → apply FFT → extract PRACH subcarriers (839 bins at 1.25 kHz spacing) Stage 2 — Root correlation: For each root u being tested: Correlate received signal with conjugate of root ZC: Y = X × ZC_u* Apply IFFT → get power-delay profile (839 taps, one per possible cyclic shift) Stage 3 — Noise estimation: Take the correlation output after IFFT Form noise vector: all taps EXCEPT the K preamble positions (exclude K windows) Noise_est = mean(noise_vector_power) [average of all "empty" delay taps] Stage 4 — Peak detection: For each of the K preamble windows: Find peak power in that window: P_peak Detection threshold: P_peak / Noise_est > peak_multiplier If exceeded → preamble detected at that cyclic shift Timing advance = peak_position × (1/FFT_size) × symbol_duration
What the noise estimator actually does — intuition
The correlation output has 839 delay taps. For a given root u with ZCZ=12, there are K ≈ 6–7 preamble windows (occupied delay taps) and 839 − K×Ncs ≈ 700+ "empty" taps where no valid preamble should appear. The noise estimator averages power in these empty taps — that average IS the noise floor. The peak multiplier then sets: "if the peak is X times above this noise floor, call it a detection."
The detection threshold trade-off
Higher peak_multiplier: → Fewer false alarms (noise spikes mistaken for preambles) ✓ → More missed detections (real weak preambles not detected) ✗ → Better for dense urban where random UEs cause false alarms Lower peak_multiplier: → More detections of weak preambles ✓ → More false alarms ✗ → Better for large cells where UEs are far away and weak RuAuto test_Rx_PRACH sweeps power and finds the threshold where miss detection rate = 1% This measured threshold must be ≤ PRACH_KPI (e.g. −87 dBm + DSA offset per band)
Timing advance from PRACH — how it works
The delay tap position of the detected peak = propagation delay of UE Round-trip delay = 2 × distance / c For 1 km cell: delay = 2×1000 / 3×10⁸ = 6.67 μs One PRACH delay tap = Ncs × FFT_size / (839 × subcarrier_spacing) = Ncs × 0.001 ms / 839 (for 1.25 kHz format 0 PRACH) Timing Advance command sent to UE = detected delay taps × tap_duration UE then transmits that much earlier so signal arrives aligned at eNB
Connecting PRACH to your test results
In your test_Rx_PRACH output: "timing advance avg/std" shows the measured round-trip delay and its consistency. High std dev = the detector is struggling at the noise floor (inconsistent peak position). Low std dev = clean detection. The miss-detection rate curves you see in the results are exactly the probability of the peak/noise ratio exceeding the threshold at each input power level.

The conversion formula and where the constants come from:
P_dBm = dBFS_reading + ADC_ADJUST. ADC_ADJUST = (total RX chain gain from RU port to ADC register) in dB. This includes: LNA gain (+20 dB), VGA gain (variable, AGC-controlled), mixer conversion gain, ADC full-scale calibration. Rather than tracking all these separately, RSSI cal measures the net offset once and stores it. Example: EXM injects −97.5 dBm at RU port. ADC reads −12.5 dBFS. ADC_ADJUST = −97.5 − (−12.5) = −85. Future readings: P_dBm = dBFS + (−85).
dBFS scale — understanding what each level means:
0 dBFS: ADC output saturated (all bits = 1). This is clipping — measurement invalid.
−12 dBFS: target operating point (12 dB headroom for peaks).
−40 dBFS: low signal — AGC should have increased gain already.
−74 dBFS: quantisation noise floor (12-bit ADC limit).
Below −74 dBFS: signal lost in quantisation noise.
Common conversion errors:
Forgetting cable loss: P_at_port = EXM_output − cable_loss. Using wrong ADC channel: get_rssi 0 is B1 Col A, not B3. ADC_ADJUST drifts with temperature: recalibrate at operating temperature. Ohm's Law to RF — the continuity:
V=IR, P=VI=V²/R=I²R in DC circuits. In RF, impedance Z replaces resistance R and includes reactance (capacitors, inductors). At RF frequencies, lumped components behave differently — a capacitor becomes a short at high freq, an inductor becomes an open. Digital communications — modulation, coding, OFDM basics

From bits to waveform:
Digital comms: bits → symbol mapping (QAM) → pulse shaping → carrier modulation → channel → demodulation → symbol decision → bits. Each step has a quality metric: Symbol Error Rate → Bit Error Rate → Block Error Rate (BLER). 3GPP uses 5% BLER as the sensitivity threshold. The entire DSA framework is calibrating your test to this 5% BLER threshold.
Why OFDM was chosen for LTE:
OFDM converts a wideband channel into many narrow subchannels (15 kHz each). Each subchannel experiences flat fading (not frequency-selective). Frequency-domain equalization becomes trivial — one complex multiply per subcarrier. Guard bands (cyclic prefix) eliminate inter-symbol interference from multipath. The price: high PAPR (10–12 dB) requiring DPD and CFR.
LTE downlink uses OFDMA with QAM modulation on each subcarrier. Uplink uses SC-FDMA (single carrier) to keep UE PAPR low (battery saving). Your RU receives SC-FDMA from UE and transmits OFDMA to UE.

OFDM in your AAU — how it actually works
LTE uses OFDM because multipath fading kills single-carrier systems at high data rates.
Resource Grid: 1ms subframe → 2 slots → 14 OFDM symbols
Subcarrier spacing: 15kHz (4G LTE)
B3 at 20MHz: 100 RBs × 12 subcarriers = 1200 subcarriers
Each subcarrier carries: 1 QAM symbol per OFDM symbol
Cyclic prefix: 5.21μs (normal) — absorbs multipath up to 5.21μs delay spread

Your EXA EVM measurement checks EACH subcarrier's symbol placement vs ideal constellation.
Coding rate and throughput
Throughput = BW × Spectral_Efficiency × Coding_Rate
B3 20MHz 64QAM 3/4 code: 20×10⁶ × 6 × 0.75 = 90Mbps (theoretical peak)
In practice: pilot overhead, CP, guard bands → peak ~75Mbps per 4T4R
With beamforming (4T4R): multiply by spatial streams = up to 300Mbps downlink
What bad modulation looks like on EXA
IQ imbalance: Constellation is rotated/scaled asymmetrically → EVM increases uniformly
Phase noise: Symbols form circular clouds instead of tight clusters → especially bad at high SNR
Clipping (CFR): Symbols near constellation edges are pulled inward → EVM worse for high-power symbols
DPD failure: Spectral regrowth visible in ACLR measurement, EVM degrades on outer subcarriers
LO leakage: Large spike at DC subcarrier in constellation view
Interview answers
Q: Why does 64QAM need better EVM than QPSK? A: 64QAM has 64 decision regions vs QPSK's 4. The minimum distance between symbols is much smaller (0.155 vs 0.707 normalized), so any IQ error is more likely to cross a decision boundary and cause a bit error.

Q: What is the cyclic prefix for? A: Absorbs inter-symbol interference from multipath. The CP (5.21μs) must be longer than the delay spread of the channel. If a reflected signal arrives within 5.21μs of the direct path, the FFT at the receiver still sees a clean circular convolution.

Q: Your EVM test shows 3.5% on ETM 3.1 — is this a pass? A: Yes for Metro/FHGW internal spec (≤3.5%). Yes for 3GPP spec (≤8%). But you have no margin. Anything above 3.5% fails the Metro spec.

The 5 measurements you run most often:

O-RAN RF — O-RU architecture, M/C/U/S planes, fronthaul timing

O-RAN planes — M/C/U/S:
M-plane (Management): configuration, SW upgrade, fault management via NETCONF/YANG. How you configure bands, power, calibration. C-plane (Control): scheduling information — which RBs active, beam index, timing. Latency sensitive (<100μs). U-plane (User): IQ data — the actual signal samples. Very high bandwidth. S-plane (Sync): timing distribution — PTP IEEE 1588 from DU to RU. Required for TDD timing.
Fronthaul timing — why ±1.5μs matters for TDD:
TDD requires all gNBs in a network to switch from DL to UL at exactly the same time. If one RU switches 5μs late, its DL transmission overlaps with another cell's UL reception → interference. PTP (Precision Time Protocol) distributes timing from GPS-locked grandmaster clock to each RU via Ethernet. Required accuracy: ±1.5μs end-to-end. GPS holdover (when GPS signal lost): clock must maintain ±1.5μs for several hours.

RF impairments — IQ imbalance, LO leakage, phase noise, compression

IQ imbalance — cause and effect:
I and Q paths have slightly different gain or phase due to component mismatch. Effect: constellation rotates and compresses → EVM increases. On EXA constellation display: asymmetric clusters (amplitude imbalance) or rotated clusters (phase imbalance). Spec: IQ amplitude balance < 0.5 dB, IQ phase imbalance < 1°. Root cause: analog baseband circuit tolerance in the DAC/mixer.
LO leakage — DC offset in IQ:
If the LO signal leaks to the output, it appears as a large carrier at the centre frequency. In the IQ constellation: all symbols offset from origin. In spectrum: spike at band centre frequency (DC offset in baseband maps to carrier frequency after upconversion). Spec: LO leakage < −30 dBc. Causes: mixer imbalance, LO feedthrough, ground bounce in analog baseband.
Compression — PA P1dB impact:
As input power approaches P1dB, PA gain drops. In the constellation: all points compress inward toward origin (amplitude compression). In spectrum: ACLR increases (spectral regrowth). EVM worsens. DPD is designed to operate right at P1dB and compensate, but if DPD model is wrong or not converged, compression shows through.

L21 Timing Advance — LTE TA, NR TA, cell radius, propagation delay L21 click to expand▾

Timing Advance — why every UE must transmit early

Why TA exists:
All UEs must arrive at the gNB at exactly the same time within the subframe window. A UE 10 km away has 33 μs propagation delay — if it transmitted at the subframe boundary, its signal arrives late and collides with the next UE. TA tells the UE to advance its transmission by 2× the propagation delay (round trip). In your lab: EXM cable ≈ 0 propagation → TA = 0. PRACH sensitivity tests still work because there is no real propagation to compensate.
How TA is measured:
When UE sends a PRACH preamble, gNB measures arrival time of correlation peak vs expected time. Offset = propagation delay. gNB sends RAR (Random Access Response) with TA value. UE advances UL timing by that amount. LTE TA granularity: 0.52 μs per step. Max TA: 0.67 ms → 100 km theoretical cell radius. TA continuously updated during call as UE moves.
NR TA vs LTE TA:
NR TA granularity is finer (0.5 μs) and includes a TA offset per carrier for multi-carrier operation. NR also introduces a TA timer — if the UE does not receive a TA update within the timer, it falls back to PRACH to re-establish timing. This is more robust but means PRACH sensitivity matters more in NR than LTE.

Beamforming — analog, digital, hybrid; your 4T4R AAU

How your 4T4R AAU does beamforming:
4 TX columns (A/B/C/D), each connected to a separate PA. Phase shifter (PE44820DS) applied per column digitally. Baseband beamforming weights computed by O-DU scheduler based on UE channel feedback (CSI-RS/SRS measurements). Phase weights sent to FHGW → applied to each column's IQ stream before DAC. Result: constructive interference in desired UE direction, destructive in other directions.
MRC on the RX side — how 4 columns combine:
Maximum Ratio Combining weights each column's received signal by its own SNR quality. Column with strong LNA and clean cable gets high weight. Degraded column gets low weight automatically. Algebraically: output SNR = Σ(SNR_i) for all columns i. For 4 equal columns: output SNR = 4× single column SNR = +6 dB. This is why combined sensitivity is always 5–6 dB better than single-column sensitivity.
Beamforming gain in the link budget:
Beamforming gain (TX): for 4-column phased array, beam gain ≈ 10·log(4) = +6 dBi over isotropic per column = +6 dB EIRP improvement. With 4 columns transmitting coherently: total EIRP = single_column_power + 4_column_BF_gain = +43 dBm + 6 dB = +49 dBm EIRP (excluding antenna element gain). Antenna element gain adds another 8–12 dBi. Total EIRP can reach +60 dBm for a 4T4R AAU with panel antenna.

L23 MIMO — SISO to Massive MIMO, SU-MIMO, MU-MIMO, spatial multiplexing L23 ⭐⭐⭐⭐ click to expand▾

MIMO — how multiple antennas multiply capacity

Spatial multiplexing — the core idea:
MIMO creates multiple independent spatial channels between gNB and UE using multipath scattering. Each channel carries a separate data stream. A 4×4 MIMO system can carry 4 independent streams simultaneously on the same frequency — quadrupling capacity without extra bandwidth. Richer scattering environment (indoor, urban) = better MIMO performance. Open field = poor scattering = MIMO gain disappears.
Your 4T4R AAU — MIMO in practice:
4 TX columns (A/B/C/D) with 4 PAs + 4 LNAs. TX side: typically used for beamforming (all 4 columns phase-shifted to steer beam in one direction) rather than 4-stream spatial multiplexing. RX side: MRC combining across 4 columns gives +6 dB diversity gain. Full 4-stream SU-MIMO requires UE to have 4 receive antennas — rare on phones, common on CPE. Typical: 2-stream MIMO per UE + 4T4R for beamforming gain.
MRC diversity gain — why dead RX columns matter:
Maximum Ratio Combining weights each column by its SNR. Output SNR = sum of all column SNRs. For 4 equal columns: output SNR = 4× single column = +6 dB. Each dead RX column loses 1.5-2 dB of MRC gain. In your unit 26050510: B1C, B1D dead, B3D dead → 2 columns lost for B1 = −3 dB sensitivity degradation. This is why individual column RX failures directly affect sensitivity test results.

Temperature testing — RF performance changes with heat and cold

Why temperature changes RF performance:
GaN PA transconductance decreases at high temperature → gain drops (typically −0.02 dB/°C). At cold: gain increases, risk of over-power. DPD coefficients calibrated at room temperature become invalid at extremes — the PA nonlinearity profile changes, so pre-distortion no longer matches → ACLR worsens. RSSI readings drift because ADC_ADJUST was calibrated at a specific temperature. All of these effects require TEMP_COMP (temperature compensation) tables in hardware.json.
Classic interview scenario — RSSI fails at +55°C:
RSSI cal done at +25°C → TEMP_COMP table missing → LNA gain drifts −0.5 dB at 55°C → ADC_ADJUST is now wrong by 0.5 dB → get_rssi reads 0.5 dB low → exceeds ±2 dB limit. Fix: run RSSI cal at both 25°C and 55°C, measure temperature coefficient, populate TEMP_COMP table. This exact scenario appears in real product qualification and in interviews at Ericsson, Nokia, and similar companies.
3GPP temperature test points:
3GPP TS 36.141 Annex C defines test conditions: Normal (25°C ±5°C), Extreme low (−30°C for indoor, −40°C for outdoor), Extreme high (+55°C for indoor, +55°C for outdoor with solar radiation add). All conformance tests must pass at all temperature conditions. The RU must maintain all specs across the full range — not just at room temperature. Qualification testing requires running the full RCT at each temperature point, typically 3-4 days of testing per temperature point.

Environmental testing — the RU must survive its deployment environment

Why env testing is separate from RF testing:
An RU might pass all RF specs in a clean lab at 25°C but fail in deployment due to: condensation shorting PCB traces (humidity), connector corrosion from sea air (salt fog), solder joint cracking from thermal expansion cycles. Environmental tests simulate the full deployment lifecycle — 10-15 years of outdoor exposure — in 50-200 hours of accelerated aging. Thermal cycling — the most common long-term failure mode:

L30 Fronthaul Transport — eCPRI, Ethernet, VLAN, QoS: how IQ data travels BBU↔RU L30 ⭐⭐⭐ click to expand▾

Transport — the network path that carries your RF signal

eCPRI fronthaul — strict latency requirements:
eCPRI carries IQ data between O-RU and O-DU over Ethernet. C-plane (control: scheduling, timing) requires < 100 μs one-way latency. If fronthaul latency exceeds the budget: IQ data arrives too late for IFFT → dropped symbols → BLER increases → throughput drops. VLAN and QoS — why misconfiguration causes intermittent RF failures:
eCPRI, PTP, and NETCONF share the same Ethernet network. Without QoS: a network burst can delay PTP timing packets → TDD sync loss → B41 DL/UL collision. VLAN separates traffic types. QoS assigns highest priority (DSCP EF) to eCPRI and PTP. Misconfigured QoS is a common field issue causing intermittent TDD frame timing errors that are impossible to reproduce in lab without load on the transport network.
Fronthaul vs backhaul — key distinction:
Fronthaul: O-RU ↔ O-DU, eCPRI, < 100 μs, < 20 km. Midhaul: O-DU ↔ O-CU, MAC/RLC PDUs, less latency-sensitive. Backhaul: O-CU ↔ Core, standard IP, tolerates higher latency. In field troubleshooting: if PRACH detection fails intermittently, check fronthaul packet loss with tcpdump on DU eCPRI interface before assuming RU hardware fault.

RF safety — protecting people and passing regulatory requirements

SAR vs MPE — two different metrics for two different products:

Tested in anechoic chamber with spectrum analyser measuring radiated emissions from RU in all directions.

In your bench: +43 dBm = 20W of RF power at antenna port. At close range: RF burns to skin, hazardous to eyes. Always use: dummy load on unused antenna ports, 30 dB attenuator between BOB SMA and EXA, shielded cables. The BOB SMA output at +13 dBm (before pad) is still above comfortable unprotected handling levels. The 30 dB pad is not optional — it protects both you and the EXA input.

L43 Interview scenarios — 8 systematic debug walkthroughs L43 ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ click to expand▾

Interview scenarios — walk through these before every RF test engineer interview

Scenario 1: ACLR degrades after software update
Step 1: Is measurement valid? Attenuation correct, preamp OFF, DPD converged (60s wait)? Step 2: Reproduce on second unit — systematic or unit-specific? Step 3: Diff the SW update — what changed? DPD algorithm? CFR threshold? DAC scale? Step 4: Roll back FW on one unit — does ACLR recover? Step 5: If rollback fixes: update changed DPD or CFR parameter. Check DPD_ENABLE, CFR_CLIP_LEVEL in changelogs. Root cause: usually CFR threshold changed → larger peaks → overdriving PA.
Scenario 2: RSSI accuracy fails only at high temperature
Step 1: Confirm failure is reproducible at +55°C. Step 2: Check TEMP_COMP table in hardware.json — populated? If empty: temperature compensation disabled → RSSI drifts. Step 3: Measure RSSI at +25°C and +55°C with same EXM injection → compute drift = X dB/°C. Step 4: Calculate required TEMP_COMP coefficient. Step 5: Run RSSI cal at both temperatures, update table. Root cause: TEMP_COMP table not calibrated or not loaded at boot.
Scenario 3: PA enters protection after 20 minutes
Step 1: Which protection? Check fault register: over-temperature vs over-current vs VSWR. Step 2: If over-temperature: thermal camera on PA, check ambient > 55°C, heatsink contact, fan. Step 3: If VSWR: VNA on antenna port — is load present? Step 4: If over-current: check PA bias vs spec, check if unit is over-driven. Monitor temperature over time — steady rise until threshold = thermal compound deficiency. Root cause in 70% of cases: thermal management, not electrical.
Scenario 4: Dynamic range fails B3 but passes B1
Step 1: Confirm back-to-back with same setup. Step 2: Check ADC channel mapping — correct ADC for B3? (ch 2/3/6/7, not ch 0/1/4/5 for B1). Step 3: Check ADC_ADJUST_B3 — calibrated correctly? Step 4: Sweep EXM −60 to −110 dBm for both bands, plot RSSI vs injected power — compare curves. Parallel but offset: ADC_ADJUST_B3 wrong → recalibrate. Different slope: hardware fault in LNA or mixer at B3 frequency. Root cause: most likely wrong ADC_ADJUST_B3.

Scenario 5: TX power passes at room temp but fails at +55°C
Step 1: Confirm failure is reproducible at temp (not a transient). Step 2: Check PA thermal protection status — did it fire? Root cause: usually missing TX gain temperature compensation.
Scenario 6: B41 PRACH detection fails intermittently — B1/B3/B8/B28 all pass
B41 is TDD — immediately suspect timing/sync. Step 1: Check DU PTP status — is it LOCKED? Even a brief GPS signal loss can cause PTP to enter holdover. Step 2: Check PTP offset over time — is it drifting beyond ±1.5 μs? Step 3: If PTP is fine: check EXA gating — are you measuring only during UL subframes? B41 PRACH arrives only in UL slots. Step 4: If gating is correct: verify EXM is synchronised to TDD frame — injection must be in correct UL subframe. Step 5: Check fronthaul latency under load — if transport QoS is misconfigured, PTP packets get delayed under traffic → sync degrades → intermittent B41 issues. Root cause: almost always PTP sync or EXA/EXM gating setup.
Scenario 7: After PA replacement, ACLR fails but TX power is correct
ACLR fail with correct power = PA is on but DPD is not working for the new PA. Step 1: Was DPD allowed to converge? PA replacement resets DPD coefficients — wait 60s after enabling TX. Step 2: If still failing after convergence: the new PA has different nonlinearity than the DPD model was trained on. The DPD coefficients calibrated for the old PA do not match the new one. Step 3: Force DPD re-adaptation — some systems require explicit re-trigger after hardware change. Step 4: Check if DPD algorithm converged at all (DPD_STATUS register). Step 5: If DPD_STATUS shows converged but ACLR still fails: PA bias may be different — check bias voltage matches spec for new PA part number. Root cause: DPD model not re-adapted after PA replacement.
Scenario 8: RSSI reads 6 dB lower than expected on one specific column
6 dB is exactly 1 bit in a 12-bit ADC — suspicious of BFP error. Step 1: Is it exactly 6 dB or approximately? If exactly 6 dB: BFP decompression fault (mantissa/exponent mismatch) → check FHGW firmware version, check eCPRI error counters. Step 2: If approximately 6 dB: check cable for that column (swap with known-good cable). Step 3: Run RSSI across all B1 columns — is it only Column X or all columns? If only one column: hardware fault on that column (LNA or cable). If all columns: ADC_ADJUST wrong for that band → re-calibrate. Step 4: Verify ADC channel mapping — is the correct get_rssi N being read for that column? (B3 Col A = get_rssi 2, not 0). Step 5: If column reads correct with EXM at full power but wrong at low power: LNA may be compressed by a strong blocker nearby during the test. Root cause: check in this order — ADC channel mapping → cable swap → LNA health → BFP error.

PROP01 Free Space Path Loss (FSPL) — why low bands cover further Propagation click to expand +

The baseline loss just from distance + frequency · explains why B28/B8 give coverage and B41 gives capacity

Formula & worked numbers
FSPL(dB) = 20·log10(d) + 20·log10(f) + 32.44 (d in km, f in MHz) B1 @ 1 km: 20log(1) + 20log(2140) + 32.44 = 0 + 66.6 + 32.44 = 99 dB B28 @ 1 km: 20log(1) + 20log(758) + 32.44 = 0 + 57.6 + 32.44 = 90 dB => B28 sees ~9 dB LESS path loss than B1 at the same range

PROP02 Fading — slow (shadow), fast (multipath), channel models Propagation click to expand +

Two time-scales of signal variation — and the 3GPP channel models (EPA/EVA/ETU) you'd use to reproduce them

Slow vs fast fading
Slow / shadow fading — large obstacles (buildings, hills). Log-normal, std dev 6–10 dB, coherence distance tens–hundreds of metres. Handled by a shadow-fade margin (~8–10 dB) in the link budget.
Fast / multipath fading — many reflected paths combine. Rayleigh (no LOS) or Rician (with LOS). Coherence time is in the ms range at vehicular speed; coherence bandwidth ≈ 1/delay-spread (~200 kHz urban).

ModelDelay spreadUse
EPA~43 nsExtended Pedestrian A — mild

ETU~5 µsExtended Typical Urban — worst case
Lab context
Your cabled bench is an AWGN channel — no fading, which is exactly why RI stays at 1 (see CSI03): no multipath to decorrelate MIMO layers. To test fading you need a channel emulator (e.g. PropSim) playing EPA/EVA/ETU; ETU (5 µs delay spread) is the stress case for equalizer/CP robustness.
Interview Q&A
Q: Rayleigh vs Rician? Rayleigh = no dominant/LOS path (deep fades common); Rician = a strong LOS component plus scatter (shallower fades), described by the K-factor.
Q: Why doesn't your bench show rank-2? AWGN cable = no multipath richness → channel matrix is effectively rank-1; you need a fading emulator to see spatial multiplexing.

Movement shifts frequency; too much Doppler breaks OFDM subcarrier orthogonality (ICI)

Formula & worked numbers
