5G NR Non-Standalone (EN-DC) — dual connectivity anchored on LTE · plain-English + how-it-works + interview Q&A
Public-knowledge write-ups of 5G NSA / EN-DC features (LTE anchor + NR leg on the EPC). Concepts only, no internal specifics. Each card feeds the Interview simulator.
NN01 EN-DC / Non-Standalone Overview 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
The fast path to 5G: keep the LTE core and anchor, add a 5G leg for extra throughput. This is what "NSA" means.
How it works
In EN-DC (E-UTRA–NR Dual Connectivity) the LTE eNB is the Master Node (MeNB) anchoring control/signalling to the EPC; the gNB is the Secondary Node (SgNB) adding an NR user-plane leg. The UE is connected to both simultaneously.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is NSA / EN-DC and why did operators start there?
A: NSA (EN-DC) attaches a 5G NR secondary node to an existing LTE anchor and EPC, so the LTE cell handles mobility/signalling while NR boosts throughput. It let operators launch 5G data without first deploying a 5G core — faster and cheaper than standalone.
NN02 SgNB Addition Procedure 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
The signalling that bolts the 5G leg onto an existing LTE connection.
How it works
The LTE UE reports NR measurements (event B1); the MeNB sends SgNB Addition Request over X2-C; the SgNB admits resources and returns its NR RRC config; the MeNB forwards it to the UE in an RRCConnectionReconfiguration; the UE does RACH to the gNB and confirms — now dual-connected.
Call flow — EN-DC SgNB Addition (message sequence)
■ request/forward · ■ response/ack · ■ random access. Solid = uplink/forward, dashed = downlink config.
Interview Q&A
Q: Walk the SgNB addition in EN-DC.
A: The UE sends a B1 measurement report (NR above threshold) to the MeNB. MeNB → SgNB Addition Request (X2AP) with UE capabilities/QoS. SgNB admits, allocates NR resources, replies with its RRC config. MeNB relays it to the UE (RRC reconfiguration). UE performs random access to the gNB and sends reconfiguration complete → the NR leg is active.
NN03 Measurement-Based CA & EN-DC 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Add the 5G leg (and extra carriers) only when the UE actually measures good NR — not blindly.
How it works
The eNB configures NR/inter-freq measurements; when the reported quality crosses the configured threshold (B1/A4-type events), it triggers SgNB addition or SCell activation, and removes them when quality drops — keeping links only where they help.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why gate EN-DC/SCell addition on measurements?
A: Adding an NR leg or SCell that the UE can't sustain wastes signalling and can hurt throughput/battery. Triggering on measurement events (e.g. B1 for NR) ensures the extra carrier is added only when the UE genuinely sees usable quality, and dropped when it fades.
NN04 NSA Packet Split (Split Bearer) 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Send one data flow down both the LTE and NR legs at once, splitting/recombining at a single anchor point for more throughput.
How it works
In EN-DC the PDCP layer (at the SgNB for a typical SCG-split bearer) splits DL traffic across the LTE and NR RLC legs and reorders on receipt. Bearer types: MCG (LTE only), SCG (NR only), and split (both).
Interview Q&A
Q: What are the EN-DC bearer types and what does split bearer do?
A: MCG bearer uses only the LTE leg, SCG bearer only the NR leg, and split bearer uses both — a single PDCP entity distributes PDUs over the LTE and NR RLC paths and reorders at the receiver. Split bearer aggregates throughput but needs PDCP reordering to handle the two legs' different delays.
NN05 NSA Data Forwarding 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Don't lose in-flight packets when the 5G secondary node changes or is released.
How it works
On SgNB change/release the source forwards buffered PDCP data to the target (or back to the MeNB) over X2-U so the flow continues; PDCP sequence numbers keep ordering and avoid duplicates.
Interview Q&A
Q: When is data forwarding needed in NSA?
A: On SgNB change or release (and at split-bearer reconfiguration), packets buffered on the NR leg would otherwise be lost. The source forwards outstanding PDCP PDUs to the new node or the master over X2-U, and PDCP sequencing preserves order and removes duplicates.
NN06 NSA + Carrier Aggregation Simultaneously 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Run LTE carrier aggregation and the NR leg at the same time for maximum aggregate throughput.
How it works
The UE aggregates multiple LTE carriers (PCell + SCells) under the MeNB and carries NR SCG carriers under the SgNB concurrently, subject to its band-combination capability and power/processing limits.
Interview Q&A
Q: What limits how many carriers a UE can run across LTE CA + EN-DC?
A: The UE's supported band combinations and its baseband/RF and power budget. Each added carrier costs processing and (for UL) transmit power; the UE advertises the legal combinations in its capability, and the network must stay within them.
NN07 NSA with VoLTE 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
In NSA, voice stays on LTE (VoLTE) while NR just boosts data — because there's no 5G core for voice.
How it works
The LTE anchor keeps the IMS/VoLTE bearers (QCI 1/5); the NR SCG carries best-effort data. If NR power/coverage would harm the voice call, the network can drop the NR leg to protect VoLTE.
Interview Q&A
Q: How is voice handled in NSA?
A: As VoLTE on the LTE anchor — NSA has no 5G core, so voice uses the existing EPC/IMS path (QCI 1). The NR secondary node only adds data throughput; the network may release the NR leg if it threatens the voice bearer's quality or the UE's power budget.
NN08 5G Cell Change (SgNB Change) 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Move the NR leg from one gNB/cell to another as the UE moves, without disturbing the LTE anchor.
How it works
On NR measurement events the MeNB coordinates an SgNB Change (release old + add new SgNB) over X2, with data forwarding; the LTE MCG connection stays put, so mobility of the 5G leg is transparent to the anchor.
Interview Q&A
Q: How does NR-leg mobility work in NSA without dropping the connection?
A: The LTE MeNB stays the anchor; only the secondary node changes via SgNB change (add target, release source) triggered by NR measurements, with PDCP data forwarding. Because control/mobility is anchored on LTE, the 5G leg can be swapped underneath without a full handover.
NN09 NSA & SA on the Same NR Cell 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
Let one NR cell serve both NSA (EN-DC) UEs and native SA UEs at the same time.
How it works
The gNB cell advertises support for both modes and connects to both the EPC (via the LTE anchor over X2) and the 5GC (via NG), scheduling NSA and SA UEs on shared PHY resources — easing migration from NSA to SA.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why run NSA and SA on the same NR cell?
A: It lets operators migrate gradually: legacy EN-DC handsets keep working via the LTE anchor while newer SA-capable devices use the 5G core on the same carrier — no separate NR deployment per mode. The gNB must terminate both X2 (to the eNB/EPC) and NG (to the 5GC).
NN10 4G–5G X2 Interface (EN-DC) 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
The interface that ties the LTE anchor and the 5G secondary node together in NSA.
How it works
EN-DC extends X2 with EN-DC-specific control (X2-C: SgNB add/modify/release, coordination) and X2-U user-plane forwarding for split/SCG bearers between the eNB and en-gNB.
Interview Q&A
Q: What role does X2 play in EN-DC?
A: It connects the MeNB (LTE) and the en-gNB (NR). X2-C carries the SgNB addition/modification/release and coordination signalling; X2-U carries user-plane data forwarding for SCG/split bearers. It's the backbone that makes dual connectivity work.
NN11 SA vs NSA — architecture at a glance (animated) 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
The one-picture difference: in NSA (EN-DC, Option 3) the LTE eNB is the master anchored to the 4G core (EPC), with the NR gNB bolted on as a secondary leg. In SA (Option 2) the gNB stands alone on the 5G core (5GC) — no LTE anchor. The moving dots below trace the active control/user paths.
The one-line difference
NSA: LTE eNB (master) + NR en-gNB (secondary) → EPC (4G core); interfaces S1, X2, S1-U. LTE remains the control anchor. SA: gNB alone → 5GC; interfaces N2 (gNB↔AMF), N3 (gNB↔UPF), N11 (AMF↔SMF), N4 (SMF↔UPF), N6 (UPF↔DN). No LTE anchor. It's a core-network difference, not a speed ranking.
Interview Q&A
Q: In one sentence, what changes between NSA and SA?
A: The core: NSA keeps the LTE eNB as the master anchored to the EPC (4G core) with NR as a secondary leg (EN-DC), while SA runs the gNB standalone on the 5G core (5GC) with no LTE anchor — so it's a core/anchoring difference, not a raw-speed difference.
Q: Which interfaces connect the gNB to the 5G core in SA?
A: N2 carries control (gNB↔AMF) and N3 carries user data (gNB↔UPF). Inside the core, N11 links AMF↔SMF, N4 links SMF↔UPF, and N6 connects UPF↔the data network. In NSA the equivalents are S1/X2/S1-U to the EPC.
Spectrum sharing, resilience & accessibility
DSS01 Dynamic Spectrum Sharing (DSS) — LTE and NR on one carrier: CRS rate matching, MBSFN, scheduling & overhead 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
An operator has LTE on a band and wants 5G there too, but most phones are still LTE-only. Switching the whole band to NR would strand those phones; splitting it in half wastes spectrum when one side is idle. Dynamic Spectrum Sharing (DSS) lets LTE and NR live on the same carrier at the same time. Every millisecond a shared scheduler decides which resource blocks go to LTE and which go to NR, based on demand. It is like a motorway lane that the traffic system gives to buses or cars minute by minute. The price is overhead: NR has to step around LTE's always-on signals.
How NR fits into an LTE subframe on a DSS carrier
LTE CRS positions depend on the LTE PCI (v-shift) and the number of CRS ports. NR SSB is typically placed in MBSFN subframes or on symbols free of CRS.
Level 1 — how DSS works
Same numerology. NR on a DSS carrier uses 15 kHz SCS so its subcarriers and 1 ms slots align with LTE's grid. The carrier is almost always FDD, where LTE is deployed today.

Key mechanisms (Rel-15, extended in Rel-16):
MechanismWhat it does
LTE CRS rate matchingThe gNB tells the NR UE where the LTE CRS REs are (lte-CRS-ToMatchAround: LTE carrier centre, bandwidth, CRS ports, v-shift, MBSFN pattern). NR PDSCH skips those REs. UE capability: rateMatchingLTE-CRS. Rel-16 allows several CRS patterns (for example LTE CA).

Shared or coordinated schedulereNB and gNB schedulers exchange load every TTI (easiest when both run in the same baseband, see CM09) and split PRBs per subframe/slot.
Allocation policies: LTE-priority, NR-priority, proportional to load/buffer, or operator thresholds (for example a minimum guaranteed share per RAT). LTE-only UEs keep working unchanged: they simply see a lightly-used LTE carrier.
Level 2 — overhead, deployment choices and what to test
Overhead. CRS rate matching alone removes LTE CRS REs from NR: 16 of 168 REs per RB pair with 2 CRS ports (≈ 9.5 %), 24 of 168 with 4 ports (≈ 14 %). Add LTE's PDCCH symbols, NR's own SSB/CSI-RS/TRS and any MBSFN subframes, and NR on DSS gives noticeably less capacity than the same bandwidth of dedicated NR. Vendors quote different net figures, so measure it on your configuration.

Typical deployments. Low and mid FDD bands with existing LTE (for example 600/700/800/850/900 MHz, 1800, 1900/AWS, 2100). The aim is fast wide-area NR coverage, often as the NR anchor band for SA or as an EN-DC leg. Mid-band TDD NR (n77/n78/n79) is nearly always greenfield NR spectrum, so DSS there is unusual. Some study notes list n78/n79 as typical DSS bands, which is misleading.

NSA and SA. DSS works with EN-DC (NR leg on the DSS carrier, LTE anchor on the same or another carrier) and with SA (NR SA cell on the DSS carrier; see NN09 for NSA+SA on one NR cell). With EN-DC on the same carrier, both legs compete for the same PRBs, so aggregate throughput is close to that of one carrier, not two.

Level 3 — expert notes and pitfalls
PCI interaction. The LTE CRS v-shift = LTE PCI mod 6 (effectively mod 3 with 2+ ports). The NR rate-matching pattern must follow it, and neighbour LTE cells' CRS still interferes with NR data REs. PCI planning (OPT01) for DSS therefore considers both RATs.

Coordination latency. If the eNB and gNB are separate boxes, the load exchange takes time and the split becomes semi-static. Co-located schedulers (the same BBU, CM09) allow true per-TTI sharing.

MBSFN trade-off. More MBSFN subframes mean cleaner NR (no CRS in the data region, room for SSB) but fewer normal LTE subframes, and CRS-dependent LTE transmission modes suffer. Operators tune the MBSFN pattern as LTE traffic declines.

Migration path. Phase 1: LTE-dominant DSS for NR coverage. Phase 2: shift the share towards NR as NR devices grow. Phase 3: refarm to NR-only when LTE traffic is small, removing the CRS overhead completely.

Related 5G spectrum tools (for context): BWP (bandwidth parts: up to 4 configured per direction, 1 active, switched by DCI or timer) can confine a UE to part of a wide carrier for power saving; mmWave FR2 bands (n257 26.5–29.5 GHz, n258 24.25–27.5, n260 37–40, n261 27.5–28.35) carry capacity with up to 400 MHz channels. Neither is DSS, but all three are ways operators stretch spectrum during the LTE-to-NR transition.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is DSS and why do operators use it?
A: Dynamic Spectrum Sharing runs LTE and NR on the same carrier, with a scheduler dividing resources between them every subframe/slot. It gives fast NR coverage on existing LTE spectrum without stranding LTE-only phones or refarming.
Q: How does an NR UE avoid LTE's reference signals on a DSS carrier?
A: The gNB signals the LTE CRS pattern (lte-CRS-ToMatchAround: centre, bandwidth, ports, v-shift, MBSFN subframes). NR PDSCH is rate-matched around those REs. NR PDCCH is placed after LTE's control symbols, and SSB goes in MBSFN or CRS-free positions.
Q: What is the main cost of DSS?
A: Overhead. CRS rate matching alone costs about 9.5 % (2 ports) to 14 % (4 ports) of REs, plus the LTE control region and MBSFN impacts. NR capacity on DSS is lower than on the same bandwidth of dedicated spectrum, and scheduling is more complex.
Q: Why is DSS unusual on n78?
A: n78/n79 are mostly new TDD spectrum with no LTE to share with. DSS is mostly used on FDD bands where LTE is already deployed.
Q: How would you verify a DSS cell in the lab?
A: Check NR BLER and throughput with LTE CRS present, confirm LTE UE performance is unchanged, load each RAT separately and together to see the PRB split move, and confirm the SSB is found at the expected GSCN and subframes.
ZT4 NSA Resilience Tests — pod/process crashes, EN-DC X2 teardown and recovery, NSA↔NSA handover 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
In 5G NSA the phone talks to an LTE anchor and a 5G booster at the same time, glued together by the EN-DC X2 link. Resilience tests ask a simple question: if one software piece crashes, does the system tear the 5G leg down cleanly and build it back by itself? The suite deliberately kills pods and processes on a Kubernetes-based 4G+5G setup — the control-plane aggregator, the eNB, the CU, the DU, the PHY, fronthaul, timing — and then checks in the captures that the right teardown messages appeared, the links were re-established, the UE got its 5G leg back and data flowed again. NT4 is the matching troubleshooting card for when the 5G leg does not come back.

How the injection is done. Pod faults: kubectl delete pod $(kubectl get pods -n pw | grep <name> …) on the controller (cpanode, bbu, phy, ptp, fh, netcon, dunode). Process faults: find the PID with ps inside the pod and send kill -11 (SIGSEGV, so the crash handler and core dump path are exercised too). Node faults: ACS reboot of the eNB or CU. Then the UE is detached and re-attached (flight mode), traffic restarted, and the test waits a fixed 250-700 s before collecting evidence.

Pass criteria. Every expected message is found in the right capture (a missing one fails with NO_PACKETS); the cells returned In-Service; and DL IN ≥ 1 and UL IN ≥ 1 in the CU PDCP log — i.e. the NR leg carried user data again after recovery. The NSA HO case additionally needs iperf ≥ 50 Mbps × 0.1 and the ENDC SgNB addition counters above.

Reading the X2AP procedure codes used: 4 SN Status Transfer, 6 X2 Setup, 17 X2AP Message Transfer (CPA encapsulation), 27 SgNB Addition Preparation, 28 SgNB Reconfiguration Completion, 31 MeNB-initiated SgNB Release, 32 SgNB-initiated SgNB Release, 36 EN-DC X2 Setup, 37 EN-DC Configuration Update, 43 EN-DC X2 Removal.
Level 3 — what to watch for when you run or extend these tests
1. The double procedureCode filter is not a bug. Filters like (x2ap.procedureCode == 17) && (x2ap.procedureCode == 28) look impossible, but the SgNB Reconfiguration Complete travels inside an X2AP Message Transfer through the CPA, so one packet holds both codes. The same is true for the "time-critical-handover" HO Request in the NSA HO case.
2. Weak data gate. DL IN ≥ 1 proves that some data reached the CU, not that throughput recovered. For a real recovery KPI compare post-recovery throughput with the baseline and measure the outage time (first ABORT → first NR data).
3. Fixed sleeps hide recovery time. A 700 s wait passes whether recovery took 40 s or 690 s. Polling for In-Service and for the EN-DC X2 Setup timestamp would turn each case into a recovery-time measurement.
4. Make sure the fault really happened. Injection steps run with warn on failure; if the kubectl or kill command fails the test can still pass without any fault. The CPA process cases re-check the PID; the pod cases should also check the pod age/restart count.
5. Result flag bug to know about. In one suite the post-test keyword sets the "test failed" suite variable to PASS when the test failed, so any logic that relies on that flag never sees a failure — read the Robot status, not the variable.
6. What is not covered. No test drives an RF-induced SCG failure (SCGFailureInformationNR from the UE) — all teardowns here are network-side. SgNB-initiated release appears only as a check in a few cases. The suite also has no CMAS/ETWS and no conditional-handover test.
Interview Q&A
Q: What should happen on X2 when the CU of an en-gNB is rebooted?
A: The EN-DC X2 relation is removed (EN-DC X2 Removal) and the UE's SCG is released. When the CU is back, the eNB and gNB perform EN-DC X2 Setup again, and the next B1 report leads to a fresh SgNB Addition Request/Ack and SgNB Reconfiguration Complete, after which NR data resumes.
Q: Why inject faults with kill -11 rather than a clean stop?
A: A clean stop lets the process say goodbye (graceful SCTP shutdown, release messages). SIGSEGV simulates a real crash: peers see an SCTP ABORT or timeout, the pod's crash handling and restart run, and the recovery path — not the shutdown path — is tested.
Q: A resilience test passed but users complain that 5G takes minutes to return after a CU restart. Why did the test not catch it?
A: Because it waits a fixed time (up to 700 s) and then only checks that the recovery messages exist and that at least one byte of NR data passed. It measures eventual recovery, not recovery time or recovered throughput; add polling and timestamps to make it a KPI.
Q: What is special about an NSA-to-NSA handover?
A: The LTE anchor changes (X2 HO between eNBs) while the SCG must be released at the source and re-added at the target. The test checks the X2 HO Request/Ack and SN Status Transfer, then a new SgNB Addition at the target eNB, and that ENDC SgNB addition attempt and success counters are 1.
NT4 NSA Accessibility Troubleshooting — B1 measurement → SgNB Addition → SCG failure: causes & checks 5G NSA click to expand ▾
In plain English
When an NSA phone "stays on 4G", something broke in a five-step chain: the LTE cell must ask the phone to look for 5G (B1 config), the phone must find it (B1 report), the LTE cell must request a 5G leg (SgNB Addition), the 5G cell must accept (Ack) and the phone must connect on 5G (SCG random access). Walk the chain in order and stop at the first missing step — that step tells you whether to look at LTE config, the 5G cell, the X2 link, capacity or the radio. Level 2 — the messages and IEs to open, and quick counters
SgNB Addition Request (X2AP proc 27) — confirm it contains: MeNB UE X2AP ID, NR UE security capabilities, SgNB security key, SgNB UE aggregate maximum bit rate, the E-RABs to be added (E-RAB ID, DRB ID, QCI, GTP tunnel endpoints — for option 3x the S1-UL endpoint), the MeNB-to-SgNB container (CG-ConfigInfo: UE capability, candidate NR cells with PCI and SSB RSRP from the B1 report), MeNB cell ID, and optional SPID / masked IMEISV. Max allowed UE power per RAT (for example 20 dBm LTE and 20 dBm NR) matters for UL coverage.
SgNB Addition Request Acknowledge — E-RABs admitted (with the DL and SgNB UL tunnel endpoints) and the SgNB-to-MeNB container (CG-Config: NR PCI, SSB ARFCN, RACH configuration, BWP, PUCCH/PDCCH, SRS/CSI-RS). The eNB then sends the UE an RRCConnectionReconfiguration carrying the NR configuration (SCG spCellConfig, reconfigurationWithSync with dedicated RACH, dedicated BWP, NR A2/A3 mobility measurements, DL split parameters), the UE answers, the eNB sends SgNB Reconfiguration Complete (proc 28), and for split/SCG bearers the MME path is updated with E-RAB Modification Indication → Confirm.

Useful X2AP filters: x2ap.procedureCode == 27 (addition), 28 (reconfiguration complete), 31 / 32 (MeNB- / SgNB-initiated release), 36 (EN-DC X2 Setup), 37 (EN-DC configuration update), 43 (EN-DC X2 removal). On a setup where X2 goes through the control-plane aggregator the messages sit inside X2AP Message Transfer (proc 17) — see ZT4.

Counter shortcut: the eNB counters ENDC.SGNB.AdditionAtt and ENDC.SGNB.AdditionSucc split the chain in two. Att = 0 → the problem is in stages 1-3 (nothing was ever requested). Att > 0, Succ low → stages 4-5 (rejected by the gNB or failed on the air). ENDC.DL.BytesToUE / ENDC.UL.BytesFromUE confirm that an added leg actually carries data.

Level 3 — patterns from the field and the lab
Add-fail-release loop. B1 → Addition → Ack → SCGFailureInformationNR (randomAccessProblem) → SgNB release → new B1 … repeating every few seconds. The DL looks perfect (B1 is a DL measurement), but the NR uplink cannot close — typical when the NR carrier is a higher TDD band than the LTE anchor, or when UL interference is high. Fix the UL budget or raise the B1 threshold so the leg is only added where UL can close.
Everything configured, still no B1 config. Check the core first: an NR restriction IE from the MME, or a subscriber profile without NR, stops the eNB before it ever measures. Then check the UE capability — the band combination, not just the NR band, must be supported.
B1 never arrives although the NR cell is strong. Almost always frequency/timing config: SSB ARFCN or SCS in the measObject does not match, or the SMTC window misses the SSB burst. A UE cannot report what it cannot find.
Stage 3 blocks quietly. The eNB receives a B1 for a PCI it has no neighbour definition for, or whose gNB has no EN-DC X2, and just ignores it — no error message anywhere. Compare the reported PCI with the NR neighbour list first.
After a restart. If the gNB or CU restarted, the EN-DC X2 must be set up again before any addition can work — ZT4 shows the exact recovery chain the resilience tests expect (X2 Removal → EN-DC X2 Setup → SgNB Addition).
Source note: the handwritten guide this card is based on says the eNB PLMN is "consistent" with the gNB PLMN as a stage-3 cause; the cause is an inconsistent (mismatched) PLMN.
Interview Q&A
Q: An NSA UE stays on LTE. How do you troubleshoot it?
A: Walk the chain: did the eNB send an NR B1 measurement config; did the UE report B1; did the eNB send SgNB Addition Request; did the gNB acknowledge or reject; did the UE complete the SCG random access or send SCGFailureInformationNR? The first missing step points to LTE config/core restriction, NR frequency/coverage, neighbour/X2/PLMN, NR admission/transport, or the NR air interface respectively.
Q: The UE reports B1 but no SgNB Addition Request is sent. Name three causes.
A: The reported NR PCI is not defined as a neighbour (or conflicts), the EN-DC X2 to that gNB is down, or the eNB's configured gNB PLMN / gNB ID does not match the gNB.
Q: What does SCGFailureInformationNR with randomAccessProblem tell you?
A: The SgNB accepted and the UE was reconfigured, but it could not complete random access on the NR cell — an uplink problem (coverage, interference, PRACH configuration or power), not a DL or configuration-exchange problem.
Q: Which counters would you look at first and why?
A: ENDC SgNB addition attempts and successes. Zero attempts means the eNB never requested a leg (stages 1-3); attempts without successes means the gNB rejected or the UE failed on NR (stages 4-5). NR byte counters confirm that added legs carry traffic.
Q: Why can the NR RSRP be excellent while EN-DC still fails?
A: B1 is a downlink measurement. The SCG must also close the uplink, often on a higher-frequency TDD carrier with less UL budget than LTE. Strong DL with weak UL gives SgNB addition followed by SCG RACH failure.
