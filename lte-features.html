LTE Features4G / E-UTRA
Core LTE feature set — CA, MIMO, scheduling, mobility, VoLTE & interworking · plain-English + how-it-works + interview Q&A
Public-knowledge write-ups of the LTE features an RF/RU/RAN validation engineer meets — concepts and how they work, no internal specifics. Each card feeds the Interview simulator.
LF01 Carrier Aggregation (up to 5 component carriers) LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Bond multiple LTE carriers together so one UE gets the sum of their bandwidths — the core lever for peak throughput. A 5CC UE can aggregate five 20 MHz carriers = 100 MHz.
How it works
One carrier is the PCell (carries RRC signalling, PUCCH, and the security anchor); the others are SCells added/removed by RRC reconfiguration based on the UE's measurements and capability. Scheduling can be per-carrier or cross-carrier (PDCCH on one CC schedules another). Aggregation can be intra-band contiguous, intra-band non-contiguous, or inter-band.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is the difference between the PCell and an SCell in CA?
A: The PCell is the primary cell — it terminates RRC/NAS signalling, carries PUCCH and the security context, and is never deactivated while connected. SCells are secondary carriers added purely for extra throughput; they can be activated/deactivated quickly (MAC CE) and only the PCell handles mobility.
LF02 Multi-Cell / Sector Expansion (up to 6 cells) LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Let a single baseband unit host more cells/sectors, increasing site capacity and coverage without new hardware boxes.
How it works
The BBU allocates PHY/MAC resources and fronthaul (eCPRI/CPRI) capacity per additional cell; cell IDs (PCI), carriers and antenna mappings are provisioned so each sector runs independently while sharing transport and timing.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why does adding cells strain the fronthaul?
A: Each cell needs its own IQ sample stream to the radio. More cells and wider bandwidth multiply the eCPRI/CPRI data rate and the timing/sync budget, so fronthaul capacity and latency, not just baseband MIPS, often set the cell-count limit.
LF03 Closed-Loop Spatial Multiplexing (TM4) LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
A MIMO transmission mode that sends two independent data layers on the same time/frequency using UE feedback to pick the best precoder — roughly doubling peak rate in good SNR.
How it works
The UE reports RI (rank), PMI (precoding matrix index) and CQI; the eNB applies the recommended precoder for closed-loop spatial multiplexing. Contrast TM3 (open-loop, no PMI, better for high mobility) and TM2 (transmit diversity, robustness over rate).
Interview Q&A
Q: TM3 vs TM4 — when do you use each?
A: TM4 (closed-loop) uses PMI feedback and gives the best throughput when the channel is stable and SNR is high. TM3 (open-loop spatial multiplexing) drops PMI and cycles precoders, which is more robust at higher UE speed where feedback goes stale.
LF04 Scheduler Optimization (FDD & TDD) LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
The MAC scheduler decides which UEs get which resource blocks each 1 ms subframe — the single biggest driver of cell throughput and fairness.
How it works
It weighs per-UE CQI (channel quality), buffer status, QoS (QCI/priority) and fairness (e.g. proportional-fair) to allocate RBs and pick MCS. TDD adds the UL/DL subframe split as a constraint.
Interview Q&A
Q: What inputs does an LTE scheduler use to allocate resources?
A: Channel quality (CQI/rank), buffer occupancy (BSR for UL), QoS class (QCI/priority/GBR), HARQ state, and a fairness policy. It balances throughput (favour good-channel UEs) against fairness and latency guarantees for GBR bearers.
LF05 HARQ — Speculative, and TDD Bundling & Multiplexing LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Hybrid ARQ retransmits only the failed transport blocks and combines them with the original, recovering errors without a full resend. TDD needs special ACK/NACK handling because UL and DL share the band in time.
How it works
Receiver stores soft bits and chase/IR combines retransmissions. In TDD, several DL subframes map to one UL ACK opportunity, so feedback is bundled (logical-AND of ACKs) or multiplexed (per-TB bits) depending on the UL/DL config.
Interview Q&A
Q: What is HARQ and why does TDD need ACK bundling?
A: HARQ = fast physical-layer retransmission with soft-combining, so a retransmission adds energy rather than replacing the packet. In TDD there are fewer UL subframes than DL, so one UL subframe must acknowledge multiple DL subframes — bundling ANDs those ACK/NACKs (or multiplexes them) to fit the feedback.
LF06 Dynamic CFI (control-region sizing) LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Right-size the PDCCH control region (1–3 OFDM symbols) each subframe so control overhead matches the number of UEs being scheduled, freeing symbols for data when the cell is lightly loaded.
How it works
The CFI carried on PCFICH tells UEs how many symbols the control region spans. Under light load CFI drops to 1 (more PDSCH data symbols); under heavy scheduling it rises to 3 to fit all the DCIs.
Interview Q&A
Q: What does the CFI control and why make it dynamic?
A: CFI sets how many leading OFDM symbols form the PDCCH control region. Fixing it high wastes symbols at low load; fixing it low starves control at high load. Dynamic CFI adapts per subframe so you spend control symbols only when there are DCIs to send.
LF07 Connected-Mode DRX & Inactivity LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Let a connected UE micro-sleep between scheduling opportunities to save battery without dropping the connection.
How it works
DRX defines an on-duration when the UE monitors PDCCH, then sleeps for the DRX cycle; an inactivity timer keeps it awake after traffic. Short and long DRX cycles trade latency for power. An RRC inactivity timer eventually releases the idle UE to RRC_IDLE.
Interview Q&A
Q: What problem does connected-mode DRX solve, and its key timers?
A: It cuts UE power by letting it stop monitoring PDCCH between bursts. Key parameters: on-duration timer, inactivity timer (extends wake after data), short/long DRX cycle, and the HARQ RTT/retransmission timers. Aggressive DRX saves battery but adds scheduling latency.
LF08 Mobility / Idle-Mode Load Balancing (MLB) LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Spread UEs across cells and carriers so no single cell is overloaded while a neighbour sits idle.
How it works
For idle UEs, tune reselection priorities/thresholds in system information to bias camping. For connected UEs, MLB triggers load-based handovers or offloads to another carrier when a cell's PRB utilisation crosses a threshold.
Interview Q&A
Q: How do you balance load between an overloaded cell and an idle neighbour?
A: Idle mode: adjust cell-reselection priorities/offsets in SIBs so new camps favour the lightly loaded carrier. Connected mode: use load-based (MLB) handovers/redirection when PRB or connected-user utilisation exceeds a threshold, moving suitable UEs to the neighbour.
LF09 Admission Control by ARP LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
When the cell is congested, decide which new bearers to admit and which to reject or pre-empt — by priority.
How it works
Each bearer carries an ARP (Allocation & Retention Priority) with a priority level and pre-emption capability/vulnerability flags. Admission control admits high-ARP bearers and can pre-empt lower-ARP ones to make room (e.g. emergency/VoLTE over best-effort).
Interview Q&A
Q: What is ARP and how does admission control use it?
A: ARP = Allocation and Retention Priority: a priority level plus pre-emption capability and vulnerability. Under congestion the eNB admits higher-priority bearers and may drop/pre-empt lower-priority, pre-emptable ones — so emergency and VoLTE survive while best-effort is squeezed.
LF10 X2 Handover (+ S1 fallback for blacklisted neighbours) LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
The fast, direct base-station-to-base-station handover, with a fallback path through the core when no direct link exists.
How it works
Over X2 the source eNB sends Handover Request straight to the target and forwards data directly — low latency. If there's no X2 (or the neighbour is blacklisted for X2), it falls back to S1 handover routed via the MME/S-GW.
Interview Q&A
Q: X2 vs S1 handover — what is the difference?
A: X2 handover goes directly between eNBs with direct data forwarding, so it is faster and lighter on the core — used when an X2 link exists. S1 handover is routed through the MME (and S-GW relocation may occur); it is the fallback when there is no X2 or across MME/vendor boundaries.
LF11 Mobility Robustness — Too-Early / Too-Late / Wrong-Cell HO counters LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Metrics that classify handover failures so you can tune mobility and cut dropped calls (MRO).
How it works
Too-late HO: RLF before the HO completes (thresholds too high/late). Too-early HO: HO then immediate RLF/return (fired too soon). Wrong-cell HO: RLF and recovery on a third cell (bad neighbour/target). These counters drive automatic tuning of A3 offset/TTT and neighbour lists.
Interview Q&A
Q: What do too-early and too-late handover counters tell you?
A: Too-late = the UE hit radio-link failure before handing over — raise sensitivity / lower thresholds or TTT. Too-early = it handed over then immediately failed or bounced back — the trigger fired too soon, so add hysteresis/offset. Wrong-cell = it recovered on a different cell, pointing at a neighbour-list/target selection problem.
LF12 DL Data Forwarding during Handover LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Move the packets already buffered/in-flight at the source over to the target during handover so the user loses as little data as possible.
How it works
At handover the source forwards un-acknowledged/queued PDCP PDUs to the target over the X2-U (or S1 via S-GW) forwarding tunnel; PDCP sequence numbers let the target continue in order and avoid duplicates after the UE re-attaches.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why is data forwarding needed at handover and how are duplicates avoided?
A: Without it, packets buffered at the source when the UE leaves would be lost. The source forwards outstanding PDCP SDUs/PDUs to the target; PDCP sequence numbering and status reporting let the target resume in order and discard duplicates, giving lossless (or near-lossless) handover.
LF13 Call Re-establishment after Radio-Link Failure LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
When the radio link drops briefly, recover the call fast instead of dropping it and re-attaching from scratch.
How it works
On RLF the UE runs RRC Connection Re-establishment toward a prepared cell; if that cell has the UE context (or can fetch it), signalling and bearers resume with minimal interruption rather than a full re-attach.
Interview Q&A
Q: What happens on radio-link failure — drop or recover?
A: The UE declares RLF after T310 expiry, then attempts RRC re-establishment on a suitable cell. If the target has (or retrieves) the stored UE context, the connection and bearers are restored quickly; if not, it falls back to a full RRC setup / re-attach.
LF14 CSFB — Circuit-Switched Fallback (fast redirect via RIM) LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
LTE has no native circuit voice, so for a CS call the UE is briefly moved to 2G/3G, then returns to LTE.
How it works
On a mobile-originated/terminated CS call the MME triggers CSFB: the UE is redirected or handed to GERAN/UTRAN for the voice call. RIM (RAN Information Management) pre-shares neighbour system info so the redirect is fast (fewer reads on the target).
Interview Q&A
Q: What is CSFB and where does RIM help?
A: CSFB moves a UE from LTE to 2G/3G to place a circuit-switched voice call, since EPS is packet-only. RIM lets the eNB pre-fetch the target 2G/3G system information, so the fallback redirection is faster because the UE doesn't have to read all the target SIBs on arrival.
LF15 UE Capability Enquiry & Optimization LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
The network asks each UE what it supports — bands, CA combos, MIMO layers, modulation, features — and configures it accordingly.
How it works
After connection the eNB sends UECapabilityEnquiry; the UE replies with UE-EUTRA-Capability (band combinations, category, supported features). The eNB caches it (and MME can store it) so it only configures features the UE can actually use.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why does the eNB query UE capability, and what breaks if it's wrong?
A: It must know the UE's supported bands, CA combinations, category and features to configure SCells/MIMO/modulation legally. If capability is misread or cached stale, the eNB may configure an unsupported combo (RRC failure) or under-configure (lost throughput).
LF16 VoLTE — Large-Scale Cell & Voice Enhancements LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Carry voice as VoIP over LTE with the QoS and capacity a real network needs at scale.
How it works
Signalling rides a QCI 5 default bearer (IMS); voice media rides a dedicated QCI 1 GBR bearer. Semi-Persistent Scheduling (SPS), ROHC header compression and TTI bundling boost voice capacity and cell-edge coverage.
Interview Q&A
Q: Which QCIs does VoLTE use and why SPS?
A: QCI 5 (non-GBR) for IMS/SIP signalling and QCI 1 (GBR, conversational voice) for the RTP media. SPS pre-allocates the small, periodic voice grants so you avoid a PDCCH assignment every 20 ms — cutting control overhead and boosting the number of simultaneous VoLTE users.
LF17 4G↔5G Interworking (Handover / Reselection / Redirection) LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Move users between LTE (EPS) and 5G (5GS) smoothly as they roam between coverage.
How it works
Idle UEs use reselection by priority; connected UEs use handover (with context/bearer mapping between MME and AMF) or lighter redirection (release with redirect to the other RAT). EPS bearers map to 5G QoS flows/PDU sessions and back.
Interview Q&A
Q: Handover vs redirection vs reselection between 4G and 5G — differences?
A: Reselection is idle-mode, UE-driven by SIB priorities. Handover is connected-mode, network-controlled and near-seamless with context/bearer transfer between MME and AMF. Redirection is connected-mode but cheaper — the network releases the UE with a target frequency/RAT and the UE reselects there, trading a short interruption for less signalling.
LF18 MORAN — Multi-Operator RAN Sharing LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Two or more operators share the same radio hardware while keeping their own spectrum and core.
How it works
In MORAN operators share the eNB/RU but each runs on its own carrier; each cell broadcasts the relevant PLMN(s) and routes S1 to that operator's core. (Contrast MOCN, where operators also share the same carrier.)
Interview Q&A
Q: MORAN vs MOCN?
A: Both share the RAN. MORAN gives each operator its own dedicated carrier on the shared radio; MOCN shares the same carrier among operators (multiple PLMNs broadcast on one cell). MORAN is simpler to isolate per-operator; MOCN is more spectrum-efficient.
LF19 2CC Parallel Dual-UE Throughput — two carriers, two UEs, no-CA vs cross-carrier CA at 5/10/15/20 MHz: method, KPIs, real results LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
One RU transmits two LTE carriers (2CC) in the same band. Does each carrier still deliver its full single-carrier throughput when both are loaded at the same time? And when UEs use carrier aggregation across both, do they get the combined capacity? This test answers both with two real UEs running simultaneous DL+UL traffic for 15 minutes per bandwidth.
Level 1 — setup and method
• Band 3, two cells on two EARFCNs, 4 antennas, TM3 (open-loop spatial multiplexing, rank up to 4).
• Power split: each cell's TX power is set 3 dB below the single-carrier configuration, so the two carriers sum to the RU's maximum capability. The PA is shared, so two carriers get half the power each. This is the most important RF detail of any multi-carrier test.
• No-CA runs: 2 UEs, each attached to its own carrier, running in parallel.
• CA runs: cross-carrier CA. Each UE has its PCell on one carrier and its SCell on the other, so both UEs share both carriers.
• Traffic: iPerf DL+UL simultaneously per UE for 900 s, with a parallel stats collector (eNB-side link/rate statistics).
• Pass/fail: per-UE average vs the 1CC KPI, and the sum of both UEs vs the "CA or 2CC" KPI (= 2 × 1CC).

Level 4 — what an expert notices beyond PASS
• Margins, not verdicts: CA 5 MHz UL is 22.9 vs 22.4, which is a pass but a warning. Re-run it and check UL power control and UE max power before release.
• Radio parameters explain the throughput: RSRP −64 to −74 dBm, SNR ~30 dB, CQI 14–15, RI 4 means a near-ideal radio link, so the numbers reflect the scheduler and stack rather than RF.
• 20 MHz anomaly worth a look: NACK ~3–4.5% and DL DTX ~5–6% (vs ~0% at 5/10 MHz), and UL RSSI rose from ~−128 to ~−100 dBm. The KPI still passed, but a higher UL noise/interference floor and more DTX suggest UL interference or PUCCH decoding issues at 20 MHz. Note that NACK/CRC counters are cumulative, so read their level, not events.
• Asymmetric CQI (12.3 vs 14.8 at 10 MHz) between two carriers of the same RU points at a per-carrier difference (antenna mapping, per-carrier power, frequency-dependent loss) rather than the UE.
• Test hygiene: the 15 MHz first run had a staggered UE stop (a script issue, not the radio), so it was re-run rather than reported. The collector did not record the UE-serial-to-cell mapping, so results were labelled by cell. Fix the collector so the evidence is complete.
Interview Q&A
Q: In a 2-carrier test, why is each cell's TX power set 3 dB below the single-carrier value?
A: Both carriers are amplified by the same PA and together must not exceed the RU's rated power. Two equal carriers each get half the power, which is −3 dB. Forgetting this over-drives the PA (ACLR/EVM fail, protection trips) or produces misleading throughput comparisons.
Q: Per-UE KPI vs aggregate KPI: what does each prove?
A: The per-UE 1CC KPI proves each carrier still performs like a single carrier while the other is loaded (no cross-carrier degradation). The aggregate "CA or 2CC" KPI (2 × 1CC) proves the RU delivers the combined capacity.
Q: Why can cross-carrier CA give more total throughput than two UEs on separate carriers?
A: With CA the scheduler can place each UE's data on whichever carrier has free resources in each TTI (frequency-selective and load-based gains). With fixed one-UE-per-carrier, any idle TTI on one carrier is wasted. The runs showed +11–21% at 5/15/20 MHz.
Q: All KPIs pass, but NACK rose to ~4% and UL RSSI rose ~28 dB at 20 MHz. Report it or ignore it?
A: Report it as an observation with data. A higher UL noise/interference floor plus rising NACK/DTX at one bandwidth suggests UL interference, PUCCH/PUSCH decoding or power-control issues that could fail under less ideal radio conditions. It is cheap to investigate now.
LF20 How to Test an LTE / 5G Feature End to End — method, test matrix, KPIs, stability & band-switch recovery, and which layer your throughput is measured at LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Feature testing is not "run it and see if it passes". It is a repeatable method: understand what the feature changes, baseline the system without it, design cases that prove it works and cases that try to break it, run them the same way every time, and judge them with the right KPIs at the right layer. This card is the method, illustrated with real tests from the lab: band switching, 2-carrier throughput and long stability runs.
The feature-test loop
Level 1 — understand the feature before you test it
For any LTE/5G feature (see LF01–LF18, NS01–NS26), write four lines first:
1. Trigger: what makes it act (a measurement event A3/A4/B1, a timer, load, a config flag)?
2. Layer and messages: which protocol layer implements it, and which messages or IEs prove it happened (e.g. RRC Reconfiguration with SCell addition, X2AP HandoverRequest, a MAC CE)?
3. Counters/KPIs that should move (HO success rate, SCell activation count, throughput, RRC drops).
4. Side effects it might break (throughput, latency, other UEs, the other carrier, power).

Axes to multiply over: band × bandwidth × number of carriers × CA on/off × number of UEs × duplex (FDD/TDD) × traffic type (UDP/TCP, DL/UL/bidirectional). Choose the combinations that exercise the feature, not all of them.
Level 3 — real examples from the lab
A. Band-switch data recovery (B3 ↔ B7), 15 iterations. Pre-check: B3 unlocked, B7 locked, UE attached with an IP, UDP bidirectional iPerf running. Each iteration: B3 down → B7 up, then poll every 30 s for up to 5 min until traffic is flowing again. Record the recovery time. On failure, capture cell status (L1/L2 ready) and TX/RX carrier status, because that is where the break usually is (CM16). Summary: success rate, min/avg/max recovery time, failure details.

B. 2-carrier throughput (LF19): per-UE vs 1CC KPI and total vs 2CC KPI, at 4 bandwidths, CA on/off.

C. Long stability (B3+B7, 12–24 h): two UEs, bidirectional UDP. The lesson learned: a stream can die silently (the UL iPerf client on the phone exits after a radio blip while DL recovers), and a blocking wait never notices. The fix is a self-healing watchdog that restarts only the dead UE+direction and reports every restart (AUTO09). Otherwise one blip turns into a 15-hour "UL = 0" result.

D. Robot Framework structure of such a test (keyword-driven): Settings (libraries: core/HNG, config, CWS, iPerf, common), Variables (bands, BW, duration, QCI), Keywords: set up SSH to the radio → configure BW and CA → attach UEs and read RSRP → start OAM KPI collection → start bidirectional throughput → stop → verify results (where pass/fail is decided) → write results to Excel. Test Cases calls the keyword.
Level 4 — evaluate at the right layer (a very common mistake)
Throughput exists at several layers and the numbers differ. In one 12 h run the same B3 carrier read:

So always state which layer a KPI is measured at. After a UE re-establishment one carrier's delivered DL stepped down ~7% while eGTP and CQI/MCS were unchanged. That is a delivery-layer effect worth a RAN look, not a radio problem. When a KPI fails, compare the layers: where the drop first appears is where to dig (PDLAY).
5G differences: the same method applies with NGAP/F1AP/E1AP instead of S1AP/X2AP, SDAP/QoS flows instead of bearers, and SA registration + PDU session (NS26) instead of attach. KPIs are at PDCP/RLC in the CU/DU, and the UE side reports 5G NR RSRP/SINR per beam (SSB).
Interview Q&A
Q: How would you design a test for a new LTE feature?
A: Understand its trigger, layer, messages and expected counters. Baseline without it. Build a matrix of positive, negative, boundary, stability and regression cases over the relevant axes (band, BW, carriers, CA, UEs, duplex, traffic). Automate it (Robot/pytest) with KPI collection. Judge with explicit per-layer KPI thresholds, and on failure debug layer by layer from the first place the numbers diverge.
Q: What does a band-switch data-recovery test measure and what do you capture on failure?
A: The time for user-plane traffic to resume after switching the serving band (e.g. B3 down → B7 up), polled every 30 s up to a 5 min limit, over many iterations, giving success rate and recovery-time statistics. On failure, capture cell L1/L2 readiness and RU TX/RX carrier state, plus UE attach/IP state, to see which hand-off failed.
Q: Your throughput KPI "fails" by 6%, but the eNB says it forwarded full rate. How can both be true?
A: They are measured at different layers. eGTP (S1-U) shows what the core offered, while PDCP/MAC show what was delivered over the air. A few-percent gap is normal (headers, retransmissions), and a larger step points at a delivery-layer issue. Always specify the KPI layer.
Q: Why is a blocking "wait for iperf to finish" dangerous in a 24-hour test?
A: If one stream dies early (e.g. the UL client exits after a radio blip), nothing checks it until the end, so the result shows hours of zero throughput with no explanation. A watchdog that polls each UE and direction, restarts only the dead stream and records the event keeps the test meaningful and leaves a breadcrumb for RCA.

Interview Q&A
Q: When does an eNB use S1 HO instead of X2? No X2 link, X2 admission failed, or an MME/S-GW relocation is required.
Q: What replaces the "Path Switch" of X2/Xn here? The MME's Modify Bearer to the S-GW re-points the downlink.
Q (PW): In a 4G Metro/FHGW deployment, why might you still see S1 HO? If X2 isn't provisioned to a neighbor (different vendor/region), mobility falls back to S1 via the MME.

CA01 CA — Setup: PCell + SCell addition (RRC Reconfiguration) Carrier Aggregation click to expand +

Why it exists

CA02 CA — SCell Activation / Deactivation (MAC CE) Carrier Aggregation click to expand +

Turning an SCell on/off fast, at L2 — a MAC CE, not RRC · plus the sCellDeactivationTimer for auto-off

Why it exists
RRC add/remove of an SCell is slow (tens of ms) and battery-costly to keep active. So the gNB adds SCells once via RRC (deactivated), then flips them on/off with a 1-byte MAC Control Element in a few ms as traffic demands. An inactivity timer auto-deactivates an idle SCell to save UE power.

Q: Why activate via MAC CE, not RRC? MAC CE is L2 and takes a few ms vs tens of ms for RRC — you can react to traffic bursts without re-signaling the whole SCell config.

CA04 CA — ACLR (why the 2nd-carrier run limit is 52 dBc) Carrier Aggregation click to expand +

In plain English
A handover never starts by magic: the base station first gives the phone a set of "tell me when…" rules called measurement events. "Tell me when your own cell drops below X" is A2; "tell me when a neighbour is 3 dB better than your cell" is A3; "tell me when a 3G cell is good enough" is B1. Each rule has a threshold, a hysteresis (a dead band so it does not flicker) and a time-to-trigger (how long the condition must hold). This card shows the real values the LTE integration tests configure — per bearer type (data, VoLTE, mission-critical) and per band — how to turn the raw numbers into dBm, and how the test proves the eNB sent exactly those values and that the handover followed. PT19 gives the taxonomy, CSI04 the A3 formula, LF10/LF11 the X2 path and the MRO counters.

Level 3 — expert reading of these configurations
1. Hysteresis moves both thresholds. With HYST 16 (8 dB) the A5 case does not trigger at "serving < −91, neighbour > −100" but at "serving < −99, neighbour > −92" — a much stricter window. Always convert thresholds and apply hysteresis before predicting when an event fires; many "the UE never reported" tickets are just this arithmetic.
2. The policy switch is itself an event. When a QCI 1 bearer is added the eNB must send a new RRCConnectionReconfiguration that replaces the default reportConfig with the VoLTE one (tests "A1_A2 VoLTE validation — QCI1 added", "updated VoLTE after A1 hit"). If the bearer is added but the reportConfig is not replaced, voice users hand over with data thresholds — a silent KPI bug that only a reportConfig assertion catches.
3. Multiband policy is a mapping test. In the multiband cases each measObject (EARFCN 300 on B1, 3625 on B8, 6300 on B20) must point to its own reportConfigId (band-1 policy, default, band-20 policy, plus the ANR report config), and the test also asserts the wrong pairings are absent (Expect Error FOUND_NOT_EQUAL).
4. Odd values are plumbing tests, not tuning advice. Some cases push values such as an MCPTT A3 offset of −27 (−13.5 dB), a VoLTE A3 hysteresis of 30 (15 dB, the maximum) or report intervals of 30-60 minutes. They check that any legal value travels from OAM to the UE unchanged; they are not field recommendations. TTT 0 speeds up lab tests but invites ping-pong in the field (LF11).
5. A1 must sit above A2 + 2 × Hys. Otherwise the gap configuration flaps: A2 starts gaps, the next sample triggers A1 and stops them. Measurement gaps also cost throughput (6 ms every 40 or 80 ms, PT19) — the reason A1/A2 gating exists at all.
6. The inter-frequency HO here is an S1 handover. The two eNBs sit behind the gateway, so the A5 case completes with S1AP HandoverRequired (HandoverType intralte) rather than X2 — compare LF10.
Interview Q&A
Q: A5 is configured with threshold1 = 49, threshold2 = 40 and hysteresis 16. When does it trigger?
A: Convert first: 49 → −91 dBm, 40 → −100 dBm, hysteresis 16 → 8 dB. Entering needs Mp + 8 < −91 and Mn − 8 > −100 (ignoring offsets), so serving below −99 dBm and neighbour above −92 dBm, held for the TTT (0 here).
Q: What is the difference between A3 and A4, and between A5 and B2?
A: A3 is relative (neighbour better than serving plus an offset); A4 is absolute (neighbour above a threshold, serving ignored). A5 and B2 have the same two-threshold shape — serving bad AND neighbour good — but A5 targets another LTE frequency and B2 another RAT (UTRA/GERAN, or NR with B1/B2 in EN-DC).
Q: Why do VoLTE and mission-critical bearers get their own measurement policy?
A: Voice is sensitive to interruption and to late handovers, so operators want different thresholds, hysteresis and TTT for those UEs. The eNB applies the policy when the QCI 1 (or 65/66/69/70) bearer exists and must reconfigure the UE when the bearer is added — which is exactly what the tests check.
Q: How would you verify in a test that the eNB configured an event correctly?
A: Capture the RRCConnectionReconfiguration, filter on the event type, and compare each reportConfig field with the configured raw value (threshold index, hysteresis, TTT, interval, amount, max cells). Then drive the RF so the condition is met and check the MeasurementReport and the resulting HO messages.
Q: What does A1/A2 gating do and what goes wrong if the thresholds overlap?
A: A2 (serving bad) starts inter-frequency measurements with gaps, A1 (serving good) stops them to save throughput. If A1 is not above A2 plus twice the hysteresis, the UE oscillates between the two and gaps start and stop repeatedly.
NT5 LTE-M (Cat-M1) — coverage-enhancement levels, PSM and eDRX: T3324 / T3412-ext, paging windows and battery maths LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
LTE-M (Cat-M1, eMTC) is LTE slimmed down for things rather than people: water meters, trackers, alarms, wearables. Three tricks make it work. Coverage enhancement repeats every message many times so a meter in a basement can still be heard, like saying a sentence 100 times in a noisy room. PSM lets the device switch its radio off for hours or days while the network remembers it, like a hotel guest who leaves the "do not disturb" sign on and checks the front desk only once a day. eDRX is a lighter version: the device naps for seconds to minutes and wakes briefly to check for paging. Together they give battery life of years on a small battery, at the price of latency.
PSM and eDRX on a time line
PSM: reachable only during T3324 after each connection. eDRX: reachable only inside each paging time window (PTW). A device can use both: eDRX during the active time, then PSM.

Interview Q&A
Q: What is the difference between PSM and eDRX?
A: In PSM the UE is unreachable after T3324 expires and until its next TAU or uplink data, but stays attached; the MME does not page it at all. In eDRX the UE is still in idle mode and pageable, but only inside paging time windows separated by a long cycle (up to about 43 min in LTE).
Q: What do T3324 and T3412 extended control?
A: T3324 is the active time: how long the UE stays pageable after going idle before entering PSM. T3412 extended is the periodic TAU timer, i.e. how long it can stay in PSM before it must check in. The UE requests both; the network grants the final values in Attach/TAU Accept.
Q: How does a Cat-M UE choose its CE level?
A: It compares measured RSRP with up to three RSRP thresholds broadcast in system information and picks CE level 0-3. Each level has its own PRACH resources, repetition numbers and attempt limits; levels 0-1 are CE mode A, 2-3 CE mode B.
Q: DL data arrives for a UE in PSM. What happens?
A: The MME knows the UE is unreachable and does not page it. The data is buffered in the core (if extended buffering is configured) or dropped, and delivered when the UE next wakes for a TAU or uplink data.
Q: Why can deep coverage kill battery life even with PSM?
A: Because every message is repeated up to hundreds or thousands of times, so the awake/transmit time per report grows by one or two orders of magnitude. The sleep floor stays tiny, but the daily wake energy dominates.

LF21 X2 GW & S1/X2 Aggregation Gateways — relayed X2AP, S1 concentration, handover through an aggregator LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
In a dense small-cell or large macro network, every eNB wanting a direct link to every neighbour and its own link to the core quickly becomes a mesh of thousands of SCTP associations. An aggregation gateway is a "switchboard": eNBs connect once to the gateway, and the gateway fans signalling out. An X2 GW (3GPP Rel-12, TS 36.300 §4.6.6) relays X2 control signalling between eNBs that have no direct X2 path; an S1 aggregator (the HeNB GW in 3GPP is the standard example) makes many eNBs look like one to the MME. The price is one extra hop and one more box that can fail, so testing focuses on latency, routing correctness and what happens when the gateway restarts.

Key X2 GW mechanics
  • Each X2AP message between eNBs is wrapped in the X2AP Message Transfer procedure with RIM (Routing Information: target eNB ID and source eNB ID). The GW does not need to understand the inner message; it routes on the eNB IDs.
  • The GW can tell an eNB that a peer is no longer reachable (X2 Release), and an eNB can remove an indirect X2 relation (X2 Removal, later release).
  • Discovery: besides OAM configuration, the S1 eNB/MME Configuration Transfer (TNL address discovery, see ANR in SON01) can return an indirect X2 address (the GW) instead of the peer eNB address.
  • Only X2-C is relayed. X2-U (DL data forwarding during HO, LF12) runs between the eNBs' own GTP-U addresses.

X2 handover via X2 GW: source eNB → (Message Transfer: HO Request) → X2 GW → target; Ack comes back the same way; SN Status Transfer and UE Context Release likewise. Forwarding tunnels are direct. The target then sends Path Switch Request on S1 — if S1 is aggregated, the aggregator either forwards it to the MME or, when both cells are behind it and it anchors S1-U, may handle the switch locally (implementation choice).

S1 handover between two eNBs behind the same S1 aggregator can be resolved inside the aggregator (MME never sees it) or passed through; the NT3/HO04 message sequence is unchanged at the eNB.

Test ideas
  • X2 Setup via GW with 1, 10, 100 peers: verify each peer's Served Cells / neighbour info arrives and the neighbour table is populated.
  • HO preparation time with and without GW (HO Request → HO Request Ack delta in source logs). Budget the extra transport + processing hop; compare to TRELOCprep.
  • Wrong routing: two eNBs with duplicate Global eNB ID behind one GW — the GW must reject/raise alarm, not mis-deliver.
  • GW restart / SCTP loss: eNBs should fall back to S1 handover (LF10) and re-establish X2 automatically; count HO failures during outage.
  • Load: HO rate × neighbours per eNB → message rate the GW must sustain; watch SCTP retransmissions and queueing latency.

Level 3 — design trade-offs and pitfalls
  • Single point of failure: an X2 GW outage silently converts every X2 HO into S1 HO (more core signalling, longer interruption). Designs use active/standby GWs with SCTP multi-homing; test failover mid-HO.
  • Double wrapping in captures: in Wireshark the real X2AP message sits inside Message Transfer — filter on the inner procedure code, not the outer one, or KPIs will count everything as "Message Transfer".
  • Timers: the X2 GW adds latency to every leg; TRELOCprep/TX2RELOCoverall must still cover the worst-case path. Too-tight timers show up as HO preparation timeouts only through the GW.
  • RLF Indication and MRO (LF11, CAP2) also traverse the GW — if the GW drops unknown message types, MRO silently stops working while HO itself works.
  • S1 aggregation hides cells from the MME: paging, overload (S1 Overload Start) and MME pool behaviour are proxied, so an overload in the MME must be propagated by the aggregator to every eNB behind it.
Interview Q&A
Q: Does an X2 GW carry user-plane data during handover?
A: No. In 3GPP the X2 GW relays X2-C (X2AP) only, wrapped in X2AP Message Transfer and routed on eNB IDs. Data forwarding tunnels (X2-U, GTP-U) go directly between the source and target eNB addresses unless a separate user-plane concentrator is deployed.
Q: Why would an operator deploy an X2 GW at all?
A: To avoid a full mesh of SCTP associations in dense small-cell deployments, to allow X2 between eNBs in different transport/security domains (NAT, separate IPsec gateways), and to simplify neighbour addition: an eNB only needs one association to reach many peers.
Q: The X2 GW reboots. What should you see in the network?
A: eNBs detect SCTP loss, mark indirect X2 relations unavailable and fall back to S1 handover, so HO keeps working but S1 HO count and HO interruption rise. After recovery X2 Setup through the GW is repeated and X2 HO share returns. A test should verify there is no HO failure spike, only a type shift.
Q: How does an eNB learn that it should reach a neighbour through a gateway?
A: Either by OAM configuration or through TNL address discovery over S1 (eNB/MME Configuration Transfer), where the answer can carry an indirect X2 transport address that points at the X2 GW instead of the peer eNB.
LF22 Forward Handover, RRC Re-establishment & Mobility Security — prepared vs unprepared cells, shortMAC-I, KeNB*, NH/NCC LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
When a call moves or recovers, two things must survive: the context (who the UE is, its bearers) and the keys (so the new cell can decrypt and integrity-check the UE). A normal handover prepares the target first. Re-establishment is the UE turning up somewhere after a failure and saying "I was C-RNTI x in cell y, here is my proof" — it works if that cell already has, or can obtain, the context. A forward handover is the common name for recovering on a cell that was not prepared, by pulling the context from the old eNB. Every hop re-derives the key from the old one (or from a fresh value from the MME), so the arithmetic on both sides must match exactly or the UE is dropped with an integrity failure. LF13 and RLF01 cover RLF timers; NT3 covers S1 HO messages; this card is the prepared/unprepared cases and the key chain.
LTE key chain on mobility (TS 33.401 §7.2.8)
NCC is 3 bits (0–7), sent to the UE in the HO command (mobilityControlInfo securityConfigHO) or in RRCConnectionReestablishment (nextHopChainingCount).

Level 3 — failure signatures and root causes
  • Integrity failure right after HO (UE sends nothing, or eNB logs PDCP MAC-I failure on SRB1): the two sides used different NCC/NH, often because the target used a stale NH, or applied the wrong EARFCN/PCI in the KDF (e.g. multi-carrier cell using the wrong carrier's EARFCN).
  • Re-est rejected although the context exists: shortMAC-I verification failed — the eNB computed it with the target's instead of the source cell's identity, or the UE's C-RNTI/PCI pair was matched to the wrong context after a C-RNTI reuse.
  • Forward HO trade-off: fetching context costs an X2 round trip while the UE waits on T301; too slow and the UE falls back to idle anyway. Measure T301 margin.
  • Retainability accounting: a successful re-establishment is usually not counted as a drop, so enabling forward HO can improve retainability KPIs without improving radio quality — always report re-est attempts by cause next to the drop rate (NT6).
Interview Q&A
Q: What is the difference between horizontal and vertical key derivation?
A: Horizontal derives KeNB* from the current KeNB (NCC unchanged), so the old eNB could compute the new key. Vertical derives KeNB* from a fresh NH supplied by the MME (NCC incremented), so the old eNB cannot — this gives forward security, after one hop for S1 HO and two hops for X2 HO.
Q: What proves the UE's identity in a re-establishment request?
A: shortMAC-I: the 16 LSBs of a MAC-I computed with the source cell's RRC integrity key over the source C-RNTI, source PCI and target cell identity. Any eNB holding the UE context can verify it without running NAS authentication.
Q: Why does re-establishment to an unprepared cell fail in basic LTE?
A: The target eNB has no UE context and no key material, so it must reject. The UE goes to idle and re-connects via NAS Service Request — a drop for VoLTE. Vendors add forward handover (context fetch over X2); NR standardises it with Xn Retrieve UE Context.
Q: What does keyChangeIndicator do?
A: Set to true in an intra-cell handover, it tells the UE to derive KeNB from the new KASME (after a new AKA run) instead of from NH/KeNB — a full re-key while staying on the same cell.
Q: After a long chain of HOs the UE drops with integrity errors. Where do you look?
A: Compare NCC in each HO command/re-est message with the NCC sent by the MME in Path Switch Ack or S1 HO Request; check for NCC wrap (0–7) handling and for the EARFCN-DL/PCI used in the KDF at the target.
LF23 Frequency-Priority HO, Service-Based Redirection & SPID — dedicated priorities, T320, release with redirection LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Not every frequency is equal: one band may be the coverage layer, another the capacity layer, a third reserved for a particular kind of subscriber. Frequency-priority features steer UEs to the preferred layer; service-based features steer by what the UE is doing (voice to the coverage layer, bulk data to the capacity layer). SPID (Subscriber Profile ID for RAT/Frequency Priority) is a number from the core that says "this subscriber belongs to profile N", so the eNB can apply a different layer policy per subscriber group without knowing who they are. When the UE is released to idle, the eNB can hand it a personal priority list or redirect it straight to another carrier or RAT. LF08 covers load-based balancing; LF17 covers 4G↔5G redirection.

Level 3 — pitfalls
  • Idle/connected mismatch: dedicated priorities push idle UEs to layer X while connected-mode thresholds push them back to Y → a UE ping-pongs between layers on every call setup. The idle priority and connected-mode A4/A5 thresholds must point the same way.
  • Sticky dedicated priorities: with a long T320 a UE keeps an old policy after it has moved into an area where it no longer makes sense; with none, SIB priorities apply immediately on release.
  • SPID not propagated: if the target of an S1/X2 HO does not store SPID, the policy is silently lost after the first HO; the UE is only re-classified at next Initial Context Setup.
  • Redirection to a RAT without the service: redirecting a VoLTE UE to a layer without IMS voice support breaks voice; service-based policies must check the UE's capabilities and the target's support.
  • Measurement gaps: frequency-priority HO needs inter-frequency measurements; gaps cost throughput (6 ms every 40 or 80 ms). Many designs only start them when A2 fires (see ZT3).
Interview Q&A
Q: What is SPID and who decides its meaning?
A: Subscriber Profile ID for RAT/Frequency Priority, a value 1–256 sent by the MME to the eNB in S1AP (and carried in HO). 3GPP defines only the transport; the operator configures what each value means in the eNB, e.g. frequency priorities or allowed RATs.
Q: How can one UE have different reselection priorities from its neighbours?
A: Through idleModeMobilityControlInfo in RRCConnectionRelease: a per-UE priority list that overrides SIB priorities until T320 expires, PLMN selection happens, or the UE goes connected.
Q: Release with redirection vs handover — when do you use redirection?
A: When the target RAT/carrier has no handover support or no neighbour relation, or speed is more important than continuity (CSFB, 5G→4G fallback). It interrupts the connection: the UE goes idle and reconnects on the target. Handover keeps bearers but needs preparation and measurements.
Q: A UE keeps bouncing between two carriers after every call. What do you check?
A: Compare the dedicated priorities in RRC release (and SIB priorities) with the connected-mode HO triggers. If idle steering prefers carrier A and connected-mode frequency-priority or load HO prefers B, the UE ping-pongs. Also check T320 and service-based rules.
LF24 Load Balancing Extras — X2 Resource Status Reporting, Mobility Settings Change, enhanced MLB & disabling CA under load LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Basic mobility load balancing (LF08) acts on what one eNB sees in its own cells. The enhanced version lets neighbours tell each other how busy they are over X2, so an overloaded cell only offloads to a neighbour that actually has room, and both agree to shift the handover border. A second lever is to stop spending resources on carrier aggregation when a cell is full: CA gives one UE more carriers, but under heavy load it consumes PUCCH resources and scheduler effort that would be better spread across many UEs. SON01 has the SON framework view; this card is the X2 mechanics and test method.
Level 1 — X2 Resource Status Reporting (TS 36.423)
Resource Status Request (eNB1 → eNB2): Registration Request (start / stop), cells to report, Report Characteristics bitmap and Reporting Periodicity (1000, 2000, 5000 or 10000 ms). eNB2 answers Response or Failure (per-cell reasons), then sends Resource Status Update every period.

Mobility Settings Change procedure: eNB1 proposes a change of the handover trigger toward a neighbour cell (Handover Trigger Change, in 0.5 dB steps within ±10 dB); eNB2 accepts, or rejects with the range it can accept. Both sides then shift their CIO so the border moves consistently (no ping-pong).
Level 2 — enhanced MLB flow, CA-under-load, and testing
Typical enhanced MLB loop (algorithm is vendor-specific):
  • Local load (PRB usage or composite capacity) crosses an overload threshold for a time window.
  • Candidate neighbours are ranked by their reported Composite Available Capacity, weighted by Cell Capacity Class (10 % free in a 20 MHz cell is more than 10 % free in a 5 MHz cell).
  • Either select specific UEs (edge UEs with good neighbour RSRP, non-GBR) for load-based HO, or negotiate a CIO shift via Mobility Settings Change so the border moves for everyone.
  • Stop when load drops below a lower threshold (hysteresis) or neighbour load approaches its own threshold.
Disabling CA under load: common strategies are (a) do not configure SCells for new UEs above a load threshold, (b) deactivate SCells (MAC CE, CA02) without removing them, (c) remove SCells for low-traffic UEs to free PUCCH format 3 / CSI resources (LF25). Trade-off: individual peak throughput drops, but cell capacity and fairness improve and control-channel blocking falls.

Test ideas
  • Two-eNB bench with X2: start Resource Status Request from eNB1, decode Updates in Wireshark (x2ap), check periodicity and that PRB % matches the eNB's own counter for the same interval.
  • Load eNB2 with traffic generators; verify eNB1 stops choosing eNB2 as an offload target. Unload it; verify it is chosen again.
  • Mobility Settings Change: trigger overload and check the accepted Handover Trigger Change, then the new CIO in RRC measurement config of connected UEs on both sides.
  • CA under load: step the number of active UEs; record the load at which SCell configuration stops, the SCell activation ratio, cell throughput and per-UE throughput distribution.
Level 3 — pitfalls and what a reviewer asks
  • Stale reports: a 10 s period is slow compared with bursty load; an MLB decision on stale data can overload the target. Short periods cost X2 signalling at scale (neighbours × cells × 1/period).
  • Composite capacity is vendor-computed: different vendors derive Capacity Value differently, so multi-vendor MLB often behaves asymmetrically. Validate what the peer actually reports.
  • Border shifts vs MRO: MLB moves CIO while MRO (LF11) tunes CIO for HO failures; without coordination they fight. Mobility Settings Change carries a cause (load balancing vs HO optimisation) for that reason.
  • CA-aware load: a UE served on PCell + SCell loads two cells. If load per cell ignores SCell traffic, the capacity layer appears idle while it is full of SCell traffic.
  • KPIs to report: PRB utilisation spread between neighbour cells before/after, load-based HO count and success, HO failure rate after CIO changes, per-UE throughput 5th percentile.
Interview Q&A
Q: What does X2 Resource Status Reporting provide that local MLB does not?
A: The load of neighbour cells: PRB usage, S1 TNL and hardware load levels and composite available capacity, reported periodically after a Resource Status Request. It lets the source offload only to neighbours with spare capacity.
Q: Why is Cell Capacity Class Value needed alongside Capacity Value?
A: Capacity Value is a percentage of the cell's own capacity. Cell Capacity Class gives relative cell size, so the source can compare absolute free capacity between a small and a large cell.
Q: What is Mobility Settings Change used for?
A: For two eNBs to agree on a handover trigger (CIO) change toward each other, so shifting the cell border for load balancing is symmetric and does not cause ping-pong. The receiver can reject with the range it accepts.
Q: Why disable carrier aggregation when a cell is heavily loaded?
A: Under high load there are no idle PRBs for CA to use, while each CA UE still consumes PUCCH format 3 resources, CSI reporting and scheduler work. Restricting SCells improves fairness and control-channel capacity at the cost of individual peak rates.

Level 2 — dynamic PUCCH resource allocation and CA counters
Dynamic resource allocation (vendor design): the eNB owns a pool of format 3 resources (each format 3 PRB carries a few users by orthogonal cover). Each CA UE is configured with up to four; the scheduler picks the ARI per TTI to avoid collisions between UEs scheduled in the same subframe. Pool sizing is the trade-off: more PUCCH PRBs → fewer PUSCH PRBs; too few → SCell scheduling blocked ("no PUCCH resource") even with free PDSCH.

Level 3 — pitfalls and root causes
  • pdsch-Start mismatch: with cross-carrier scheduling the UE assumes the SCell PDSCH starts at pdsch-Start. If the SCell actually uses a different CFI (dynamic CFI, LF06) the first symbols are decoded as data → high BLER only on the SCell.
  • ARI collisions: two UEs pointed at the same format 3 resource in the same subframe → eNB sees DTX/garbled HARQ for both → retransmissions and falling SCell throughput with UE count. Check per-resource allocation logs.
  • DTX on SCell PDCCH: if the UE misses the SCell DCI, with format 3 it reports NACK/DTX for that cell; with channel selection, missed PCell DCI can make the whole feedback unusable. HARQ DTX rate per CC is a key debug counter.
  • Uplink power: format 3 needs more power than format 1b; at the cell edge HARQ feedback for all carriers can fail together. Many designs remove SCells at the edge (A2 on PCell).
  • TDD CA: bundling/multiplexing of ACK across subframes (LF05) plus several carriers pushes payload up; format 3 with spatial bundling is often required.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why can PUCCH format 1b with channel selection not support 3CC CA?
A: It carries at most 4 HARQ bits and is defined for two serving cells. With 3–5 FDD carriers (each up to 2 codewords) up to 10 bits are needed, which requires PUCCH format 3 (or formats 4/5 in Rel-13 for more).
Q: What is ARI?
A: The ACK/NACK Resource Indicator: in SCell PDSCH assignments the TPC field is re-interpreted to select one of up to four RRC-configured PUCCH format 3 (or channel-selection) resources, letting the eNB avoid PUCCH collisions dynamically.
Q: When would you use cross-carrier scheduling?
A: When an SCell's control region is unreliable or should be protected — HetNet interference, carriers without a usable control region, or LAA — so all DCIs are sent on a robust cell. The cost is PDCCH load concentrated on the scheduling cell and the need for pdsch-Start.
Q: SCell throughput collapses when more CA UEs join, PCell is fine. Top suspects?
A: PUCCH format 3 pool exhaustion or ARI collisions, PDCCH blocking on the scheduling cell with cross-carrier scheduling, and periodic CSI collisions leaving stale SCell CQI. Check HARQ DTX per CC and PUCCH/PDCCH allocation-failure counters.
LF26 UL Scheduling & Link Adaptation — ULLA BLER loop, UEs per TTI (PDCCH/PHICH limits), UL FFR/ICIC, TDD I_TBS estimation LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Uplink scheduling decides every millisecond which phones may transmit, on which PRBs and with which MCS. Link adaptation (ULLA) picks the MCS: too high and packets fail, too low and capacity is wasted, so a closed loop nudges it until about one packet in ten needs a retransmission. Squeezing more UEs into one TTI sounds free, but every scheduled UE needs a grant on PDCCH and an ACK on PHICH, and those channels run out first. UL fractional frequency reuse keeps cell-edge users of neighbouring cells on different PRBs so they do not jam each other. PC01 covers UL power control; NS15 the 5G equivalent of link adaptation.

Level 2 — UL FFR (ICIC), TDD specifics, and testing
UL fractional / soft frequency reuse: split PUSCH into a cell-edge sub-band and a centre band; neighbouring cells use different edge sub-bands (reuse 3 on the edge, reuse 1 in the centre). Edge UEs (low RSRP or high path loss) are scheduled in the edge band, possibly with higher power; centre UEs anywhere else. Coordination over X2 Load Information (TS 36.423): UL High Interference Indication (per-PRB bitmap, per target cell: "I will put edge UEs here") and UL Interference Overload Indication (per PRB: high/medium/low interference received).

TDD: fewer UL subframes (e.g. 2 of 10 in config 2) mean fewer CRC samples, so the outer loop converges slowly and SINR estimates age between UL opportunities. Typical approaches: SRS in UpPTS for more frequent channel sounding, filtering/aging of SINR per UE, a conservative initial I_TBS for new UEs, and TDD config 0 UL index for multi-subframe grants. TTI bundling (4 consecutive TTIs) is the coverage tool for VoLTE at the edge.

Test ideas
  • Fix UE at several attenuation steps; log first-transmission BLER, average I_MCS, PRBs and UL throughput. BLER should stay near target at every step (flat), MCS should drop smoothly.
  • Step the outer-loop target (if configurable) and check the converged BLER moves accordingly.
  • Many-UE UL test (8, 16, 32 UEs with small full-buffer UL): count UEs scheduled per TTI, PDCCH blocking for DCI 0, PHICH collisions / NACK-on-DTX, and total UL cell throughput vs UE count.
  • FFR: two cells with edge UEs; compare UL SINR and edge throughput with FFR on/off; decode X2 Load Information HII/OI bitmaps.
  • TDD: repeat the attenuation sweep in TDD configs 1 and 2; compare convergence time of MCS after a sudden 10 dB step.
Level 3 — pitfalls and root causes
  • BLER target met but throughput low: the loop only tracks the target; if the SINR estimate is biased low (e.g. SRS on a different bandwidth than PUSCH), MCS sits too low. Check that BLER actually reaches target; far below target means wasted capacity (or the loop is clamped).
  • Outer loop windup: after a long period without scheduling the offset may be stale, or run away if DTX is counted as NACK. Offset clamping and reset on inactivity are common fixes.
  • PHICH collisions masquerading as radio problems: two UEs with the same starting PRB and the same DMRS cyclic shift share a PHICH resource; one receives the wrong ACK/NACK → unnecessary or missing retransmissions. Signature: HARQ anomalies only when many UEs are scheduled.
  • FFR costs peak rate: edge restrictions cut the bandwidth available to edge UEs; in lightly loaded networks FFR can reduce throughput. Adaptive or load-triggered FFR is preferred.
  • PHR-limited UEs: giving them more PRBs lowers power per PRB and SINR; the scheduler must use PHR to cap PRBs, otherwise more PRBs gives less throughput.
Interview Q&A
Q: How does UL outer-loop link adaptation reach a 10 % BLER target?
A: It adds an offset to the measured SINR before MCS selection: small step up on each first-transmission ACK and a larger step down on each NACK. It settles where BLER = up / (up + down), so a down step 9 times the up step converges near 10 %.
Q: Why can't you schedule 50 UEs in one UL TTI in 20 MHz?
A: Each needs a DCI 0 on PDCCH (with DL DCIs competing for limited CCEs), a unique PHICH resource (lowest PRB + DMRS cyclic shift), and enough PRBs after the PUCCH region to be useful. PDCCH and PHICH usually limit first, and many tiny grants waste overhead.
Q: What do HII and OI mean in X2 Load Information?
A: UL High Interference Indication is a per-PRB bitmap telling a neighbour where the sender will schedule cell-edge (high-interference) UEs. UL Interference Overload Indication tells neighbours on which PRBs the sender is suffering high, medium or low interference. They drive UL ICIC / fractional reuse.
Q: Why is UL link adaptation harder in TDD?
A: Fewer UL subframes give fewer CRC and SINR samples, so estimates are older and the outer loop converges slower. Designs use SRS in UpPTS, SINR aging/filtering and a conservative initial I_TBS; reciprocity helps DL precoding but UL interference still needs UL measurements.
LF27 System Information On-the-Fly Modification — modification period, systemInfoValueTag, paging flag, ETWS/CMAS exceptions & enhanced cell setup LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Every LTE cell continuously broadcasts its "rule book" — the MIB and the SIBs — telling phones how to access it, which neighbours to look for and what thresholds to use. Changing that rule book while the cell is live is tricky: millions of phones cache it and will not keep re-reading it. So the standard defines a notice board with fixed change windows: the eNB first shouts "the rules will change" via paging for a whole window (the modification period), then switches to the new SI exactly at the next window boundary, and bumps a version counter (systemInfoValueTag) in SIB1. Emergency broadcasts (ETWS/CMAS) are the exception — they cannot wait for a window. A good implementation applies most parameter edits this way without restarting the cell; "enhanced cell setup" is the related effort to bring a cell on air quickly and cleanly in the first place.
SI change timeline (TS 36.331 §5.2.1.3)
The new content is only allowed from a boundary; UEs that saw the paging flag re-acquire SI from that boundary. Worst-case delay for a change to reach idle UEs ≈ up to two modification periods.

Level 2 — how to test it
  1. Baseline: cell on air, several idle UEs camped, one connected UE with traffic. Record SIB1 valueTag from a UE or a scanner/RF analyser decoding SI.
  2. Change a harmless parameter (e.g. a SIB3 reselection threshold or add an inter-frequency carrier to SIB5) through OAM without locking the cell.
  3. Check the eNB side: paging messages carrying systemInfoModification over one full modification period, new SIB content starting at SFN mod m = 0, valueTag +1.
  4. Check the UE side (QXDM/UE log or test UE): Paging decoded → MIB/SIB1/SI re-acquisition at the boundary → new parameter used (e.g. reselection behaves per new threshold).
  5. No collateral damage: connected UE did not drop, no RRC re-establishment, idle UEs did not lose camping, S1 stays up. Compare RRC setup SR and paging counters before/after.
  6. Negative / edge cases: change applied twice within one period; many changes in a row (tag wrap); UE powered off during the change and powered on later (must detect tag mismatch); change to a "restart-required" parameter must be rejected or must perform a controlled cell restart with an alarm.
  7. ETWS/CMAS: trigger a warning (see EB01/EB02) and confirm it goes out immediately, independent of the modification boundary.

Enhanced cell setup checks: time from "unlock" to first SIB broadcast, S1 Setup / eNB Configuration Update ordering, cell not advertised (barred or not broadcasting) until all planes are ready, clean cell-state alarms. Measure setup time over many repetitions — a regression here often reveals serialised steps that could run in parallel.

Level 3 — pitfalls and design trade-offs
  • Paging not sent in every PO — if the eNB only pages in some paging frames, UEs whose PO was skipped keep old SI until the 3-hour timer or a tag check. Symptom: a "random" subset of UEs ignores a new neighbour.
  • Applying mid-period — switching SI before the boundary means UEs that already read the period's SI miss it; after the change UEs mix old and new values.
  • Paging load — the SI-change flag is added to every paging occasion; in a busy cell combine it with normal paging records, and avoid many back-to-back edits.
  • Long modification periods (coeff 16, cycle 256) save UE battery but a change can take tens of seconds to propagate — surprising during lab tests where people expect "instant".
  • SIB scheduling changes (si-Periodicity, SI window, which SIB in which SI message) also count as SI changes; broken SIB1 scheduling info leads UEs to decode garbage and fail to camp.
  • Rel-13 eMTC/NB-IoT add per-SI value tags and direct indication in DCI; long eDRX cycles use H-SFN based modification periods — see NT5.
Interview Q&A
Q: How does a UE learn that system information has changed?
A: Through a Paging message with systemInfoModification set during the modification period before the change, and by comparing systemInfoValueTag in SIB1 with its stored value. It re-acquires from the next boundary (SFN mod m = 0).
Q: How is the modification period calculated?
A: modificationPeriodCoeff (2, 4, 8 or 16) times defaultPagingCycle (32–256 radio frames), both broadcast in SIB2. Example: 2 × 128 frames = 2.56 s.
Q: Which SI changes do not follow the modification period?
A: ETWS and CMAS (flagged directly in paging), SIB8/SIB16 timing information, and EAB parameters. They can change at any time and do not bump the value tag.
Q: Why can't you change PCI or bandwidth on the fly?
A: They define the physical layer (CRS sequence, PBCH, resource grid). Connected UEs would lose sync instantly, so vendors require a cell lock/restart for those parameters.
Q: A new neighbour was added but some idle UEs never measure it — where do you look?
A: Check that paging with systemInfoModification was sent in all paging occasions for a full period, that SIB5/SIB4 really changed at the boundary and the value tag was incremented, and the UE log for SI re-acquisition.

Level 2 — how counters are validated in test
  1. Controlled scenario, known answer: e.g. 100 attaches with an automated UE; expect RRC.ConnEstabAtt.mo-Signalling = 100 and Succ = 100 in the ROP. Then block the S1 path (or reject in the core) to force known failures and check the right failure counter and cause move.
  2. Drops on purpose: attenuate a UE with active traffic until RLF, don't let it re-establish. Expect ERAB.RelActNbr +1, UE Context Release Request with cause radio-connection-with-ue-lost. A release via user-inactivity must not count as a drop.
  3. Handover counters: run N ping-pong X2 handovers with a fader/attenuator; compare source prep/exec counts with target admitted/complete counts and with the UE log. Repeat without X2 (S1 fallback) and confirm only S1 counters increment.
  4. Availability: lock a cell for 5 minutes in a 15-minute ROP → expect ~33% unavailable, attributed to manual cause, not fault.
  5. Cross-check against traces: protocol analyser on S1/X2 (count messages) vs counters; UE-side logs vs eNB counters. Differences of more than a couple of events usually mean a counting bug.
  6. ROP boundary cases: procedures that start in one ROP and finish in the next; counter reset after eNB restart; counters for cells that were locked during the period (should report 0, not missing).

Typical acceptance targets used in the industry (operator-specific, indicative only): RRC and E-RAB setup SR > 99%, E-RAB drop rate < 1%, intra-frequency HO SR > 98%, cell availability > 99.9%.

Level 3 — pitfalls that make KPIs lie
  • Re-attempts inflate accessibility failures: one unlucky UE retrying 10 times looks like 10 failures. Many KPI definitions de-duplicate by S-TMSI within a short window.
  • Cause mapping errors: an eNB that releases on inactivity but uses a radio-network abnormal cause will show huge "drop rates" with zero real user impact. Always check the S1AP cause in traces.
  • Where a drop is counted during HO: if a UE is lost after HO Command, is it a source drop, a target drop or a HO failure? Define it once, test it, and make sure source and target do not both count it.
  • Normalisation: drop rate per call favours cells with many short calls; per session-time (32.450) is fairer between busy and quiet cells.
  • Denominator zero / low samples: a night-time cell with 3 attempts and 1 failure shows 33% failure. Dashboards should use sample thresholds.
  • Feature regression masked by load: always compare KPIs at similar PRB utilisation and user counts, not simply before/after.
Interview Q&A
Q: What are the main KPI families for an LTE eNB?
A: Accessibility (RRC, S1 and E-RAB setup success), retainability (E-RAB/UE-context abnormal release), mobility (HO preparation/execution success), integrity (throughput, latency), availability (cell unavailable time) and utilisation (PRB usage, active UEs). Defined in TS 32.450 with counters in TS 32.425.
Q: How do you tell a normal release from an abnormal one?
A: By the release cause and data activity: user-inactivity, normal-release, successful-handover or CSFB are normal; radio-connection-with-ue-lost, radio interface procedure failure or relocation timer expiry are abnormal. ERAB.RelActNbr counts only abnormal releases with data still in the buffer.
Q: Why keep separate X2 and S1 handover counters?
A: They fail for different reasons (X2 link/admission vs MME/core relocation) and have different latencies. Separate counters show quickly whether a mobility problem is on a neighbour relation, in the transport, or in the core.
Q: How would you prove a new counter is correct?
A: Create a controlled scenario with a known number of events (including forced failures), then compare the counter with UE logs and S1/X2 protocol traces over the same ROP, including cases that cross an ROP boundary or an eNB restart.
Q: E-RAB drop rate doubled after an upgrade, but users see nothing — what do you suspect?
A: A change in release-cause mapping or counting (e.g. inactivity releases now counted as abnormal), or a change in the denominator. Check S1AP causes in traces before looking for a radio problem.

Level 1 — RSRQ reality check

RSRQ = N × RSRP / RSSI, with RSSI measured over whole OFDM symbols carrying CRS. In an empty cell only CRS REs are transmitted, so RSRQ is about −3 dB (1 CRS port) or −6 dB (2 ports). At full load with equal RE power it falls to about −10.8 dB even with no interference. So an RSRQ threshold tuned in a loaded live network may never trigger in an empty lab — and a busy cell can look "bad" purely because it is busy.

  • Config check: decode RRCConnectionReconfiguration — two measIds pointing to the same measObject, triggerQuantity rsrp vs rsrq, reportQuantity both, correct index-to-dB mapping (a classic bug: RSRQ threshold written in dB instead of index).
  • Trigger isolation: with two cells and attenuators, raise neighbour RSRP while keeping load constant → RSRP report fires. Then load the serving cell (or inject interference) at fixed RSRP → only the RSRQ report fires. Confirm the report content always contains both quantities.
  • Race: make both triggers fire within a few ms; verify only one HO preparation to the target, no duplicate X2 Handover Request.

Level 3 — expert notes and pitfalls
  • RSRQ-triggered ping-pong: RSRQ reacts to load; a UE that hands over can load the target and flip RSRQ back. Use larger hysteresis/TTT for RSRQ than for RSRP, or require an RSRP floor on the target (A5 threshold2 on RSRP is not possible within one reportConfig — vendors check it in the eNB after receiving "both").
  • Filter coefficients differ: RSRQ is noisier; many designs use a heavier L3 filter for RSRQ.
  • Measurement gaps: inter-frequency RSRQ triggers still need gaps (unless the UE supports gap-less measurement); starting gaps by an RSRQ A2 in a busy cell can cost throughput for many UEs.
  • E-CID and TA: TA is only as good as the eNB's UL timing estimate; multipath and repeaters add delay, and a UE served through a repeater or DAS appears further away than it is.
  • Rel-13+ added RS-SINR reporting options; newer UEs can report it, but triggerQuantity for classic events remains RSRP/RSRQ.

A: 16 Ts ≈ 0.52 µs of round-trip time, i.e. about 78 m of UE-to-eNB distance.
LF30 LTE TDD Deployment & Fixed Wireless Access — UL/DL and special-subframe configs, guard period vs radius, TDD alongside FDD, sync with neighbours, FWA design LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
FDD gives the downlink and uplink their own frequency, like a two-lane road. TDD uses one frequency and takes turns, like a single-lane bridge with traffic lights: some milliseconds for DL, some for UL, and a short empty gap (the guard period) so the two directions never collide. TDD is attractive for fixed wireless access (FWA) — broadband to homes via a rooftop or window CPE — because unpaired spectrum is often plentiful and the DL/UL split can match internet traffic. The price: every TDD cell nearby (including other operators in adjacent spectrum) must switch at exactly the same moment, so TDD needs phase/time sync, not just frequency sync. NR TDD patterns are covered in TDD01; HARQ in TDD in LF05.

Level 2 — deploying TDD next to FDD and for FWA
  • Synchronisation: all TDD cells in the band and area need a common frame start — GNSS at the site or PTP (G.8275.1/.2) with about ±1.5 µs error to a common reference (ITU-T G.8271; TS 36.133 cell phase sync accuracy: 3 µs between cells for small radius, 10 µs for large). FDD cells only need frequency accuracy. When reintroducing TDD onto a network that was FDD-only, the timing chain is usually the biggest piece of new work (see CM11).
  • Same UL/DL config as neighbours: every operator in adjacent TDD blocks must use the same (or a compatible) configuration and frame start, otherwise one base station transmits while its neighbour receives → BS-to-BS interference that no power control can fix. If alignment is impossible, guard bands and extra filtering are needed.
  • Interworking with FDD: inter-frequency measurement objects/SIB5 priorities between FDD and TDD layers, handover in both directions, and optionally FDD-TDD carrier aggregation (Rel-12) with an FDD PCell (robust UL, PUCCH) and a TDD SCell for DL capacity.
  • FWA design: fixed outdoor/indoor CPE with high antenna gain (often 10–20 dBi) and higher UE power class where the band allows, so UL — normally the weak link — closes at longer range; no mobility, so handover tuning matters less than per-sector capacity planning (subscribers per sector × busy-hour rate × contention ratio). Choose config 2 for download-heavy households, config 1 if upload (video calls, cloud backup) matters. TDD channel reciprocity allows UL-sounding-based beamforming (TM7/8/9) to boost cell-edge rates.
  • Test ideas: verify frame structure and ON/OFF transient with a signal analyser (TDD time-gated ACLR/EVM, OFF power); check frame start against a GNSS 1PPS reference; peak DL/UL throughput per config (e.g. 20 MHz 2×2: roughly 110 Mbps DL with config 2 / SSF7, about 10 Mbps UL vs ~20 Mbps UL with config 1 — theoretical, order of magnitude); HARQ/ACK bundling behaviour (LF05); maximum range test with a delay line to confirm PRACH detection within the GP budget.
Level 3 — real-world pitfalls
  • Loss of sync: a TDD site that loses GNSS keeps running on holdover; once drift exceeds the budget it starts interfering with its neighbours' UL. Vendors typically stop transmitting (or alarm) after a holdover limit — test that behaviour by unplugging the GNSS antenna.
  • Atmospheric ducting / remote interference: under certain weather, DL from a TDD site hundreds of km away arrives after the guard period and lands in the victim's UL subframes. Signature: UL noise rise in the first UL symbols, at night/early morning, over wide areas. Mitigation: larger GP (switch SSF), UL scheduling away from the first UL symbols; NR added a formal RIM framework.
  • UL coverage in DL-heavy configs: config 2 gives only 2 UL subframes per 10 ms → UL throughput and VoLTE/HARQ timing suffer at the edge; TTI bundling or config 1 may be needed.
  • Mixed configs between layers in FDD-TDD CA or TDD-TDD inter-band CA complicate HARQ timing — check UE capability for the specific band combination.
  • FWA CPE placement dominates performance: a few dB from window vs outdoor mounting changes MCS far more than any scheduler tuning.
Interview Q&A
Q: Which LTE TDD configuration is most common and why?
A: Config 2 (DSUDDDSUDD) with special subframe 7 — it gives about 80% DL time, matching internet traffic, with a 5 ms switching period and a guard period good for typical macro radii.
Q: What limits TDD cell radius?
A: The guard period in the special subframe: the round-trip delay plus switching time must fit inside it. One OFDM symbol ≈ 71.4 µs ≈ 10.7 km one-way.
Q: Why does TDD need phase synchronisation while FDD does not?
A: All TDD base stations in an area must switch DL/UL at the same instant; otherwise one site transmits while its neighbour receives, causing BS-to-BS interference. FDD uses separate frequencies, so only frequency accuracy is required.
Q: What makes TDD suitable for FWA?
A: Unpaired spectrum is widely available, the DL/UL split can be tuned to traffic, reciprocity enables beamforming, and fixed high-gain CPEs overcome the UL link-budget limit. No mobility simplifies optimisation.
Q: UL noise rise appears in the first UL symbols at night across many TDD sites — cause?
A: Atmospheric ducting: DL from distant TDD sites arrives after the guard period. Mitigate with a larger guard period and avoiding the affected UL symbols.
LF31 DL Transmission Efficiency — PRB blanking, MBSFN-subframe energy saving, CRS power reduction, TX-branch reduction & cell DTX LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
A base station spends a large share of its energy just keeping the power amplifier on, even at 3 a.m. when almost nobody is using it. In LTE the worst offender is the cell-specific reference signal (CRS): it must be sent in every subframe across the whole bandwidth, so the PA can never rest. Downlink energy-efficiency features attack this: don't transmit empty resource blocks (PRB blanking), declare some subframes as MBSFN so they carry almost no CRS and the PA can sleep through most of them, reduce CRS power or the number of transmit branches at low load, and pack traffic so whole subframes stay empty (cell DTX). The goal is to save watts without users noticing. Symbol-level PA gating is in USLEEP01; carrier/cell sleep and O-RAN energy saving in CM22.

Level 2 — how to test and measure
  • Power at defined loads: measure site/RU DC power at 0%, 10%, 30%, 50% and 100% PRB load with the feature off and on (the ETSI ES 202 706 style of static load levels). Use an independent DC meter, not only the RU's self-reported value (see CM14/CM21).
  • See it on air: spectrum/signal analyser in zero-span or time-domain view — MBSFN subframes show only the first 1–2 symbols; decode SIB2 for the MBSFN pattern; check CRS power vs configured referenceSignalPower.
  • RF conformance with the feature active: ACLR/SEM and transient behaviour when the PA switches on/off; EVM of the first symbol after an off period (warm-up droop).
  • User impact KPIs: DL throughput and latency (ping) at low and medium load, RSRP/RSRQ distribution, HO success and cell-edge reselection, VoLTE MOS. Trigger rising load to verify the feature backs off (MBSFN pattern removed, branches re-enabled) before capacity is hurt.
  • Legacy/UE mix: UEs in TM3/TM4 vs TM9; UEs measuring RSRP only in non-MBSFN subframes; check neighbour-cell measurement is unaffected.
Level 3 — design trade-offs and pitfalls
  • Why MBSFN "works" for non-MBMS cells: legacy UEs are told those subframes are MBSFN and therefore do not expect CRS beyond the control region; no MBMS service needs to exist. It is a clever reuse of a broadcast feature purely for energy saving.
  • Measurement side effects: fewer CRS samples slightly increase RSRP/CQI estimation noise; some older UEs have shown CQI or RLM issues with aggressive patterns — include a diverse UE set.
  • Hysteresis between states: changing the MBSFN pattern needs an SI modification (up to two modification periods) — so switching must be driven by load trends, not instantaneous load, otherwise the cell oscillates and floods paging with SI-change flags.
  • Neighbour interference shift: when one cell blanks, its neighbours' SINR improves; when it wakes, their RSRQ drops. Coordinated patterns across a cluster behave more predictably than per-cell decisions.
  • PA physics: a class-AB/Doherty PA at low output is inefficient; only turning the bias off (DTX) gives big savings. The switch-on time must fit in the cyclic prefix/first symbol — this is where RF hardware limits the feature.
  • NR comparison: NR has no always-on CRS (only SSB every 20 ms by default), which is why NR idle power is fundamentally lower and why LTE needs these tricks.
Interview Q&A
Q: Why is LTE hard to make energy efficient at low load?
A: The CRS is transmitted in every subframe across the full bandwidth regardless of traffic, so the PA can never switch off; PA bias power dominates at low load.
Q: How do MBSFN subframes save energy in a cell without MBMS?
A: Subframes declared as MBSFN carry CRS only in the control region (1–2 symbols). If no data is scheduled there, the PA can be blanked for the rest of the subframe. Up to 6 of 10 subframes in FDD (1,2,3,6,7,8).
Q: What is the downside of MBSFN energy saving?
A: TM1–TM8 UEs cannot be scheduled with PDSCH in those subframes, so capacity drops if load rises; the pattern can only change through an SI modification, which is slow.
Q: How would you verify a DL energy-saving feature?
A: Measure DC power with an independent meter at fixed load steps with the feature on/off, confirm on-air behaviour with an analyser (time domain, SIB2 decode), re-run ACLR/EVM/transients, and compare throughput, latency and mobility KPIs to show no user impact.
Q: What does CRS power reduction trade off?
A: Energy versus coverage: RSRP drops everywhere, moving cell borders and reselection/HO points, so neighbours and thresholds must be re-planned.

Picture a loud speaker (the macro cell) and a quiet one (a small pico cell) in the same room. People near the quiet speaker cannot hear it over the loud one. eICIC fixes this by having the loud speaker go almost silent for a few agreed moments. In those moments the quiet cell serves the users it would otherwise lose. Level 3 — pitfalls and why gains are smaller than slides
  • ABS is not empty. CRS on up to 4 ports still hits the CRE UE in every ABS. At high bias, CRS collisions (same PCI mod 3/mod 6 shift) dominate the SINR. This is why FeICIC added UE-side CRS interference cancellation; without it bias above about 9 dB stops helping.
  • RLF without measurement restriction. If measSubframePattern is missing, a CRE UE measures its pico in unprotected subframes, sees very low SINR, declares radio link failure (see LF13) and bounces back to the macro.
  • Synchronisation is mandatory. ABS only protects if the subframe boundaries line up. That needs phase sync across macro and pico (GPS or PTP; see CM11). A small cell drifting by a few hundred µs loses the protection quietly.
  • Macro capacity cost. Each ABS costs about 1/10 of macro DL capacity per frame. Adaptive ABS (SON) trims the ratio to the load in the CRE zone.
  • CoMP JT needs ideal backhaul. Joint transmission needs the same data and aligned phase at several points within about a millisecond. That is realistic only inside one baseband (C-RAN / vRAN sectors on the same server). Across eNBs over normal X2, only CS/CB really works — this is why Rel-12 “non-ideal backhaul CoMP” (TR 36.874) is scheduling-based.
  • CSI-RS / CSI-IM planning. TM10 fails silently if the CSI-IM RE pattern does not match where the neighbour’s zero-power CSI-RS actually is — the UE then measures the wrong interference and CQI is optimistic.
  • NR link. NR has no always-on CRS, so ABS is not needed in the same way; multi-TRP (Rel-16) with TCI states is the NR successor of CoMP (see NS40).

Interview Q&A
Q: What is an Almost Blank Subframe and why “almost”?
A: A macro subframe with no PDSCH and no unicast control, used to give pico range-expansion UEs a quiet window. It is “almost” blank because CRS, PSS/SSS, PBCH and (when scheduled) SIB1/paging must still be sent for legacy UEs.
Q: Why does a CRE UE need measurement resource restriction?
A: Its SINR is good only in ABS subframes. If it averages RLM/RRM/CSI over all subframes it sees very low quality, reports low CQI and may declare RLF. measSubframePattern limits measurement to protected subframes.
Q: Compare CoMP JT, DPS and CS/CB.
A: CS/CB: one serving point, neighbours coordinate scheduling and beams — normal X2 is enough. DPS: one serving point that can change per subframe. JT: several points send the same data simultaneously — needs near-ideal backhaul and tight sync.
Q: What does TM10 add over TM9?
A: Multiple CSI processes (up to 4), each with an NZP CSI-RS and a CSI-IM resource, and the PQI field in DCI 2D that tells the UE which PDSCH RE mapping and QCL assumption to use — so the network can switch transmission points dynamically.
Q: Why did FeICIC appear?
A: The remaining macro CRS in ABS limits the SINR of CRE UEs. UE-side CRS interference cancellation (plus reduced-power ABS) lets the operator use larger CRE bias and gain more offload.

Interview Q&A
Q: Why does LAA need a licensed PCell?
A: Unlicensed access is not guaranteed — LBT can block any transmission. Keeping RRC, mobility, paging and control on a licensed PCell means the connection survives even when the unlicensed SCell cannot get the channel. (NR-U standalone removes this requirement at the cost of reliability.)
Q: Explain Cat-4 LBT.
A: The node senses the channel idle for a defer period (16 µs + mp×9 µs), then counts down a random backoff drawn from [0, CW] in 9 µs slots, freezing when busy. When the counter hits zero it may transmit for up to MCOT. CW grows on repeated NACKs and resets on success.
Q: What is a CBSD heartbeat and what happens if it fails?
A: A periodic message to the SAS that keeps a spectrum grant authorized. If no successful heartbeat response arrives before the transmit-expiry time, the CBSD must stop radiating on that grant.
Q: Why are eLAA and NR-U uplinks interlaced?
A: Regulations require occupied bandwidth of at least 80 % of the channel and limit PSD. Spreading a UE’s PRBs across the whole 20 MHz (interlace) meets OCB and lets the UE send more total power under a PSD cap.
Q: Name two ways NR-U differs from LAA.
A: NR-U can run standalone; it does LBT per 20 MHz subband; it adds COT sharing with short Type 2A/2B/2C access; and it uses an SSB discovery burst window with extra SSB candidate positions.

Level 3 — the numbers behind the coverage, and pitfalls
  • Where +20 dB comes from: a single-tone 3.75 kHz NPUSCH concentrates the UE’s 23 dBm into 1/48 of a PRB — about 17 dB more power spectral density than spreading it over 180 kHz. Repetitions (with combining) add the rest: 128 repetitions give up to about 21 dB in theory, less in practice because of channel-estimation loss and frequency error.
  • Frequency error matters more: with 3.75 kHz subcarriers, a 50 Hz error is a much larger fraction of the spacing than for LTE. Cheap UE oscillators and Doppler drive tight eNB frequency accuracy (see FC04 on frequency error).
  • Latency is huge by design: a CE-level-2 access can take several seconds (NPRACH ×128, NPDCCH ×hundreds, NPDSCH ×thousands). KPI dashboards built for LTE (setup time, RRC timers) show false failures — timers must be scaled per CE level.
  • Capacity cost of repetitions: one deep-coverage UE can occupy the anchor for seconds. Non-anchor carriers (Rel-14) and early data transmission (EDT, Rel-15: data in Msg3) are the main fixes; wake-up signal (WUS, Rel-15) reduces paging energy.
  • CIoT optimisations: Control-Plane optimisation sends small data inside NAS over SRB (no DRB); User-Plane optimisation suspends/resumes the RRC context. Mismatch between UE and MME support is a classic attach-reject cause.
  • 5G path: NB-IoT and LTE-M can coexist inside an NR carrier (reserved resources), which is how they are carried forward alongside RedCap (NS44).

LF35 eMBMS / MBSFN & SC-PTM — broadcast over LTE: MCE, MCCH/MTCH, MBSFN subframes and NR MBS LTE click to expand ▾
In plain English
Unicast is like phoning every viewer one by one; broadcast is like a radio station. eMBMS lets LTE act like a radio station for popular content — a live match, a software update, public warnings — so one transmission serves everybody in the area. In MBSFN many cells send exactly the same signal at exactly the same time; to the phone, the extra cells look like helpful echoes rather than interference. SC-PTM is the lighter, single-cell version for small groups (e.g. IoT firmware). LF31 reuses MBSFN subframes for energy saving; this card covers what they were designed for.

  • Area-edge problem: at the edge of an MBSFN area, neighbouring cells not in the area send different signals in the same subframes. Operators add a ring of “reserved” cells that blank those subframes.
  • Capacity trade-off: each MBSFN subframe is 10 % of the DL frame taken from unicast in every cell of the area, even if few UEs watch. Counting and switch-to-unicast avoid waste.
  • Measurement trap for RF test: MBSFN subframes change the CRS pattern (CRS only in the non-MBSFN region), so tools that average RS power or RSRP over all subframes read low. The same trap appears with MBSFN-based energy saving (LF31).

Interview Q&A
Q: Why does MBSFN use the extended cyclic prefix?
A: Signals from several distant cells arrive with large delay differences. The 16.7 µs extended CP absorbs them so they combine constructively instead of causing inter-symbol interference.
Q: Which FDD subframes can never be MBSFN, and why?
A: 0, 4, 5 and 9 — they carry PSS/SSS, PBCH, SIB1 and paging, which every UE needs.
Q: How does a UE find and follow an eMBMS service?
A: SIB2 gives the MBSFN subframes, SIB13 points to the MCCH of each area, MCCH lists sessions (TMGI) and PMCH config, MSI tells which subframes each MTCH uses. PDCCH with M-RNTI signals MCCH changes.
Q: MBSFN vs SC-PTM?
A: MBSFN: multi-cell, synchronised, dedicated subframes, extended CP, MCH/PMCH — best for large audiences over wide areas. SC-PTM: single cell, PDSCH with G-RNTI, dynamic scheduling — best for small or changing groups.
Q: What are M1, M2 and M3?
A: M1: user-plane IP multicast from MBMS-GW to eNBs. M2: MCE ↔ eNB control (resource allocation). M3: MME ↔ MCE control (session start/stop).
